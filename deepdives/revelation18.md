*Setting:* Chapter 17 showed Babylon as a prostitute riding the beast, and ended by naming her plainly: "the great city that has dominion over the kings of the earth" (17:18). Chapter 18 shows her fall. There is almost no action in it. It is made of songs and speeches: an angel's announcement, a call from heaven to God's people, three laments from the kings, the merchants and the sailors, a call to rejoice, and a final acted parable with a millstone. Nearly every line borrows from the Old Testament prophets' songs over the great cities of their day: Babylon (Isa 13–14; 47; Jer 50–51) and the trading port of Tyre (Ezek 26–28). John's first readers lived in cities whose wealth flowed to and from Rome, and many of them earned their living in that trade.

Readers identify the city in the same four ways as in chapter 17:
- **Futurists** expect a final world capital of trade and pleasure in the last days. Some look for a literally rebuilt Babylon in Iraq. Guzik thinks it more likely a picture of the whole world system, distinct from the religious Babylon of chapter 17, and falling at a later point.
- **Historicists** like Henry and JFB see papal Rome. JFB argues that Rome "was not a commercial city," so the cargo stands for spiritual goods that were bought and sold.
- **Preterists** see the fall of pagan Rome, the greatest trading city of its world. The Tyndale notes mention the Gothic sackings of the AD 400s. Some preterists see Jerusalem's fall in AD 70 instead.
- **Idealists** see the judgment that waits for every economy and culture built on pride, luxury and the exploitation of others.

All agree the chapter is about what God thinks of a civilization's wealth when it is built on idolatry and blood.

#### The Fall of Babylon (vv1–24)
- **"Another angel coming down from heaven, having great authority, and the earth was made bright with his glory" (v1).** He has come straight from God's presence and still shines with it, as Moses' face did (Exod 34:29). Ezekiel saw the earth shine with God's glory when it returned to the temple (Ezek 43:2). Henry: "attended with great power and lustre." Before the darkness of Babylon's end comes a burst of heaven's light.
- **"Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!" (v2).** These words come from Isaiah 21:9 and were already heard in 14:8. The tense is past, though the fall is still being described. Prophets often speak of a future judgment as already done because God's word makes it certain. Henry says the angel announces it "as a thing already come to pass."
- **"A dwelling place for demons… a haunt for every unclean bird" (v2).** This is the old prophetic picture of a ruined city, overrun by desert animals and spirits (Isa 13:21–22; 34:11–15; Jer 50:39). The word for "haunt" (*phylakē*) usually means "prison," and JFB renders it "a keep or prison." The proud, glittering city ends as a cage for the unclean.
- **"All nations have drunk… the kings… the merchants" (v3).** Three groups are named, and they are the same three that mourn in vv9–19. "The power of her luxurious living" uses *strēnos*, a word for wanton, overflowing luxury. The charge is not that she traded, but that she lived for indulgence and drew the world in with her.
- **"Come out of her, my people" (v4).** This is the heart of the chapter for its first readers. The call comes from Jeremiah 51:6, 45 ("Go out of the midst of her, my people!") and Isaiah 52:11, which Paul applies to the church (2 Cor 6:17). In John's day it hardly meant moving house. Believers in Asia could not leave the empire. It meant refusing Babylon's idolatry and greed while living inside her economy, as the letters to Pergamum and Thyatira required (2:14, 20). The two reasons go together: "lest you take part in her sins," and so "lest you share in her plagues." Guzik compares Lot, who was dragged out of Sodom before it burned (Gen 19:15–16). JFB says of God's people inside apostate churches that "their only safety is in coming out of her at once."
- **"Her sins are heaped high as heaven" (v5).** The verb means "stuck together" or "piled up." JFB renders it "reached so far as to come into close contact with, and to cleave unto." Guzik hears the tower of Babel, which also reached to heaven (Gen 11:4). Jeremiah 51:9 says Babylon's judgment "has reached up to heaven." **"God has remembered her iniquities"** looks back to 16:19. God's patience is not forgetfulness.
- **"Pay her back as she herself has paid back others, and repay her double" (v6).** The voice seems to call on the agents of judgment, perhaps the beast and the kings of 17:16, or the angels. The principle is exact justice: what she did, she receives (Jer 50:15, 29; Ps 137:8). "Double" echoes Old Testament restitution, where a thief repaid double (Exod 22:4, 7, 9), and Jeremiah 16:18. Some scholars think the Hebrew idiom behind "double" means a matching equivalent, a full measure, rather than twice as much. Either way the point is that her judgment fits her crimes. Isaiah 40:2 uses the same idea for Jerusalem, which received "double for all her sins" and then was comforted.
- **"I sit as a queen, I am no widow, and mourning I shall never see" (v7).** This is taken almost word for word from Isaiah 47:7–8, the boast of old Babylon: "I shall not sit as a widow or know the loss of children." Guzik names three sins in the verse: living for luxury, pride, and the belief that she is beyond suffering. A widow in the ancient world had no protector and no income. Babylon thinks she can never be that vulnerable. Rome was often shown on coins as a goddess, *Roma*, seated on a throne.
- **"Her plagues will come in a single day… for mighty is the Lord God who has judged her" (v8).** Isaiah 47:9 continues: "These two things shall come to you in a moment, in one day." The four plagues, **death, mourning, famine and fire**, are the opposite of everything she boasted. Her fall is not only the work of the beast and the kings (17:16). The Lord God has judged her.
- **"The kings of the earth… will weep and wail over her" (v9).** This is the first of three laments, and they are modeled on the laments over Tyre in Ezekiel 26:16–18 and 27:28–36. The kings shared her immorality and luxury, so they grieve, but notice that they **"stand far off, in fear of her torment"** (v10). No one comes to help. They mourn what they have lost, not what she did.
- **"Alas! Alas! You great city… for in a single hour your judgment has come" (v10).** "Alas" (*ouai*) is the same word as "woe." The phrase "in a single hour" comes three times (vv10, 17, 19) like a refrain. Centuries of wealth vanish in an hour.
- **"The merchants of the earth weep and mourn for her, since no one buys their cargo anymore" (v11).** The second lament is honest about its motive. The merchants weep because their market is gone. Henry: "mere worldly sorrow adds to the calamity." Paul makes the same contrast between "godly grief" and "worldly grief" (2 Cor 7:10).
- **The cargo list (vv12–13).** There are nearly thirty items, set out roughly in groups: precious metals and gems; fine fabrics; luxury woods, ivory and building stone; spices and perfumes; food; animals and vehicles; and finally people. Ezekiel 27:12–24 gives a similar list for Tyre. The Tyndale notes say these were among the most expensive goods of the ancient world, all flowing to the capital. A few details:
  - **Silk** came all the way from China and was worth its weight in gold in Rome.
  - **Scented wood** (*thyinon*) was citrus wood from North Africa, prized for tables that could cost a fortune.
  - **Cinnamon** and **spice** (*amōmon*, an Indian perfume plant) were luxury imports from the East.
  - **Fine flour** was the best wheat, eaten by the wealthy, while ordinary people ate coarser bread. John has already heard the price of wheat in a famine (6:6).
- **"And slaves, that is, human souls" (v13).** The Greek reads literally "bodies, and souls of men." "Bodies" (*sōmata*) was a normal trade term for slaves, sold like cattle. John adds "souls of men," as if to insist that these are people, not cargo. Ezekiel 27:13 says Tyre traded in "human beings." The Tyndale notes estimate that slaves made up perhaps a fifth of the empire's population. The list ends at its moral bottom: a civilization that buys and sells human souls. Guzik applies this to the modern trade in human beings, including prostitution and pornography. JFB, true to his historicism, also sees the sale of masses and indulgences, the buying and selling of spiritual things.
- **"The fruit for which your soul longed has gone from you" (v14).** "Soul" appears again, now hers. The goods she craved are gone and will be "never to be found again." "Never" here is the strongest negative in Greek, the first of a run of "no mores" that fills vv21–23.
- **"Clothed in fine linen, in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold" (v16).** The merchants describe the city in the same words John used for the prostitute (17:4). The woman and the city are one.
- **"All shipmasters and seafaring men" (v17).** The third lament comes from the sea trade. Rome depended on ships, above all on the grain fleets from Egypt. Ostia, Rome's port, was among the busiest in the world. The Tyndale notes point out that Roman control of piracy made this trade safe, so Rome's fall would collapse it.
- **"What city was like the great city?" (v18).** This copies Ezekiel 27:32 about Tyre: "Who is like Tyre?" It is also a twisted echo of worship. Israel sang "Who is like you, O LORD?" (Exod 15:11), and the world sang "Who is like the beast?" (13:4).
- **"They threw dust on their heads" (v19).** This was a sign of grief (Josh 7:6; Ezek 27:30).
- **"Rejoice over her, O heaven, and you saints and apostles and prophets, for God has given judgment for you against her!" (v20).** The tone changes completely. The kings, merchants and sailors weep, but heaven rejoices. The Greek reads literally "God has judged your judgment on her," meaning he has upheld the case his people brought, the cry of the martyrs under the altar, "How long?" (6:10). Guzik clarifies that the joy is not in suffering as such but in God's justice and the end of oppression. Henry says "heaven and earth, angels and saints, would join in it." This rejoicing is answered in 19:1–8.
- **"A mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone and threw it into the sea" (v21).** This acts out Jeremiah 51:63–64. Jeremiah's officer Seraiah tied a stone to the scroll of Babylon's doom, threw it into the Euphrates, and said, "Thus shall Babylon sink, to rise no more." A great millstone, turned by a donkey, could weigh tons. Jesus used the same picture for anyone who causes a little one to stumble (Matt 18:6). A stone thrown into the sea does not come back.
- **"Will be heard in you no more" (vv21–23).** Six times "no more" sounds, counting the "found no more" of v21: no music, no craftsmen, no mill, no lamp, no bridegroom and bride. These are the ordinary sounds of a living city, its art, work, food, light and marriage. Jeremiah 25:10 used almost the same list for the judgment on Judah. The last silence matters most. Babylon's weddings stop just before the wedding of the Lamb is announced (19:7).
- **"Your merchants were the great ones of the earth" (v23).** Here is the reason for her judgment, given in three parts (vv23–24). The first is that commerce had become the measure of greatness. Isaiah 23:8 says the same of Tyre, "whose merchants were princes."
- **"All nations were deceived by your sorcery" (v23).** "Sorcery" is *pharmakeia*. It comes from a word for drugs or potions, and Guzik takes the drug idea to mean Babylon's appeal works like an addiction. The lexicon gives "use of drugs, potions, spells" as the word's range, but in the New Testament and the Greek Old Testament it means sorcery or witchcraft (Gal 5:20). The Greek Old Testament uses this word in Isaiah 47:9, 12, the very passage behind Babylon's queen boast in v7. So the main point is that she bewitched the nations, though the picture of a drugged world fits the "wine" of v3.
- **"In her was found the blood of prophets and of saints, and of all who have been slain on earth" (v24).** The final charge is murder. Jesus said the blood of all the righteous, from Abel to Zechariah, would come on the Jerusalem of his day (Matt 23:35), and JFB connects the verses. Guzik argues that no single city could hold the blood of all who have been slain on earth, so Babylon must be the whole world system across history. Henry speaks of Babylon "destroying and murdering those whom she could not deceive." The chapter began with her luxury and ends with what that luxury cost.
- **Commentators:** All three hear Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel behind every line, and all three stress that the mourners grieve lost profits, not their sins. They differ on the city. Henry, a historicist, reads it as papal Rome and stresses God's people being "called out of Babylon, and called effectually." JFB, also a historicist, argues that Rome "was not a commercial city" and so takes the cargo as "spiritual wares," and he widens Babylon to "the WHOLE of the faithless Church" of both Testaments. Guzik, a futurist, separates commercial Babylon from the religious Babylon of chapter 17. He thinks a literal rebuilt Babylon is possible but more likely a figure for the whole world system, and he is careful to say that joy in v20 is joy in God's justice. His drug reading of *pharmakeia* is possible from the word's history, but the lexicon and the Isaiah 47 background point mainly to sorcery. The Tyndale notes, more preterist, keep the focus on first-century Rome: its slave market, its sea trade and its eventual fall.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter holds up a whole civilization that lives for luxury, sells human souls and drinks the blood of the saints, and it shows it gone in an hour. The kings and merchants have nothing to say but "Alas." The one thing heaven says to God's people is "Come out of her," a call to belong to another city while living in this one. That call is possible because of the one who was himself thrown out of the city and suffered "outside the gate," so that his people would know that "here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (Heb 13:12–14). Babylon's lamps go out and her wedding songs stop. In the next chapter heaven sings, because the true Bridegroom has come for his bride.
