*Setting:* Isaiah 60–62 announced Zion's coming glory, and 61:2 promised both "the year of the LORD's favor" and "the day of vengeance of our God." Chapter 63 opens with that day of vengeance in a vivid scene: a warrior striding home from Edom, his clothes soaked red. Then the mood turns completely. From v7 the prophet speaks for God's people, remembering the Exodus and God's mercy, and from v15 he begins a long prayer that runs through the end of chapter 64. Edom was Israel's southeastern neighbor, descended from Jacob's brother Esau (Gen 25:30; 36:1). The two nations were kin and bitter enemies, and Edom cheered and looted when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC (Ps 137:7; Obad 10–14). Isaiah wrote around 740–700 BC; these chapters speak to the people after the exile, praying in the rubble.

#### The LORD's Day of Vengeance (vv1–6)
- **The scene.** The section is a dialogue, set like a watchman on the wall seeing a figure approach. A voice asks, "Who is this?" (v1), the warrior answers, the voice asks, "Why is your apparel red?" (v2), and the warrior answers at length (vv3–6). It reads like the ancient question-and-answer of Psalm 24:8: "Who is this King of glory? The LORD, strong and mighty."
- **"Who is this who comes from Edom, in crimsoned garments from Bozrah" (v1).** Bozrah was Edom's chief city, a fortress about 25 miles southeast of the Dead Sea. The names are wordplays. Edom sounds like *ʾadom*, "red," the word in v2, and Esau was nicknamed Edom after the red stew he sold his birthright for (Gen 25:30). Bozrah (*botsrah*) sounds like *batsar*, "to gather grapes," as the Tyndale notes point out. The sounds set up the winepress picture before it is even named. Isaiah 34 already pictured the LORD's sword "bathed in blood… a sacrifice in Bozrah" (34:5–6).
- **Why Edom?** Edom stands for every enemy of God's people. The Tyndale notes say its judgment typifies the judgment of all the nations, and JFB calls it "the type of the last and most bitter foes of God and His people." Notice that v3 and v6 say "peoples," plural. Edom is the first name on a much longer list (compare Obad 15: "the day of the LORD is near upon all the nations").
- **"It is I, speaking in righteousness, mighty to save" (v1).** The warrior's answer is surprising. A blood-soaked fighter introduces himself as a Savior. His judgment of the enemy *is* the rescue of the oppressed. "Mighty to save" has become a favorite phrase in Christian worship for that reason.
- **"Why is your apparel red, and your garments like his who treads in the winepress?" (v2).** Grapes were trodden by foot in a stone vat, and the red juice splashed the treaders' legs and clothes. Harvest songs and shouts went with the work (Jer 25:30; 48:33). Here the joyful harvest becomes a picture of judgment.
- **"I have trodden the winepress alone, and from the peoples no one was with me" (v3).** "Alone" is the key word. No ally fought alongside him. The same idea came in 59:16: "He saw that there was no man… then his own arm brought him salvation." Many Christians have sung this verse of Christ's lonely sufferings on the cross, and Christian hymns and art often use it that way. In context, though, the blood is the enemies' blood, not his own, and JFB puts it plainly: he treads "not as a sufferer, but as an inflicter of vengeance." Revelation draws directly from this passage when Christ rides out to judge: "He is clothed in a robe dipped in blood… He will tread the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God the Almighty" (Rev 19:13–15; see also 14:19–20).
- **"For the day of vengeance was in my heart, and my year of redemption had come" (v4).** This repeats 61:2 with the same proportion: a *day* of vengeance and a *year* of redemption. Vengeance is brief and necessary. Redemption is the long, generous purpose. "Redemption" here is *geʾulay*, "my redeemed," from *goʾel*, the kinsman who rescues a relative. The warrior is acting as next of kin for his captive family. Biblical vengeance is not personal spite. It is God, as rightful judge, setting wrongs right (Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19).
- **"I looked, but there was no one to help; I was appalled" (v5).** God "looking" and finding no helper repeats 59:16 almost word for word. In 59:16 his righteousness upheld him; here his "wrath upheld me," as JFB observes. Wrath in Scripture is God's settled, holy opposition to evil, not a loss of temper.
- **"I made them drunk in my wrath" (v6).** The cup of God's wrath is a common picture in the prophets (51:17, 22; Jer 25:15–16). Those who forced it on Jerusalem now drink it themselves.
- **Past or future?** The Hebrew verbs are mostly past tenses, but prophets often describe a future event as already done because God's word makes it certain. Most interpreters read the scene as God's final judgment of the nations at the end of history, seen as already accomplished.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the warrior as Messiah returning to Jerusalem after avenging his people: "The same Messiah that destroys the unbeliever saves the believer." Henry reads it on two levels, as God's victory "over the Edomites themselves" and as the victory "obtained by the grace of God in Christ over our spiritual enemies." Guzik ties the scene to Christ's return in Revelation 19 and to John 5:22, where the Father has committed all judgment to the Son. He contrasts the two "alone" works of Christ, atoning at the cross and judging at his coming, and stresses, as JFB does, that the blood here belongs to the enemies.

#### The LORD's Mercy Remembered (vv7–14)
- **"I will recount the steadfast love of the LORD" (v7).** The voice changes. Now the prophet speaks as one of the people, "us." "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love, and it appears twice in this one verse, opening and closing it. The verse piles up words: steadfast love, praises, all he granted, great goodness, compassion, abundance. JFB says the plurals show language cannot fully express God's goodness. When prayer feels hard, the prophet starts by remembering.
- **"Surely they are my people, children who will not deal falsely" (v8).** God speaks as a father full of hope for his children. "Deal falsely" (*shaqar*) means to lie, betray, break faith. The Tyndale notes observe that God expected his own people to keep faith, unlike the nations' long history of treachery. The tragedy of v10 is that they didn't.
- **"And he became their Savior" (v8).** God's saving came first, before Israel had proved anything. At the Exodus he rescued them and then gave them the law (Exod 20:2).
- **"In all their affliction he was afflicted" (v9).** One of the tenderest lines in the Old Testament: God felt his people's suffering. At the burning bush he said, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people… I know their sufferings" (Exod 3:7), and in the days of the judges "he became impatient over the misery of Israel" (Judg 10:16). There is a textual question here, stated calmly. The written Hebrew text (ketiv) can be read "in all their affliction he was *not* afflicted" or "there was no adversary," while the traditional reading aloud (qere) is "to him there was affliction," that is, "he was afflicted." The two words sound identical in Hebrew. The ESV, KJV and most translations follow the second, and JFB mentions the first. The Greek translation reads differently again: "not an envoy or an angel, but his own presence saved them."
- **"And the angel of his presence saved them" (v9).** Literally "the angel of his face." At Sinai God promised, "My presence will go with you" (Exod 33:14), and the angel who went before Israel had God's name "in him" (Exod 23:20–23). The angel of the LORD often speaks as God himself (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6). Many Christian interpreters, including Guzik and JFB, see this angel as the Son of God before he became man. JFB says the language is "applicable to no creature." Others take it as a way of saying that God came in person. Either way the point is that God himself saved them, not a distant deputy. Henry: "the highest angel in heaven, even the angel of his presence."
- **"He lifted them up and carried them all the days of old" (v9).** Moses told Israel, "the LORD your God carried you, as a man carries his son, all the way that you went" (Deut 1:31), and God bore them "on eagles' wings" (Exod 19:4). Isaiah 46:3–4 adds that God carries his people from birth to old age.
- **"But they rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit" (v10).** This verse and v11 are two of only three places in the Old Testament that use the phrase "Holy Spirit" (the other is Ps 51:11). The Spirit here is personal: he can be grieved, which only a person can be. Psalm 78:40 recalls how often Israel "grieved him in the desert," and Psalm 106:33 says they "made his spirit bitter." Paul draws on this verse: "do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God" (Eph 4:30). Stephen accused the council, "you always resist the Holy Spirit. As your fathers did, so do you" (Acts 7:51). The passage moves through the LORD, the angel of his presence and his Holy Spirit, and many readers, including Guzik, see an early hint of the Trinity here.
- **"Therefore he turned to be their enemy, and himself fought against them" (v10).** The God who fought for them at the Red Sea (Exod 14:14) fought against them in the wilderness, the judges, and finally the fall of Jerusalem (Lam 2:5: "The Lord has become like an enemy"). The Tyndale notes list those periods.
- **"Then he remembered the days of old, of Moses and his people" (v11).** The Hebrew can be read with God or with the people as the one remembering. The ESV's "he" is most naturally the people, Israel recalling the past and asking, "Where is he…?" It is the question of someone in exile who knows God has done great things and cannot see him now (compare Judg 6:13; Ps 77:5–20).
- **"Who brought them up out of the sea with the shepherds of his flock" (v11).** The shepherds are Moses and Aaron (Ps 77:20: "You led your people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron"). Some manuscripts read the singular, "shepherd," meaning Moses. Moses was literally a shepherd before God called him (Exod 3:1).
- **"Who put in the midst of them his Holy Spirit" (v11).** God gave his Spirit to Moses and to the seventy elders who shared his load (Num 11:16–17, 25). Haggai later encourages the returned exiles with the same memory: "My Spirit remains in your midst. Fear not" (Hag 2:5).
- **"Who caused his glorious arm to go at the right hand of Moses" (v12).** Moses stretched out his hand, but God's arm did the work. JFB: Moses was only the instrument. **"To make for himself an everlasting name."** People still speak of the Red Sea crossing more than three thousand years later. God's honor and his people's rescue are bound together.
- **"Like a horse in the desert, they did not stumble" (v13).** "Desert" here is open grassland, where a horse runs freely over level ground. That is how sure-footed Israel was walking through the seabed (Exod 14:22). JFB and Guzik both explain the image that way.
- **"Like livestock that go down into the valley, the Spirit of the LORD gave them rest" (v14).** Cattle are led down from the hot, dry hills to a valley with water and shade. "Rest" points to the promised land itself, where God gave his people rest from their enemies (Josh 21:44; Deut 12:9–10). Hebrews later says a deeper rest still remains for God's people (Heb 4:8–10).
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines this section as the people meeting God's mercy with "a thankful acknowledgment of the great favours God had bestowed upon them," magnified by his relationship to them, his compassion, and their unworthiness. On v10 he writes, "The ungrateful rebellions of God's children against him are a vexation to his Holy Spirit." Guzik calls *chesed* perhaps the closest Old Testament word to the New Testament's *agapē*, and connects the lie of v8 to a dry well or barren tree that disappoints. The lexicon gives *shaqar* simply as "deal falsely, lie," so that second picture is an illustration, not the meaning of the word. JFB and Guzik both read the angel as Messiah, and JFB alone notes the textual alternative in v9.

#### Prayer for Mercy (vv15–19)
- **"Look down from heaven and see, from your holy and beautiful habitation" (v15).** The prayer that runs through 64:12 begins. Solomon's temple prayer asked God to "hear in heaven your dwelling place" (1 Kings 8:30). With the temple in ruins (v18), heaven is where they look.
- **"Where are your zeal and your might?" (v15).** These are the qualities God showed at the Exodus and promised again in 9:7 ("the zeal of the LORD of hosts will do this") and 42:13. **"The stirring of your inner parts and your compassion are held back from me."** "Inner parts" is literally "bowels," the Hebrew seat of deep feeling, much as we speak of the heart. Jeremiah uses it of God's yearning for Ephraim (Jer 31:20). The prayer does not doubt that God has compassion. It asks why he seems to be holding it back.
- **"For you are our Father, though Abraham does not know us" (v16).** God is rarely called "Father" in the Old Testament, perhaps about fifteen times, so this is striking (see 64:8; Deut 32:6; Mal 2:10). Abraham and Jacob ("Israel") were long dead and could do nothing for their descendants. Even if their own famous ancestors would not recognize these ruined people, God would. JFB paraphrases: even though Abraham, "our earthly father… disown us, Thou wilt not." The Tyndale notes say God's fatherhood is far more secure than national ancestry. Jesus taught his disciples to pray, "Our Father in heaven" (Matt 6:9), and the Spirit lets believers cry "Abba! Father!" (Rom 8:15).
- **"Our Redeemer from of old is your name" (v16).** *Goʾel* again, as in v4 and through these chapters (41:14; 44:6; 47:4). A redeemer is family, obligated by love to buy back what was lost.
- **"O LORD, why do you make us wander from your ways and harden our heart?" (v17).** This is hard to hear. The people seem to blame God. But the prayer is not denying their guilt (64:5–7 confesses it plainly). It recognizes that God has handed them over to their stubbornness as judgment, as he warned in Isaiah's call (6:9–10) and as Paul describes in Romans 1:24–28. JFB softens it: God "suffers" them to err and be hardened. The plea is that only God can turn them back: "Return for the sake of your servants." Jeremiah prays the same way: "Restore us to yourself, O LORD, that we may be restored!" (Lam 5:21). The verb for "harden" here (*qashach*) is rare, used elsewhere only of an ostrich dealing harshly with her young (Job 39:16).
- **"Your holy people held possession for a little while; our adversaries have trampled down your sanctuary" (v18).** The temple Solomon built stood about four centuries, a short time against God's promises, before Babylon burned it in 586 BC (2 Kings 25:9; Ps 74:3–7). The prayer argues that God's own house, and so God's own honor, is at stake.
- **"We have become like those over whom you have never ruled, like those who are not called by your name" (v19).** The worst part of the exile is not loss of land but looking exactly like any other nation, as if God had never claimed them. JFB notes the line can also be read, "We are thine from of old; thou barest not rule over them," setting Israel against the outsiders. In Hebrew Bibles the cry "Oh that you would rend the heavens" (ESV 64:1) is the end of 63:19, so the verse numbers of chapter 64 run one behind in Hebrew.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "a very humble and earnest prayer to God to appear for them in their present distress," pleading God's mercy, their relationship to him, their desire toward him, and their enemies' insolence. Guzik admits the prayer is not fully accurate theologically in v17 yet powerfully honest about anguish, and reads "Return" as a plea for God's glory to come back to the temple as in Ezekiel 43. JFB explains why Isaac is not named with Abraham and Israel in v16, since not all Isaac's descendants were in the covenant, and stresses that Israel's cause is God's own.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter holds two pictures side by side. The warrior who treads the winepress alone is the Christ of Revelation 19, who will judge every enemy of his people. The God who "in all their affliction… was afflicted" and carried his people like a child came closer still in Jesus, who took his people's afflictions on himself (53:4). The winepress of wrath that he will tread at the end is the same wrath he drank in their place at the cross, where, alone, he cried out to his Father. Because of that, the prayer "you, O LORD, are our Father" is no longer a bold hope. It is what every believer is taught to pray.
