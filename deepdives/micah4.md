*Setting:* Jerusalem, late 700s BC. Chapter 3 ended with the most shocking word Micah ever spoke: because of its corrupt rulers, priests and prophets, "Zion shall be plowed as a field… and the mountain of the house a wooded height" (3:12). Chapter 4 opens with that same phrase, "the mountain of the house," and turns it inside out. The hill that will be ploughed is the hill God will one day raise above every other. Micah's second round of messages (chs 3–5) moves from the sins of the present, through exile, to a coming kingdom of peace and a coming King. His contemporary Isaiah records nearly the same opening oracle word for word (Isa 2:2–4). At the time, Assyria was the great power, yet Micah names Babylon as the place of exile, more than a century before it happened.

#### The Mountain of the LORD (vv1–5)
- **"It shall come to pass in the latter days" (v1).** The phrase (*be'acharit hayyamim*) means "at the end of the days," the time when God brings history to its goal (Gen 49:1; Deut 4:30; Hos 3:5). The New Testament says those "last days" began with the coming of Christ and the Spirit (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2), and that they will be completed when he returns.
- **"The mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established as the highest of the mountains" (v1).** Mount Zion is not physically impressive. The Mount of Olives across the valley is higher. The point is importance, not altitude. In the ancient world, gods were thought to live on great mountains, like Baal on Mount Zaphon in the north. Micah says the LORD's small hill will tower over every rival's holy mountain. "Established" is *kun*, set firm, the same word used of David's throne (2 Sam 7:16).
- **"Peoples shall flow to it" (v1).** Rivers flow down from mountains. Here the nations flow uphill, drawn to God like water running the wrong way. It is a miracle of grace.
- **"Come, let us go up to the mountain of the LORD… that he may teach us his ways" (v2).** The nations do not come as conquerors or tourists but as students. They want to learn and to "walk in his paths." This is God's promise to Abraham coming true, that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3). Gentiles go up to Jerusalem to learn God's law, the reverse of Israel learning the ways of the nations.
- **"For out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem" (v2).** "Law" is *torah*, which means instruction or teaching, not only commands. Jesus told his disciples that "repentance for the forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47). On the day of Pentecost the word went out from that very city (Acts 2).
- **"He shall judge between many peoples, and shall decide disputes for strong nations far away" (v3).** God himself becomes the judge who settles the disputes that cause wars. JFB notes that "rebuke" (as the older translations render it) includes convicting nations of sin.
- **"They shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks" (v3).** Iron for weapons is reworked into farm tools. Since there is no more war to fight, there is no point keeping the weapons. Joel reverses the image when he calls the nations to battle (Joel 3:10). A bronze statue of a man beating a sword into a plough, given by the Soviet Union in 1959, stands in the gardens of the United Nations in New York, a sign of how deeply this verse has shaped the world's hope. But Micah's peace comes only when God judges the nations, not when they agree among themselves.
- **"Neither shall they learn war anymore" (v3).** Not just a ceasefire, but the end of military training. War becomes a forgotten skill.
- **"They shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree, and no one shall make them afraid" (v4).** This verse is Micah's own; Isaiah does not have it. It is an old picture of peace, used of Solomon's golden age (1 Kgs 4:25). A vine and fig tree took years to bear fruit, so only people secure on their own land could enjoy them. For readers who had just heard about land grabbers seizing family farms (2:1–2), this is justice restored: every family on its own plot, no one stealing it. There is irony too. In 701 BC the Assyrian envoy stood outside Jerusalem's walls and promised the people exactly this if they would surrender: "eat each one of his own vine, and each one of his own fig tree" (2 Kgs 18:31). The empire offered it as a bribe. God gives it as a gift. George Washington loved this verse and quoted it in many letters, including his famous 1790 letter to the Hebrew congregation in Newport, Rhode Island.
- **"For the mouth of the LORD of hosts has spoken" (v4).** "Hosts" means armies, heavenly and earthly. The commander of all armies promises the end of war. JFB says the promise is certain because God has spoken it, however unlikely it seems now.
- **"For all the peoples walk each in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of the LORD our God forever and ever" (v5).** The vision returns to the present. For now, the nations still follow their own gods. But Micah and the faithful remnant answer with a vow: we will walk in the LORD's name. JFB reads the opening "for" as "though." The future peace is a reason for faithfulness today.
- **Micah and Isaiah.** Micah 4:1–3 and Isaiah 2:2–4 are almost identical. Three views are held: Micah borrowed from Isaiah, Isaiah borrowed from Micah, or both quoted an older prophecy already known in Jerusalem. The two men were contemporaries in the same city, and the question cannot be settled. Either way, God gave this word twice, through two witnesses.
- **How is it fulfilled?** Christians agree that this is fulfilled in Christ, but differ on how. Many, like Henry, see it fulfilled in the church, as the nations stream to Christ through the gospel and the law goes out from Jerusalem, with completion in the new creation. Premillennial readers, like Guzik, see a future literal kingdom on earth, with Jerusalem as its capital, after Christ returns. Others see the whole vision fulfilled in the new heavens and new earth (Rev 21–22). All three take the text seriously and agree that the peace comes only through the King's rule.
- **Commentators:** Henry says these promises are for the Christian church, built on the ruins of the old order: it "shall be advanced and enlarged by the accession of the nations," "protected in tranquility and peace," and "kept close, and constant, and faithful to God." JFB explains Zion's height as one of "moral dignity," not geography, and notes small differences from Isaiah, who has "all nations" where Micah has "peoples." Guzik reads the passage as a millennial prophecy and stresses that enforced peace on earth does not by itself change hearts, so individuals still need faith in Jesus. He compares the passage's freedoms, from ignorance, war, want and fear, with Franklin Roosevelt's "Four Freedoms" speech of 1941, and links vv1–2 back to 3:12.

#### The LORD Shall Rescue Zion (vv6–13)
- **"In that day, declares the LORD, I will assemble the lame" (v6).** The Hebrew word is feminine, "she who limps," a picture of a hurt ewe that can't keep up with the flock. The same verb (*tsala'*) describes Jacob limping after he wrestled with God (Gen 32:31). God does not gather the strong and leave the stragglers. He gathers the injured, **"those who have been driven away and those whom I have afflicted."** He admits he is the one who wounded them, in judgment. He will be the one who heals them (Hos 6:1). Zephaniah later repeats this promise almost word for word (Zeph 3:19).
- **"The lame I will make the remnant, and those who were cast off, a strong nation" (v7).** Weakness becomes the raw material of God's new people. This fits his pattern: Sarah barren, Jacob limping, Gideon's tiny army, David the youngest son. Paul says the same about the church: "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Cor 1:27).
- **"The LORD will reign over them in Mount Zion from this time forth and forevermore" (v7).** Israel's human kings had failed. The LORD himself will be king, and his reign will have no end.
- **"And you, O tower of the flock, hill of the daughter of Zion" (v8).** "Tower of the flock" is *migdal-'eder*, the shepherd's watchtower from which a herdsman watched his sheep. There was a place by that name near Bethlehem, where Jacob camped after Rachel died (Gen 35:21). Later Jewish tradition, in the Targum on Genesis 35:21, said the Messiah would be revealed at Migdal-eder, and Jerome linked it to the shepherds who heard the angels at Jesus' birth. Here, though, it stands in parallel with "hill (*'ophel*) of the daughter of Zion," the fortified ridge in Jerusalem (2 Chr 27:3), so most take it as a name for Jerusalem watching over God's flock. JFB rejects Jerome's reading for that reason. Even so, Micah's next prophecy turns straight to Bethlehem (5:2).
- **"The former dominion shall come, kingship for the daughter of Jerusalem" (v8).** "The former dominion" is the kingdom as it was under David and Solomon, before the northern tribes broke away. God promised David a throne forever (2 Sam 7:16). It looked lost. It will come back.
- **"Now why do you cry aloud? Is there no king in you?" (v9).** "Now" brings the reader back to the coming disaster. Zion cries out in panic. The question may be a taunt (your king can't save you) or a lament (your king is gone). JFB weighs both. Either way, the human king and his counsellors are helpless, as Zedekiah would be when Babylon came (2 Kgs 25:4–7).
- **"Pain seized you like a woman in labor" (vv9–10).** Labour pain is the prophets' picture for sudden, unavoidable anguish (Isa 13:8; Jer 4:31). But labour pains have a purpose. They end in birth. Something new will come out of this suffering.
- **"For now you shall go out from the city and dwell in the open country; you shall go to Babylon" (v10).** The order of the steps is exact. The people leave the city, camp in the fields as prisoners on the march, and arrive in Babylon. In Micah's day Assyria ruled the world, and Babylon was a rebellious province. The Babylonian king Merodach-baladan sent envoys to Hezekiah (2 Kgs 20:12), and Isaiah warned that everything in the palace would one day be carried "to Babylon" (2 Kgs 20:17). Some scholars think this line was added after the exile. Those who accept prediction see here a striking case of it, as JFB argues.
- **"There you shall be rescued; there the LORD will redeem you" (v10).** "There" is repeated for emphasis. The place of defeat becomes the place of deliverance. "Redeem" is *ga'al*, the word for a kinsman-redeemer, the close relative who buys back family land or family members from slavery (Lev 25:25; Ruth 4). God acts as Israel's next of kin. Cyrus of Persia freed the exiles in 539 BC (Ezra 1:1–4), but Isaiah says the real Redeemer behind him was the LORD (Isa 44:24–28).
- **"Now many nations are assembled against you, saying, 'Let her be defiled, and let our eyes gaze upon Zion'" (v11).** A third "now." The enemy wants to see Zion violated and gloat over her shame. JFB lists Babylon's subject peoples and Judah's gloating neighbours like Edom (Ps 137:7; Obad 11–12).
- **"But they do not know the thoughts of the LORD; they do not understand his plan" (v12).** The nations think they are gathering for a victory. In fact God is gathering them **"as sheaves to the threshing floor."** Sheaves of grain were spread on a flat floor and beaten or trampled to separate the grain from the stalk. The very assembly the nations planned is God's harvest of judgment. Compare Psalm 2:1–4: the nations plot, and "he who sits in the heavens laughs."
- **"Arise and thresh, O daughter of Zion, for I will make your horn iron, and I will make your hoofs bronze" (v13).** Oxen were driven over the grain to trample it. Zion becomes a supernaturally strong ox. The horn was a symbol of strength (1 Sam 2:10; Ps 92:10). Iron and bronze mean unbreakable.
- **"And shall devote their gain to the LORD, their wealth to the Lord of the whole earth" (v13).** "Devote" is *charam*, the word for war spoil set apart wholly for God (Lev 27:28; Josh 6:19). The plunder the nations took by violence will be given back to its true owner. **"Lord of the whole earth"** is an old title from the conquest (Josh 3:11, 13). God is not a tribal deity.
- **Peace and threshing.** How does a chapter that begins with swords beaten into ploughshares end with Zion threshing the nations? The Bible's consistent pattern is that God's peace comes through God's judgment. Evil must be dealt with before it is ended. Revelation shows the same sequence: the nations gather against God's people and are defeated, and then the new creation comes (Rev 19–21).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "under Christ's government, all its grievances shall be redressed," and that the church's enemies "shall be destroyed in and by their attempts against it." JFB pictures the lame as worn-out sheep and Jerusalem as the tower from which the King and Shepherd guards his flock. He calls "there… there" in v10 emphatic: "the place of her calamity" becomes the place of her deliverance, with Cyrus a type of the greater Deliverer. He notes that the Hebrew of v12 is "sheaf," singular, the enemies bound together in one bundle. Guzik says Micah blends prophecies from different eras, moving straight from the coming kingdom to the Babylonian exile, and he reads vv11–13 as finally fulfilled in the millennium. He quotes Waltke: "The ox pulling the threshing-sledge represents the people of God."

#### Pointing to Christ
The King who reigns from Zion is the one Micah introduces in the next breath, born in Bethlehem near the tower of the flock (5:2). He gathers the lame, literally: "the blind receive their sight and the lame walk" (Matt 11:5). From Jerusalem his word went out, and the nations have been flowing to him ever since, from Pentecost to today. He is the Redeemer who bought his people back not with silver but with his own blood (1 Pet 1:18–19). And the peace of swords into ploughshares rests on him, the Prince of Peace, whose government and peace will have no end (Isa 9:6–7), who "himself is our peace" between peoples who were once enemies (Eph 2:14).
