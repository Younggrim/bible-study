*Setting:* One month after the exodus. Israel left Egypt on the fifteenth day of the first month (Num 33:3), crossed the sea, sang the Song of Moses, found bitter water made sweet at Marah, and rested by the twelve springs of Elim (15:22–27). Now they head south into the open desert of the Sinai peninsula, and the food brought from Egypt has run out. Chapter 16 tells how God fed a whole nation in the wilderness: quail in the evening, and every morning a strange "bread from heaven" that would last forty years. It is also a lesson in trust. God gives enough for each day, and he gives the Sabbath, before Sinai and before the Ten Commandments.

#### Bread from Heaven (vv1–36)
- **"The wilderness of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai" (v1).** "Sin" has nothing to do with the English word. It is probably linked with the name Sinai, and some connect it with a moon god of the same name. Its exact place is unknown, but it lies on the way south to the mountain. **"The fifteenth day of the second month"** is one month to the day since they left Egypt.
- **"The whole congregation… grumbled" (v2).** At Marah some grumbled (15:24). Now it is "the whole congregation," against both Moses and Aaron. "Grumble" (*lun*) and its noun "grumbling" come back again and again in vv2–12.
- **"Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt" (v3).** They wish they had died in the tenth plague. **"When we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full."** Slaves in Egypt did get rations, but "sat by the meat pots" is memory edited by hunger. A month earlier they were groaning under brutal labor (2:23). The Tyndale notes put it well: complaining blinds people to reality and glorifies a past that never was. **"You have brought us out… to kill this whole assembly."** They accuse Moses of murder.
- **"Behold, I am about to rain bread from heaven for you" (v4).** God's answer is not anger but provision. "Rain" is the word used for the hail (9:18) and the fire on Sodom (Gen 19:24); here God rains down food. Psalm 78:24–25 calls it "the grain of heaven… the bread of the angels."
- **"A day's portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not."** The manna was a test as well as a gift. Moses explains it later: God "humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna… that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but… by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD" (Deut 8:3). Jesus quoted that verse when he was hungry in his own wilderness (Matt 4:4).
- **"On the sixth day… it will be twice as much" (v5).** The Sabbath pattern is built in from the start, though it is not yet explained.
- **"At evening you shall know… and in the morning you shall see the glory of the LORD" (vv6–7).** Evening and morning, as in Genesis 1. Israel will "know" God through what he gives. **"For what are we, that you grumble against us?"** Moses and Aaron are only servants. **"Your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD" (v8).** Complaining about God's appointed leaders, in a matter where they are only carrying out his will, is complaining about God.
- **"The glory of the LORD appeared in the cloud" (v10).** "Glory" (*kavod*) means weight, the felt reality of God's presence. They look toward the very wilderness they feared, and God's glory is there. This is the first time the glory is said to appear in the cloud. It will fill the tabernacle (40:34).
- **"At twilight you shall eat meat, and in the morning you shall be filled with bread" (v12).** **"Then you shall know that I am the LORD your God."** The phrase that ran through the plagues for Egypt now becomes Israel's lesson too.
- **"In the evening quail came up and covered the camp" (v13).** Quail migrate across the Sinai between Africa and Europe in spring and autumn, in huge flocks. They fly low and land exhausted, so they are easy to catch. God's timing is the miracle. A second, more famous quail story in Numbers 11:31–34 ends in judgment on greed. Here it is simply a gift.
- **"A fine, flake-like thing, fine as frost on the ground" (v14).** When the morning dew evaporated, the ground was covered with small, thin flakes.
- **"What is it?" (v15).** In Hebrew, *man hu'*. The question became the name: **"the house of Israel called its name manna"** (v31). For forty years Israel ate "what-is-it." **"It is the bread that the LORD has given you to eat."** Moses gives the answer that matters: never mind what it is, consider who gave it.
- **What was manna?** Some have pointed to the sweet, sticky droplets left by insects feeding on tamarisk trees in Sinai, still called *mann* by the Bedouin. But that substance appears only for a few weeks a year, in small amounts, and does not melt in the sun, breed worms overnight, double on Fridays or stop on Saturdays. The text treats manna as God's direct gift, and it stops the day Israel eats the produce of Canaan (Josh 5:12).
- **"You shall each take an omer, according to the number of the persons" (v16).** An omer was about two quarts or two liters, a daily portion per person. Each household gathered for itself.
- **"Whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack" (v18).** Strong and weak, fast and slow, all ended with enough. Paul quotes this verse when urging the Corinthians to share with poorer believers: God's people should look after each other so that "there may be fairness" (2 Cor 8:13–15).
- **"Let no one leave any of it over till the morning" (v19).** Hoarding was forbidden. **"But they did not listen… it bred worms and stank" (v20).** Trying to secure tomorrow without God ends in rot. **"Moses was angry with them."** **"When the sun grew hot, it melted" (v21).** The manna had to be gathered early, every morning. Each day began with God's provision.
- **"On the sixth day they gathered twice as much" (v22).** The leaders are puzzled and report to Moses. They have not yet been told why.
- **"Tomorrow is a day of solemn rest, a holy Sabbath to the LORD" (v23).** This is the first time the word "Sabbath" appears in the Bible. It comes from the verb *shavat*, "to stop" or "rest," used of God resting on the seventh day (Gen 2:2–3). **"Bake what you will bake and boil what you will boil."** Prepare the food ahead. On the seventh day the extra manna **"did not stink, and there were no worms in it" (v24).** The same food that spoiled overnight on other days stays fresh on the Sabbath. Obedience is protected; disobedience rots.
- **Was the Sabbath known before Sinai?** Readers differ. Some, like Henry, think a seventh-day rest was known from creation and is here renewed. Others think this is its first introduction to Israel, now taught by practice before it is commanded at Sinai (20:8–11). Either way, it comes as a gift before it comes as a law: **"See! The LORD has given you the Sabbath" (v29).**
- **"On the seventh day some of the people went out to gather, but they found none" (v27).** There is always someone who has to check. **"How long will you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?" (v28).** God uses the same "how long will you refuse" he spoke to Pharaoh (10:3). Israel is acting like Egypt.
- **"Remain each of you in his place" (v29).** Later Jewish tradition developed rules about how far one could walk on the Sabbath from this verse (compare the "Sabbath day's journey" in Acts 1:12). **"So the people rested on the seventh day" (v30).**
- **"It was like coriander seed, white, and the taste of it was like wafers made with honey" (v31).** Coriander seeds are small, round and grayish-white. Numbers 11:7–8 adds that the people ground it, boiled it or baked it into cakes that tasted like cakes baked with oil.
- **"Let an omer of it be kept throughout your generations" (v32).** A sample was kept so that later generations could **"see the bread with which I fed you in the wilderness."** Aaron put it in a jar **"before the testimony" (v34).** "The testimony" means the tablets of the covenant kept in the ark (25:16), which did not yet exist. The narrator is telling us where the jar was eventually kept. Hebrews 9:4 says the ark held "a golden urn holding the manna, and Aaron's staff that budded, and the tablets of the covenant."
- **"The people of Israel ate the manna forty years" (v35).** This verse looks ahead to the end of the wilderness years, when the manna stopped at the border of Canaan (Josh 5:10–12). The narrator wrote with the whole journey in view. Through all their rebellion, God never stopped the bread.
- **"An omer is the tenth part of an ephah" (v36).** An ephah was a dry measure of roughly 20 liters, about half a bushel. The note explains a term that had become unfamiliar.
- **Commentators:** Henry says this chapter "gives us an account of the victualling of the camp of Israel," and notes that "it was not immediately against God that they murmured, but (which was equivalent) against Moses and Aaron." Israel "must live upon daily providence, as the fowls of the air," which Henry links to the prayer "Give us this day our daily bread." Hoarded manna "putrefied, and bred worms, and became good for nothing." He argues the Sabbath was known "not only before the giving of the law upon Mount Sinai, but before the bringing of Israel out of Egypt." JFB places the wilderness of Sin in "the great plain" of El-Kaa, calls the quail "of the gallinaceous kind" (like chickens and pheasants) brought by "a wind from the Lord," and compares the tamarisk gum, which is sweet like honey but differs from Israel's manna in "not breeding worms," among other things. Guzik observes that the people had to gather the manna themselves, as God would not do their part, and that the seventh-day searchers picture people looking for satisfaction where God has said none will be found. He also says the end of the manna was its own kind of blessing, as God returned Israel to ordinary provision through work.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus makes this chapter about himself. After feeding the five thousand, the crowd asked for a sign, quoting "He gave them bread from heaven to eat." Jesus answered that the Father "gives you the true bread from heaven… I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger" (John 6:31–35). The manna kept Israel alive for a day, and "your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died," but "if anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever. And the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (John 6:49–51). He taught his disciples to pray "give us this day our daily bread" (Matt 6:11), trusting the Father one morning at a time. The God who sent manna to grumblers who did not deserve it sent his Son to sinners who did not deserve him. And Jesus promises the one who conquers "some of the hidden manna" (Rev 2:17).
