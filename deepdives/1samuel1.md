*Setting:* The last days of the judges, roughly 1100 BC. Judges ended with "there was no king in Israel" (Judg 21:25), and Ruth ended with the name David (Ruth 4:22). The books of Samuel (one book in the Hebrew Bible) tell how Israel moved from judges to kings: through Samuel, the last judge and a prophet; Saul, the first king; and David, the king after God's heart. The writer is not named. Jewish tradition credited Samuel with the early part, but the book records his death (25:1), so others finished it. The story does not begin with a battle or a palace. It begins in a troubled household, with a woman who cannot have children and prays. The tabernacle stands at Shiloh in the hill country of Ephraim, where it has been since Joshua (Josh 18:1), served by the aging priest Eli and his corrupt sons.

#### The Birth of Samuel (vv1–20)
- **"A certain man of Ramathaim-zophim… whose name was Elkanah" (v1).** The opening is almost identical to the openings of the stories of Samson's father (Judg 13:2) and Micah (Judg 17:1). The reader expects another dark Judges story. This one goes a different way. Ramathaim, "the two heights," is the full name of Ramah, Samuel's hometown (v19). Its exact site is debated, but it lay in the hills north of Jerusalem. "Zophim" connects it to the district named after Elkanah's ancestor Zuph (9:5).
- **"An Ephrathite" (v1).** Here this means an Ephraimite, a man living in Ephraim, not someone from Ephrathah near Bethlehem as in Ruth 1:2. First Chronicles 6:22–28, 33–38 lists Elkanah and Samuel among the Levites of the Kohath clan. Levites lived in towns among the other tribes, so he could be a Levite by descent and an Ephraimite by residence. The long family line suggests a man of standing.
- **"He had two wives… Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children" (v2).** Hannah means "grace" or "favor." Peninnah may mean "pearl" or "coral." Hannah is named first, probably the first wife, and Peninnah was perhaps taken because Hannah had no children. Scripture records polygamy without approving it, and every such household in Genesis is full of rivalry: Sarah and Hagar, Rachel and Leah. The writer simply states the painful fact.
- **"Year by year… to worship and to sacrifice to the LORD of hosts at Shiloh" (v3).** The law called men to appear before the LORD at three festivals a year (Exod 23:14–17; Deut 16:16). Elkanah's "yearly" trip may be a family pilgrimage at one of them, probably the autumn Feast of Booths. This is the first time in the Bible that God is called "the LORD of hosts," *YHWH tseva'ot*. "Hosts" means armies, especially the armies of heaven. The title will run all through the prophets (more than 200 times). In a book about Israel's wars, it reminds the reader who commands every army.
- **"Where the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests" (v3).** They are mentioned in passing, but chapter 2 will expose them as "worthless men" who "did not know the LORD" (2:12). Phinehas bears the name of Aaron's faithful grandson (Num 25:7–13; Judg 20:28), which only makes his corruption sadder. Elkanah worships faithfully even though the priests are wicked.
- **"Portions… but to Hannah he gave a double portion, because he loved her" (vv4–5).** Peace offerings were shared: part was burned, part went to the priest, and the worshiper's family ate the rest as a festive meal (Lev 7:11–18). The Hebrew of "double portion" is difficult. It may mean a double share or one choice portion. Either way it showed his love, and it probably made Peninnah's jealousy worse.
- **"The LORD had closed her womb" (vv5–6).** The narrator says it twice. Hannah's barrenness is not an accident, and it is not presented as punishment. It is God's doing for a purpose not yet revealed. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel and Samson's mother were all barren before bearing a child God had a special role for.
- **"Her rival used to provoke her grievously" (vv6–7).** "Rival" (*tsarah*) is the technical word for a co-wife, and it sounds like the word for distress. "Provoke" means to vex or irritate, and it happened "year by year," at the very place and moment of worship. Hannah wept and would not eat the festival meal.
- **"Am I not more to you than ten sons?" (v8).** Elkanah's love is real, and his question is tender. But it misses her grief. Being loved by her husband did not remove the shame of barrenness in that culture, or her longing for a child. Compare Ruth 4:15, where the women tell Naomi that Ruth "is more to you than seven sons."
- **"Eli the priest was sitting on the seat beside the doorpost of the temple of the LORD" (v9).** "Temple" (*hekal*) means palace or sanctuary. By now the tabernacle at Shiloh had some permanent structure, with doors (3:15). Eli is sitting on a "seat," the word often used for a throne. He will fall from this same seat when he dies (4:18).
- **"She was deeply distressed and prayed to the LORD and wept bitterly" (v10).** "Deeply distressed" is literally "bitter of soul," the same phrase used of the "angry fellows" of Judges 18:25 and close to Naomi's "call me Mara" (Ruth 1:20). Hannah takes her bitterness to God rather than to Peninnah.
- **The vow (v11).** "If you will indeed look on the affliction of your servant and remember me and not forget your servant." She uses the language of God's care in Genesis: he "looked on" Leah's affliction (Gen 29:32) and "remembered" Rachel (Gen 30:22). She calls herself "your servant" three times. She promises that her son will be given "to the LORD all the days of his life, and no razor shall touch his head." That is the sign of a Nazirite, a person set apart to God (Num 6:1–8). Most Nazirite vows were for a set time, and a Levite's service ran from age 25 or 30 to 50 (Num 8:24–25). Hannah promises her son to God for life, like Samson (Judg 13:5). A Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel and the Greek translation make the Nazirite link explicit.
- **"Hannah was speaking in her heart; only her lips moved" (vv12–13).** People in the ancient world normally prayed aloud. Silent prayer was unusual enough that Eli misread it.
- **"How long will you go on being drunk?" (v14).** Festival feasting at Shiloh evidently included drinking, and some got drunk. Eli is quick to rebuke an anguished woman praying, yet he will be slow to rebuke his own sons' real sins (2:22–25; 3:13). His spiritual sight is as dim as his eyes will become (3:2).
- **"I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD" (v15).** Her answer is respectful and clear. "Pouring out" is the picture of emptying a vessel completely (compare Ps 62:8, "pour out your heart before him"). She asks not to be counted "a worthless woman," literally "a daughter of Belial" (v16), the very phrase chapter 2 will use of Eli's sons (2:12). The irony is sharp: the woman Eli suspected is godly, and the sons he trusted are worthless.
- **"Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition" (v17).** Eli does not know what she asked for, but he blesses her. "Petition" is *she'elah*, from the verb *sha'al*, "to ask." This word will echo through the rest of the chapter.
- **"Her face was no longer sad" (v18).** Nothing had changed yet. She was still childless, and Peninnah was still there. But Hannah had poured out her heart and received a word of blessing, and she believed it. She went and ate.
- **"The LORD remembered her" (v19).** God had never forgotten her. "Remember" in Scripture means to act on behalf of someone in faithfulness to a promise, as God "remembered" Noah (Gen 8:1) and his covenant with Abraham (Exod 2:24).
- **"She called his name Samuel, for she said, 'I have asked for him from the LORD'" (v20).** The name Samuel (*Shemu'el*) most likely means "name of God" or "his name is God." It sounds partly like *sha'al*, "ask," and partly like *shama'*, "hear," as in "God heard." Hebrew name explanations are often wordplay on sound rather than strict etymology, and Hannah's point is clear: this child is God's answer to her asking. Some scholars notice that "asked" (*sha'ul*) is exactly the name of Saul, and the wordplay quietly anticipates the king Israel will soon "ask" for (8:10; 12:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry traces the household's trouble to "Elkanah's marrying two wives, which was a transgression of the original institution of marriage." He describes Peninnah as one who "upbraided Hannah with her affliction," and observes that "being in bitterness of soul, she prayed." He takes the name to "signify asked of God." Guzik notes that the title "LORD of hosts" appears for the first time here and some 260 times in all, that Hannah's lifelong vow went beyond a Levite's limited term of service, and that God can speak a true blessing through an imperfect priest, as he later did through Caiaphas (John 11:49–52). He also reads Hannah's changed face as faith receiving the promise before it was fulfilled. JFB says Elkanah "practised polygamy," contrary to the original law, explains Ramathaim as a "double city," and notes that Hannah's portion was the honor given a favored guest. The Tyndale notes add that Elkanah was a Levite and suggest Hannah may have hoped her son would be a deliverer like Samson.

#### Samuel Given to the LORD (vv21–28)
- **"To offer to the LORD the yearly sacrifice and to pay his vow" (v21).** Elkanah may have made his own vow, or he is joining in Hannah's. Under the law, a husband could cancel his wife's vow on the day he heard it (Num 30:6–8). By going up "to pay his vow," Elkanah confirms hers.
- **"As soon as the child is weaned" (v22).** Children in the ancient Near East were usually weaned at around two or three years old (compare 2 Macc 7:27, "nursed you for three years"). Weaning was celebrated (Gen 21:8). Hannah keeps him for those precious years and then will bring him "so that he may appear in the presence of the LORD and dwell there forever."
- **"Only, may the LORD establish his word" (v23).** No word from God has been recorded yet, only Eli's blessing and Hannah's vow. Elkanah's prayer is that God will bring his purpose for this child to pass. The Greek translation and the Dead Sea Scroll read "your word," meaning Hannah's vow.
- **"A three-year-old bull, an ephah of flour, and a skin of wine" (v24).** The traditional Hebrew text has "three bulls," while the Dead Sea Scroll and the Greek have "a three-year-old bull." The ESV follows the second, which fits v25 ("they slaughtered *the* bull"). JFB agrees it is "probably the true rendering." A bull was an expensive offering, an ephah of flour was more than half a bushel, and the wine went with the sacrifices (Num 15:8–10). Hannah gives generously. "And the child was young" is a simple line that carries a mother's whole heart: he was very small.
- **"I am the woman who was standing here in your presence, praying" (v26).** Some three years later she returns to the same spot. Eli may not remember her. She reminds him.
- **"For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition" (v27).** "Petition" is again *she'elah*. Her prayer was not general. She asked for this child, and God gave him.
- **"Therefore I have lent him to the LORD" (v28).** "Lent" is a form of the same verb *sha'al*, "ask." It means "to give what was asked," or "to grant on request." So the verse runs: "I asked, the LORD gave what I asked, so I give back to the LORD what was asked." "Lent" can mislead English readers, as if she expected him back. "As long as he lives, he is lent to the LORD" means the gift is permanent. The chapter has played on *sha'al* seven times.
- **"And he worshiped the LORD there" (v28).** "He" probably means Samuel, the small boy, bowing before the LORD, though some manuscripts read "they." Either way, the chapter that began with a woman too sad to eat at the place of worship ends with worship. Chapter 2 opens with Hannah's song of praise.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads "lent" as a permanent gift: "I have lent him to the Lord as long as he liveth." He notes the size of her offering, taking the traditional reading of three bulls, "no less than three bullocks." JFB prefers the reading "a bullock of three years old" and says Hannah waited because his age would then "enable her to fulfill her vow." Guzik explains that the Hebrew behind "lent" means she had asked for him, so Samuel was the answer to her prayer and is now returned to the God who gave him. He also notes that Elkanah supported Hannah's vow when he could have overruled it, and that the family ends by worshiping together.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hannah's story follows a pattern that runs through the Bible: when God's people are in a dark time, he begins his rescue with a barren woman and a miraculous birth. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother and Hannah all lead up to two births in the Gospels: John the Baptist, born to barren Elizabeth, and Jesus, born to a virgin. Mary's song (Luke 1:46–55) borrows heavily from Hannah's song in the next chapter. Hannah gave her son to the LORD to serve at his house for life. The Father gave his Son, and the Son gave himself fully, to bring us into God's presence for good. And Hannah's poured-out prayer and changed face before anything had changed point to the access every believer has through Christ: "Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Heb 4:16).
