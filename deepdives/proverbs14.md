*Setting:* The collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" continues (10:1–22:16; see Proverbs 10 on how it was gathered and how to read a proverb). The ESV has no headings inside it, so the headings below simply group the sayings in order. Chapter 14 is one of the richest in the book. It moves from the household (v1) to the nation (v34) and the royal court (v35). It contains the famous warning "There is a way that seems right to a man" (v12), some of the Bible's most honest lines about the hidden heart (vv10, 13), a strong word on the poor as made by God (v31), and one of the rare glimpses in Proverbs of hope beyond death (v32). The fear of the LORD frames it, at the start (v2) and in the middle (vv26–27).

#### Building or Tearing Down (vv1–9)
- **"The wisest of women builds her house, but folly with her own hands tears it down" (v1).** The Hebrew is literally "the wisdoms of women," the same plural as Wisdom in 9:1 ("Wisdom has built her house"). So the verse may mean "a wise woman builds her house," or, by a small change, "Wisdom builds her house, but Folly tears it down," echoing the two women of chapter 9. Either way, the house is a household and family. The Tyndale notes take building and tearing down as strengthening or weakening a family. JFB: the wise increase the household's wealth, and the foolish waste it. Guzik stresses how much power a wife and mother has to shape a home. The sharp detail is "with her own hands." The folly that wrecks a home is usually self-inflicted.
- **"Whoever walks in uprightness fears the LORD, but he who is devious in his ways despises him" (v2).** How a person walks shows what they think of God. The fear of the LORD is not mainly a feeling. It is a straight path. Crooked living is a form of contempt for God, whatever a person says with their mouth.
- **"By the mouth of a fool comes a rod for his back" (v3).** The Hebrew word for "rod" here (*choter*) is rare, a shoot or branch, and the phrase may read "a rod of pride." JFB takes it as "the rod of pride," the punishment that pride's words bring on. The Tyndale notes: fools hand others the weapon to use against them.
- **"Where there are no oxen, the manger is clean, but abundant crops come by the strength of the ox" (v4).** A farmer could keep a spotless stable by owning no oxen, but he would have nothing to plow with. JFB reads "clean" as "empty," with nothing to eat. Guzik applies it to churches: growth brings mess, and too much concern for tidiness can stifle it. Anything worth having, a family, a business, a ministry, brings some mess with it.
- **"A faithful witness does not lie, but a false witness breathes out lies" (v5).** "Breathes out" (*yapiach*) pictures lies coming as naturally as breathing. The Tyndale notes: truth and lies both come from character. Compare 6:19 and 12:17.
- **"A scoffer seeks wisdom in vain, but knowledge is easy for a man of understanding" (v6).** The scoffer may even look for wisdom, but his contempt keeps him from finding it. Wisdom opens to the humble. JFB: a "teachable spirit" succeeds.
- **"Leave the presence of a fool, for there you do not meet words of knowledge" (v7).** Practical advice: some company is not worth keeping. JFB: avoid those who cannot teach you. It pairs with 13:20.
- **"The wisdom of the prudent is to discern his way, but the folly of fools is deceiving" (v8).** The prudent think about where they are going. The fool's folly fools first of all himself.
- **"Fools mock at the guilt offering, but the upright enjoy acceptance" (v9).** The word *ʾasham* means guilt, and also the "guilt offering," the sacrifice for making amends (Lev 5:14–6:7). The verse can mean fools laugh at the idea of making amends, or, as JFB suggests, "sin deludes fools," that is, guilt mocks them. "Acceptance" (*ratson*) is favor, God's good pleasure. The Tyndale notes: the wise admit their sins and make peace, while fools defend themselves by laughing at guilt.
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws his church application from v4. JFB and the Tyndale notes take different routes through v9, JFB offering "sin deludes fools" and the Tyndale notes reading fools mocking guilt, which reflects how difficult the Hebrew is.

#### The Heart No One Else Sees (vv10–14)
- **"The heart knows its own bitterness, and no stranger shares its joy" (v10).** Every person carries pain and joy that no one else can fully enter. The Tyndale notes call bitterness and joy a merism, two extremes that cover every emotion in between. Guzik draws the lesson that we should be slow to judge others. Only God knows the heart completely (1 Kgs 8:39; Ps 139:1–4).
- **"The house of the wicked will be destroyed, but the tent of the upright will flourish" (v11).** A deliberate surprise: a house is sturdier than a tent. JFB notes the contrast. The wicked may live in stone, and still fall. The upright may live in canvas, and still flourish.
- **"There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death" (v12).** One of the most famous sayings in Proverbs, repeated word for word in 16:25. "Seems right" is literally "straight before a man's face." The danger is not obvious evil but the path that looks good. Henry: "It seems right to themselves; they please themselves with a fancy that they are as they should be," and "Self-deceivers will prove in the end self-destroyers." The Tyndale notes connect Jesus' narrow and wide gates (Matt 7:13–14). Guzik: since our own judgment is unreliable, we need God's revealed wisdom to know which way leads to life. "The way to death" is plural in Hebrew, "ways of death": a road that branches but always ends the same.
- **"Even in laughter the heart may ache, and the end of joy may be grief" (v13).** An honest line. A smiling face may hide a breaking heart. The Tyndale notes: appearances may not show true feelings. JFB reads it as the disappointment of wicked or untimely joy. Guzik says joy is good, but it should not replace the fear of the Lord. Ecclesiastes says something similar (Eccl 7:2–4).
- **"The backslider in heart will be filled with the fruit of his ways, and a good man will be filled with the fruit of his ways" (v14).** "Backslider" (*sug leb*) is one whose heart has turned away. Both people get what their lives produce. The second line is literally "and a good man from upon himself," which JFB reads as the good man keeping away from the backslider. The ESV supplies "the fruit of his ways" from the first line. Either way, everyone eats the harvest of their own choices.
- **Commentators:** Henry's lines on v12 are some of his sharpest in Proverbs. Guzik pairs v12 with 16:25 and stresses the need for God's word over instinct. On v10 Guzik and the Tyndale notes both draw the lesson that no one fully knows another's heart.

#### Caution, Temper and the Poor (vv15–25)
- **"The simple believes everything, but the prudent gives thought to his steps" (v15).** The naïve accept every claim, every rumor, every sales pitch. JFB: they believe "not from love but from heedlessness." Guzik adds that prudence examines one's own steps, not only other people's words. Believers are told to "test everything" (1 Thess 5:21; 1 John 4:1).
- **"One who is wise is cautious and turns away from evil, but a fool is reckless and careless" (v16).** "Cautious" is literally "fears," the same root as the fear of the LORD. "Reckless" (*mitʿabber*) means he flies into a rage or oversteps. JFB: he "rageth." "Careless" is "confident," trusting himself.
- **"A man of quick temper acts foolishly, and a man of evil devices is hated" (v17).** Two kinds of trouble: the hot-tempered man who acts on impulse, and the cold schemer who plans harm. JFB says the schemer is the more hated. The Tyndale notes: the short-tempered don't think before acting.
- **"The simple inherit folly, but the prudent are crowned with knowledge" (vv18–19).** The simple don't have to work for folly, it is their natural inheritance. The prudent wear knowledge like a crown. In v19, the evil "bow down" to the good in the end, as Joseph's brothers bowed before him (Gen 42:6; 50:18).
- **"The poor is disliked even by his neighbor, but the rich has many friends" (v20).** This is an observation, not an approval. JFB: "a sad but true picture of human nature." The very next verse judges it.
- **"Whoever despises his neighbor is a sinner, but blessed is he who is generous to the poor" (v21).** The neighbor of v20 who looks down on the poor is called what he is: a sinner. The Tyndale notes gather Proverbs' many sayings on kindness to the poor (3:27–28; 28:27; 29:7).
- **"Those who devise good meet steadfast love and faithfulness" (v22).** "Steadfast love and faithfulness" (*chesed weʾemet*) is the great pair used of God's own character (Exod 34:6; Ps 85:10). JFB takes it as God's mercy and truth. Those who plan good find God's faithfulness waiting for them.
- **"In all toil there is profit, but mere talk tends only to poverty" (v23).** Literally "word of lips." Big plans, endless talk and no work lead nowhere. JFB: "idle, vain promises and plans."
- **"The crown of the wise is their wealth, but the folly of fools brings folly" (v24).** The Tyndale notes say this is a general idea, since fools are sometimes rich and the wise sometimes poor (13:23; 16:8). The point is that the fool's folly produces nothing but more of itself.
- **"A truthful witness saves lives" (v25).** In a court where testimony decided guilt, an honest witness could literally save a life, and a false one could take it, as with Naboth (1 Kgs 21:10–13).
- **Commentators:** Guzik on v15 pushes the reader to examine their own path as well as others' claims. JFB treats v20 as description, not approval, and the Tyndale notes suggest it may even counsel moderation, having neither too little nor too much (30:8–9).

#### The Fear of the LORD, the King and the Nation (vv26–35)
- **"In the fear of the LORD one has strong confidence, and his children will have a refuge" (v26).** Fearing God produces confidence, not anxiety. The one who fears God has nothing else to fear. And the blessing reaches the next generation. JFB: "The blessings of piety descend to children."
- **"The fear of the LORD is a fountain of life" (v27).** Nearly identical to 13:14, with "the fear of the LORD" in place of "the teaching of the wise." Wise teaching and reverence for God are the same spring.
- **"In a multitude of people is the glory of a king" (v28).** A king's glory is not his palace but his people. The Tyndale notes: a ruler's success is the well-being of his people. JFB calls it the teaching of "a true political economy."
- **"Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding" (v29).** "Slow to anger" (*ʾerek ʾappayim*) is literally "long of nostrils," since anger was pictured as heat in the nose. It is one of God's own titles (Exod 34:6). A short temper puts folly on display. JFB: it makes folly conspicuous.
- **"A tranquil heart gives life to the flesh, but envy makes the bones rot" (v30).** "Tranquil" is literally "healing" (*marpeʾ*), as in 12:18. A calm heart is medicine for the body. "Envy" (*qinʾah*) is jealousy, passion directed at what others have, and it rots the bones from inside, the same word as the shameful wife in 12:4. JFB: a sound heart is "a source of health." The Tyndale notes add that freedom from jealousy is good for physical health. Guzik: letting go of resentment is worth the effort.
- **"Whoever oppresses a poor man insults his Maker, but he who is generous to the needy honors him" (v31).** This is one of the great sayings of Proverbs. The poor are made in God's image like everyone else (Gen 1:27), so contempt for them is contempt for the God who made them. JFB: "God is the Maker of the poor as well as the rich." It is repeated in 17:5 and turned positive in 19:17: "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD." Jesus went further: "as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matt 25:40). The Tyndale notes connect Israel's laws for the poor (Lev 19:9–10; Deut 15:11) and James 2.
- **"The wicked is overthrown through his evildoing, but the righteous finds refuge in his death" (v32).** The Hebrew reads "in his death" (*bemoto*). The Greek translation reads "in his integrity" (*betummo*), which differs by two letters swapped, and some versions follow it. The ESV keeps the Hebrew. The Tyndale notes say Proverbs rarely speaks of life after death, and some take the verse to mean God gives courage to face death calmly. Guzik reads it as one of the few glimpses in Proverbs of hope that goes beyond death. JFB: the righteous who trust God have assurance of help. Psalm 73:24 and 49:15 point the same way.
- **"Wisdom rests in the heart of a man of understanding, but it makes itself known even in the midst of fools" (v33).** Wisdom is at home and quiet in a wise heart. The second line is harder. Fools may notice wisdom even when they don't have it, or, following the Greek, wisdom "is not known" among fools. JFB contrasts the wise man's quiet wisdom with the fool who advertises his folly.
- **"Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is a reproach to any people" (v34).** "Reproach" here is unusual: the word is *chesed*, normally "steadfast love," but in a rare second sense "shame" or "disgrace" (Lev 20:17 uses it so). A nation's real greatness is moral. The Tyndale notes: not wealth, power or military victory. Guzik agrees: strength does not exalt a nation the way righteousness does. "Any people" means this applies to every nation, not only Israel.
- **"A servant who deals wisely has the king's favor" (v35).** The chapter ends in the royal court, where Joseph and Daniel found favor by serving wisely (Gen 41:38–40; Dan 6:3). JFB: the wise are discreet and prudent.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both read v31 as grounded in God as Maker of rich and poor alike. On v32 the Tyndale notes are more cautious than Guzik about life after death. Guzik and the Tyndale notes both see v34 as judging nations by character, not power.

#### Pointing to Christ
Proverbs 14 warns about "a way that seems right" but ends in death (v12), and it hints that the righteous find "refuge in his death" (v32). Jesus answers both. He is the way that does not merely seem right but is right: "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). And he turned death itself into a refuge for his people, because he entered it and broke it: "O death, where is your sting?" (1 Cor 15:55). He knows the bitterness no stranger shares (v10), because he was "a man of sorrows" who carried ours (Isa 53:3–4; Heb 4:15). And the God whom the poor bear the image of became poor himself, so that every act of kindness to "the least of these" is done to him (Matt 25:40).
