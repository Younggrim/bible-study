*Setting:* 841 BC. More than a decade earlier, after Ahab and Jezebel had Naboth murdered for his vineyard at Jezreel, Elijah announced that dogs would lick Ahab's blood, dogs would eat Jezebel, and Ahab's whole male line would be cut off (1 Kgs 21:19–24). Ahab repented a little, and God delayed the full judgment to his son's days (1 Kgs 21:29). Ahab himself died at Ramoth-gilead (1 Kgs 22). Before that, at Mount Horeb, God told Elijah to anoint Hazael over Syria and Jehu over Israel (1 Kgs 19:15–17). Hazael has now taken Syria's throne (8:15). Joram son of Ahab lies wounded at his palace in Jezreel, and his nephew Ahaziah king of Judah has come to visit (8:28–29). The army is still at Ramoth-gilead, east of the Jordan, under its commanders. One of them is Jehu. The long-delayed judgment on the house of Ahab falls in a single day.

#### Jehu Anointed King of Israel (vv1–13)
- **"Tie up your garments, and take this flask of oil" (v1).** "Tie up your garments" means tuck the long robe into the belt to run (compare 4:29; 1 Kgs 18:46). Elisha, now an old man and well known, sends one of the young prophets instead. A famous prophet walking into the army camp would be noticed at once. Jewish tradition says this young man was Jonah (compare 14:25), but that is only tradition.
- **"Look there for Jehu the son of Jehoshaphat, son of Nimshi" (v2).** This Jehoshaphat is not the king of Judah. Jehu was a senior army officer who had served Ahab. He was there years before when Elijah pronounced judgment on Ahab (v25).
- **"Pour it on his head and say, 'Thus says the LORD, I anoint you king over Israel'" (v3).** Anointing with oil set a person apart for an office from God (1 Sam 10:1; 16:13). Jehu is the only king of the northern kingdom said to be anointed by a prophet. **"Then open the door and flee; do not linger."** It is secret and dangerous. If Joram learned of it before Jehu moved, the young man would be the first to die.
- **"I have a word for you, O commander." … "To which of us all?" (v5).** The commanders are in council together. The young prophet is careful to single out one man and take him into an inner room.
- **"King over the people of the LORD, over Israel" (v6).** Even an apostate northern kingdom is still "the people of the LORD." God has not given up his claim on them.
- **"You shall strike down the house of Ahab your master" (v7).** The reason is given first: "that I may avenge on Jezebel the blood of my servants the prophets." Jezebel had hunted the LORD's prophets (1 Kgs 18:4, 13; 19:2). The house of Ahab will go the way of the two earlier dynasties God ended, Jeroboam's and Baasha's (v9; 1 Kgs 15:29; 16:11–12). Jehu is given a mission of judgment. How he carries it out is another matter, as chapter 10 and Hosea 1:4 will show.
- **"The dogs shall eat Jezebel in the territory of Jezreel, and none shall bury her" (v10).** This repeats Elijah's word (1 Kgs 21:23). To lie unburied and eaten by scavengers was the worst disgrace of the ancient world.
- **"Why did this mad fellow come to you?" (v11).** The officers dismiss the prophet as a *meshugga*, a crazy man. Prophets were often mocked this way (Jer 29:26; Hos 9:7), and Jesus' own family thought he was "out of his mind" (Mark 3:21). Hold onto that word: it comes back in v20.
- **"You know the fellow and his talk" (v11).** Jehu tries to brush it off, perhaps testing whether his fellow officers would back him. They press him, and he tells them.
- **"Every man of them took his garment and put it under him on the bare steps" (v13).** The steps of the house become a makeshift throne, and the cloaks a royal carpet. Compare the crowd spreading cloaks before Jesus as he rode into Jerusalem (Matt 21:8). The trumpet blast was the signal of a coronation (1 Kgs 1:34, 39). "Jehu is king." The army's support in one stroke made the coup possible.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Elisha sent all the needed words with the messenger: "Those whom God sends on his errands shall not go without full instructions." He and JFB both observe how quickly the officers turned, and JFB calls the stairs "a most convenient substitute for a throne." Guzik, quoting Trapp, notes that God's prophets have always been called mad by a mad world. He passes on the Jewish tradition identifying the young prophet as Jonah and notes it is only a tradition.

#### Jehu Assassinates Joram and Ahaziah (vv14–29)
- **"Let no one slip out of the city to go and tell the news in Jezreel" (v15).** Surprise is everything. Jezreel is about 45 miles from Ramoth-gilead, across the Jordan and up the Jezreel Valley. Jehu sets out at once.
- **"Is it peace?" (vv17–22).** The question comes five times in the chapter (vv17, 18, 19, 22, 31). The Hebrew is *shalom*, which means much more than the absence of war: well-being, wholeness, things being right. Joram's men want to know if the army's news is good. Jehu answers the first two riders, **"What do you have to do with peace? Turn around and ride behind me."** Both join him.
- **"He drives furiously" (v20).** The Hebrew is *beshigga'on*, "in madness." It comes from the same root as "mad fellow" in v11. The word appears only three times in the Bible, and in Deuteronomy 28:28 it is one of the curses God sends on a disobedient Israel. The writer seems to enjoy the wordplay. The officers laughed at the mad prophet, and now their new king drives like a madman. Jehu's reckless energy was known across the army.
- **"Met him at the property of Naboth the Jezreelite" (v21).** The two kings ride out to meet Jehu and stop, of all places, on the field Ahab stole by murder. God's word picks the spot.
- **"What peace can there be, so long as the whorings and the sorceries of your mother Jezebel are so many?" (v22).** "Whorings" in the prophets usually means spiritual unfaithfulness, worshiping other gods (Hos 2:2–5), and Baal worship involved sexual rites as well. "Sorceries" points to occult practices. Jehu's point is that there can be no *shalom* while the land is soaked in idolatry. Isaiah says it plainly: "There is no peace for the wicked" (Isa 48:22).
- **"Treachery, O Ahaziah!" (v23).** Joram turns to flee and warns his nephew.
- **"Shot Joram between the shoulders, so that the arrow pierced his heart" (v24).** Jehu draws "with his full strength." Ahab was killed by an arrow that found a gap in his armor (1 Kgs 22:34). His son dies by an arrow too.
- **"Throw him on the plot of ground belonging to Naboth" (vv25–26).** Jehu remembers riding behind Ahab when the LORD's word came, probably when Ahab went down to take the vineyard (1 Kgs 21:16–19). He quotes a detail not recorded in 1 Kings: "the blood of Naboth and the blood of his sons." So Naboth's sons were killed too, which made sure no heir could claim the land. The blood Ahab shed is repaid "on this plot of ground."
- **"Ahaziah the king of Judah… fled in the direction of Beth-haggan" (v27).** Beth-haggan ("house of the garden") is probably modern Jenin, south of Jezreel, on the road toward Samaria. He is wounded near Ibleam and dies at Megiddo. Chronicles adds that he hid in Samaria, was found and brought to Jehu, and was killed (2 Chr 22:9). The two accounts are compressed and hard to fit exactly. One likely reconstruction is that he was wounded, hid for a while, and was caught and died at Megiddo. Chronicles also explains why he died: "it was ordained by God that the downfall of Ahaziah should come about through his going to visit Joram" (2 Chr 22:7). He was Ahab's grandson and had made himself part of Ahab's house.
- **"Buried him in his tomb with his fathers in the city of David" (v28).** The king of Judah gets a royal burial. Chronicles says it was "for he was the grandson of Jehoshaphat, who sought the LORD with all his heart" (2 Chr 22:9).
- **An ancient inscription.** In 1993–94 archaeologists at Tel Dan in northern Israel found pieces of a stone monument set up by a king of Damascus, very probably Hazael. He boasts that he killed "[Jo]ram son of [Ahab] king of Israel" and "[Ahaz]iah son of [Jehoram] king of the House of David." It is the earliest reference outside the Bible to the "House of David." Most scholars think Hazael is claiming credit for deaths that Kings credits to Jehu. Ancient kings often took credit for what happened in their wars, and Joram was first wounded fighting Hazael (8:28–29). Some suggest Jehu acted as Hazael's ally for a time. The Bible gives the inside story: God was using both men.
- **"In the eleventh year of Joram" (v29).** This differs by one year from 8:25 ("twelfth year"). Kingdoms counted years differently, some counting the year a king came to the throne as his first year and some not, so one-year differences like this are expected.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws the moral of Jehu's answer: "The way of sin can never be the way of peace." He sees Ahaziah's death as the cost of his company: he fell because he chose Ahab's house. JFB keeps to geography, placing Ibleam near Megiddo, and notes that the death of Naboth's sons is implied in the seizure of the land. It does not try to reconcile Kings and Chronicles on Ahaziah's death. Guzik stresses that God's justice falls on the very land taken by murder. He notes that Jehu had no explicit command to kill Judah's king, but that God's purpose was at work in it.

#### Jehu Executes Jezebel (vv30–37)
- **"She painted her eyes and adorned her head and looked out of the window" (v30).** Women in the ancient Near East darkened their eyelids with kohl, a black powder made from antimony or lead ore. Jezebel is not trying to seduce Jehu. She is in her sixties or older. She dresses as a queen, to die as one or to stare him down. Archaeologists have found carved ivory panels from this period showing "the woman at the window," a common royal or religious motif, which fits the picture of her framed in the palace window.
- **"Is it peace, you Zimri, murderer of your master?" (v31).** It is the chapter's last *shalom* question, and it drips with scorn. Zimri was the officer who murdered his king Elah and seized the throne, then lasted seven days before Omri, Jezebel's father-in-law, besieged him and he died in the burning palace (1 Kgs 16:9–18). She is warning Jehu that usurpers come to bad ends. The difference is that Zimri acted from ambition. Jehu acts under a prophet's anointing.
- **"Who is on my side? Who?" (v32).** Jehu calls up to the window. **"Two or three eunuchs looked out at him."** The word *saris* can mean a castrated court servant or simply a court official. Either way, her own household staff turn on her the moment the wind changes.
- **"Throw her down" (v33).** Her blood spatters the wall and the horses, and Jehu drives his chariot over her body.
- **"Then he went in and ate and drank" (v34).** He sits down to a meal in her palace while her body lies in the street. It is cold, and it shows the kind of man Jehu is. Only afterward does he remember: "bury her, for she is a king's daughter." She was the daughter of Ethbaal, king of the Sidonians (1 Kgs 16:31).
- **"No more of her than the skull and the feet and the palms of her hands" (v35).** The town dogs, half-wild scavengers in the ancient Near East, have already done their work.
- **"This is the word of the LORD, which he spoke by his servant Elijah the Tishbite" (vv36–37).** Jehu recalls the prophecy in fuller form than 1 Kings 21:23 records it: her corpse "as dung on the face of the field… so that no one can say, This is Jezebel." The woman who had the prophets killed, who swore to kill Elijah, and who arranged Naboth's murder, ends with no grave and no name.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Jezebel misused Zimri's example, since Jehu acted under God's commission and Zimri under ambition alone, and concludes that the wicked leave behind nothing but infamy. JFB explains the eye paint as kohl and judges that her purpose was "not to captivate, but to overawe Jehu." On v36 it observes that Jehu's quotation of Elijah is "not a literal but a paraphrased quotation." Guzik, quoting Patterson and Austel, also reads her makeup as queenly defiance, and he notes that desecration of a body was considered worse than death in the ancient world. He points ahead to Hosea 1:4, where God judges Jehu's own house for "the blood of Jezreel."

#### Pointing to Christ
"Is it peace?" runs through the whole chapter, and the answer every time is no. There can be no peace while sin is unrepented and innocent blood cries out. The judgment Elijah announced was delayed for years, which was mercy, but it came exactly as spoken, down to the very field. Jehu was God's instrument but not a model king, and the Bible later holds him responsible for his bloodshed (Hos 1:4). Only one king brings real *shalom*. Jesus also had cloaks spread before him by people who hailed him as king, but he came not to shed the blood of the guilty but to give his own. "He himself is our peace" (Eph 2:14), "making peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20).
