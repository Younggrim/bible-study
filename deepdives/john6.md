*Setting:* About a year has passed since the Passover of chapter 2, and another Passover is near (v4), probably spring of AD 29. Jesus is back in Galilee. Chapter 5 ended with Jesus telling the Jerusalem leaders that Moses "wrote of me." Chapter 6 shows that claim in action. At Passover, the feast of the exodus, Jesus feeds a crowd in the wilderness as God fed Israel with manna, crosses the sea, and then teaches that he himself is the true bread from heaven. This is the only miracle, apart from the resurrection, recorded in all four Gospels. It is also the high point of Jesus' popularity in Galilee and the moment it begins to fall apart. The chapter begins with five thousand people wanting to make him king and ends with only the Twelve, one of whom will betray him.

#### Jesus Feeds the Five Thousand (vv1–15)
- **"The other side of the Sea of Galilee, which is the Sea of Tiberias" (v1).** The lake is about 13 miles long and 8 wide. Herod Antipas built the city of Tiberias on its western shore around AD 20 and named it after the emperor Tiberius. Luke places the feeding near Bethsaida, on the northeast shore (Luke 9:10).
- **"Because they saw the signs that he was doing on the sick" (v2).** The crowd follows for the miracles. This sets up the problem of the whole chapter: they want what Jesus gives more than they want Jesus.
- **"Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand" (v4).** Only John mentions the feast here, and it is the key to the chapter. Passover remembered the lamb, the escape through the sea and the manna in the wilderness. All three themes return in this chapter. Jesus apparently did not go up to Jerusalem for this Passover (7:1).
- **"Where are we to buy bread?" (v5).** Moses asked a similar question in the wilderness: "Where am I to get meat to give to all this people?" (Num 11:13). Philip was from nearby Bethsaida (1:44), so he would know the area. John tells us Jesus asked "to test him" (v6). He already knew what he would do.
- **"Two hundred denarii worth of bread would not be enough" (v7).** A denarius was a day's wage for a laborer (Matt 20:2), so this was about eight months' pay. Philip does the math accurately and concludes it's impossible.
- **"A boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish" (v9).** Only John mentions the boy and the barley. Barley bread was the cheap food of the poor. The loaves were small and flat, and the fish were probably dried or pickled, a relish to go with the bread. It was one person's lunch. The prophet Elisha once fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves, and there was some left over (2 Kings 4:42–44). Jesus does far more with far less.
- **"Now there was much grass in the place" (v10).** It fits springtime near Passover. Mark adds that the grass was green and the people sat in groups (Mark 6:39–40). "About five thousand" counts the men. Matthew adds "besides women and children" (Matt 14:21), so the total may have been well over ten thousand.
- **"When he had given thanks, he distributed them" (v11).** "Given thanks" is *eucharisteō*, the word from which "Eucharist" comes. It was the ordinary Jewish blessing over bread: "Blessed are you, LORD our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth." John doesn't describe the moment of multiplication. He shows only the result: "as much as they wanted."
- **"Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost" (v12).** Jews customarily gathered leftover bread after a meal. "That nothing may be lost" uses the same verb as Jesus' later promise: "that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me" (v39). God's abundance is not wasteful.
- **"Twelve baskets" (v13).** The word *kophinos* means a sturdy wicker basket, the kind Jews carried. There is one basket for each disciple, and perhaps a hint of the twelve tribes.
- **"This is indeed the Prophet who is to come into the world!" (v14).** Moses promised that God would raise up "a prophet like me" (Deut 18:15). The crowd connects the dots: Moses gave bread in the wilderness, and so has Jesus. Some Jewish writings also expected the Messiah to bring down manna again.
- **"Take him by force to make him king" (v15).** Galilee was a hotbed of revolutionary hopes against Rome. A man who could feed an army in the wilderness looked like the perfect leader for a revolt. Jesus "withdrew again to the mountain by himself." Matthew and Mark add that he first sent the disciples away by boat, perhaps to keep them from being swept up in the excitement (Mark 6:45–46). His kingdom is "not of this world" (18:36), and he will not be crowned by a crowd. He will be crowned with thorns.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Philip's arithmetic was correct but missed the point, since Philip thought in terms of money rather than in terms of Jesus. He sees the Shepherd of Psalm 23 in the green grass, and in the leftovers a Lord who is generous but not wasteful. JFB links "the Prophet" back to 1:21 and notes that Jesus apparently kept away from Jerusalem at this Passover, for the reason given in 7:1. On v15, JFB says Jesus went to the mountain to rest and pray, and from there kept watching the disciples' boat.

#### Jesus Walks on Water (vv16–21)
- **"When evening came, his disciples went down to the sea" (v16).** They head for Capernaum on the northwest shore, a crossing of only a few miles.
- **"It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them" (v17).** Darkness in John is never only physical (3:2; 13:30). The disciples are alone in the dark, on rough water, without Jesus.
- **"The sea became rough because a strong wind was blowing" (v18).** The Sea of Galilee sits about 700 feet below sea level, surrounded by hills. Cool air from the heights can rush down through the valleys and whip up sudden storms, especially in the evening.
- **"About three or four miles" (v19).** Literally "twenty-five or thirty stadia." A stadion was about 600 feet. They are well out in the middle of the lake. Mark says this was "about the fourth watch of the night," between 3 and 6 a.m., so they had been rowing against the wind for hours (Mark 6:48).
- **"They saw Jesus walking on the sea" (v19).** In the Old Testament, walking on the sea is something only God does: he "trampled the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8), and "your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters" (Ps 77:19). John doesn't explain how. He simply reports what they saw.
- **"It is I; do not be afraid" (v20).** The Greek is *egō eimi*, literally "I am." It is the ordinary way to say "It's me," but in John, and especially in a scene where Jesus does what only God does, it echoes God's name revealed to Moses (Exod 3:14) and the words of comfort in Isaiah: "Fear not… when you pass through the waters, I will be with you" (Isa 43:1–2). This is the first of several "I am" sayings in the chapter.
- **"Immediately the boat was at the land" (v21).** Only John mentions this. Whether it is a second miracle or simply means the rest of the trip passed quickly, the disciples reach shore safely with Jesus on board. Psalm 107:29–30 describes God calming the storm and bringing sailors "to their desired haven."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the disciples, many of them experienced fishermen, struggled for hours and covered only a few miles, and draws the lesson that obedience is sometimes hard. JFB calls the boat's immediate arrival "This additional miracle," found only in John, and notes that Matthew and Mark add "Be of good cheer!"

#### I Am the Bread of Life (vv22–59)
- **The crowd follows (vv22–25).** The crowd works out that Jesus did not leave in the disciples' boat, so they take boats from Tiberias and go to Capernaum. "Rabbi, when did you come here?" (v25) is a natural question, but Jesus ignores it and goes to their motives.
- **"Not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves" (v26).** They saw the miracle but missed the sign. A sign points beyond itself, and they never looked past their full stomachs.
- **"Do not work for the food that perishes" (v27).** Jesus doesn't despise physical bread. He just made it. But bread rots, and people who eat it get hungry again. "On him God the Father has set his seal": a seal marked something as authentic and owned. The Father has stamped his approval on the Son, perhaps at his baptism (1:32–34).
- **"What must we do, to be doing the works of God?" (v28).** They hear "work" and ask for a list. Jesus' answer turns the question around: "This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent" (v29). The plural "works" becomes a singular "work." God requires faith in his Son, and even that faith is God's own work in them (v44).
- **"Then what sign do you do?" (v30).** The irony is heavy. These people were fed by a miracle the day before. Now they ask for a sign, and they suggest which one. Moses gave manna for forty years, and Jesus has given one meal. Some Jewish writings expected that the Messiah would renew the manna: "the treasury of manna shall again descend from on high" (2 Baruch 29:8).
- **"He gave them bread from heaven to eat" (v31).** This quotes Psalm 78:24 (compare Exod 16:4, 15; Neh 9:15). Jesus' answer works through the quotation phrase by phrase, like a synagogue sermon. It was not Moses who gave, but God. It is not only "gave," past tense, but "gives," present tense. And the true bread is not manna but "he who comes down from heaven" (vv32–33).
- **"Sir, give us this bread always" (v34).** The request echoes the Samaritan woman's "Sir, give me this water" (4:15), with the same misunderstanding: they still want a supply of food.
- **"I am the bread of life" (v35).** This is the first of seven "I am" sayings in John with a picture attached: bread (6:35), light (8:12), the door (10:7), the good shepherd (10:11), the resurrection and the life (11:25), the way, the truth and the life (14:6), and the vine (15:1). Bread was the most basic food of life in that world. Jesus does not say he gives bread. He says he is the bread. "Whoever comes to me shall not hunger… shall never thirst." Coming and believing are the same act, described two ways.
- **"All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (v37).** The verse holds both sides together. God's giving guarantees that some will come, and the promise is open to anyone who does come. "Never cast out" is an emphatic double negative in Greek, "I will certainly not throw out." No one who comes to Jesus will be turned away.
- **"That I should lose nothing… but raise it up on the last day" (vv39–40).** "On the last day" appears four times in this section (vv39, 40, 44, 54). Eternal life begins now (v47), and it ends in bodily resurrection. Jesus' mission is to keep everyone the Father gives him (10:28–29; 17:12).
- **"So the Jews grumbled about him" (v41).** "Grumbled" (*gongyzō*) is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for Israel grumbling in the wilderness about the manna (Exod 16:2, 7–8; Num 11). History repeats itself: God gives bread from heaven, and his people complain. "The Jews" here are probably the Galilean synagogue crowd at Capernaum (v59), not the Jerusalem leaders.
- **"Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph?" (v42).** They know his family. How can a local carpenter's son say he came down from heaven? Familiarity becomes a barrier to faith (Mark 6:3).
- **"No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (v44).** "Draws" is *helkō*, which can mean "drag" (a net in 21:6, a man in Acts 16:19) and, figuratively, "draw, lead, impel." Christians have long discussed this verse. Reformed readers take it as God's effective drawing of those he has chosen, which always succeeds. Arminian and Wesleyan readers see a drawing that can be resisted, pointing to 12:32, where Jesus says he will draw "all people" to himself. Both agree that no one comes to Christ without God taking the first step.
- **"They will all be taught by God" (v45).** This is from Isaiah 54:13 (compare Jer 31:33–34). In the new covenant God himself teaches his people. Those who truly listen to the Father recognize the Son. No one has *seen* the Father except the Son (v46; 1:18), but the Father can still be *heard*.
- **"Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died" (v49).** The manna kept people alive for a day. It could not stop death. Jesus is bread that one may eat "and not die" (v50).
- **"The bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (v51).** Here the discourse takes a sharp turn. The bread is now Jesus' "flesh," given "for" (*hyper*, on behalf of) the world. That is the language of sacrifice. Jesus is pointing to his death. "Flesh" echoes 1:14: "the Word became flesh."
- **"How can this man give us his flesh to eat?" (v52).** The crowd takes him literally, and the idea is shocking. The Law forbade eating blood (Lev 17:10–14), and eating human flesh was unthinkable.
- **"Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood" (v53).** Jesus doesn't soften the image. He makes it stronger. Flesh and blood separated is the language of a death, a sacrifice. And this is Passover, when every family ate the lamb.
- **"Whoever feeds on my flesh" (vv54–58).** In v54 the verb changes from the usual "eat" (*esthiō*) to *trōgō*, which in classical Greek meant to munch or chew. The lexicon notes that by New Testament times it had become an ordinary word for "eat" (as in Matt 24:38 and John 13:18), so the change shouldn't be pushed too far.
- **What does "eat my flesh and drink my blood" mean?** Faithful Christians read this in three main ways. Catholic and Orthodox traditions see it as directly about the Lord's Supper, in which believers truly receive Christ's body and blood. Lutheran and many Anglican readers also see the Supper here, with Christ truly present. Most Reformed, Baptist and evangelical readers take it as a vivid picture of faith: believing in Jesus' sacrificial death and taking him into one's life as completely as food is taken in. They point to v35 and v40, where coming and believing bring the same results that eating and drinking do in v54. Many readers in every tradition see the Supper as the visible picture of what Jesus describes here, since John wrote long after the church began celebrating it, even though Jesus had not yet instituted it.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "The work of faith is the work of God." On v35 he notes that ordinary bread is lifeless and needs a living body to nourish it, but "Christ is himself living bread, and nourishes by his own power." He reads v37 as "not in the nature of a promise, but a prediction," while JFB prints its key clauses in capitals for emphasis: "ALL THAT THE FATHER GIVETH ME SHALL COME TO ME… IN NO WISE CAST OUT." JFB calls the drawing of v44 "an internal and efficacious operation," a Reformed reading, and Henry describes it as producing "not only a compliance, but a cheerful compliance." On vv53–58 both reject a sacramental reading. Henry calls it "a spiritual eating and drinking… not a sacramental," noting the Supper "was not yet instituted." JFB says the discourse "has nothing to do with the Sacrament of the Supper," though the Supper is "the visible embodiment of these figures." Guzik takes the words as a figure for faith that takes Christ in fully, and notes that others apply them directly to communion. He makes the point that bread admired, studied or sold satisfies no one. It has to be eaten.

#### The Words of Eternal Life (vv60–71)
- **"This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?" (v60).** "Many of his disciples," not the crowd, are now offended. "Hard" (*sklēros*) means harsh or offensive, not hard to understand. They understood well enough that Jesus was claiming to come from heaven and saying that life depended on his death. That was what they couldn't accept.
- **"What if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?" (v62).** The sentence is left unfinished in Greek. If they stumble at his coming down from heaven (v42), what will they make of his going back up? John's "lifted up" (3:14; 12:32) includes the cross, the resurrection and the ascension.
- **"It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all" (v63).** This is the key to the hard saying. "Flesh" here means human nature on its own. It is not the same as Jesus' "flesh" in v51. Merely physical eating, or merely human understanding, gives nothing. Life comes by the Spirit through Jesus' words: "The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life." Many readers see this verse as Jesus' own guard against a crudely literal reading of vv53–58.
- **"Jesus knew from the beginning… who it was who would betray him" (v64).** John wants his readers to know that Judas's betrayal didn't take Jesus by surprise.
- **"Many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him" (v66).** It is one of the saddest sentences in the Gospels. "Walked with him" was the language of following a rabbi on the road. They had been disciples and they stopped.
- **"Do you want to go away as well?" (v67).** The question in Greek expects the answer "no," but it is a real question. Jesus doesn't chase the departing crowd or lower the cost. He turns to the Twelve and lets them choose.
- **"Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life" (v68).** Peter's answer isn't that everything makes sense. It's that there is no one else. He may not understand the hard saying any better than those who left, but he knows where life is found. This is John's version of Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi (Matt 16:16).
- **"You are the Holy One of God" (v69).** "We have believed, and have come to know" are both perfect tense: a settled faith and knowledge. Later manuscripts read "the Christ, the Son of the living God," which the KJV follows, probably to match Matthew 16:16. The earliest manuscripts read "the Holy One of God," a rare title used by a demon in Mark 1:24 and echoing God's title in Isaiah, "the Holy One of Israel."
- **"Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil" (v70).** "Devil" (*diabolos*) means "slanderer" or "accuser." Jesus does not say Judas *is* Satan, but that he acts as Satan's agent. Satan will later enter him (13:2, 27). Jesus says it right after Peter's confession, much as he rebuked Peter in Matthew 16:23.
- **"Judas the son of Simon Iscariot" (v71).** "Iscariot" most likely means "man of Kerioth," a town in southern Judea (Josh 15:25). If so, Judas was the only one of the Twelve not from Galilee. Others connect the name with the *sicarii*, Jewish dagger-men, but that is less likely. John's final words, "one of the twelve," underline the horror of it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik observes that the saying was hard because it was clear, not because it was confusing. What the hearers understood was what offended them. He calls "the flesh is no help at all" the theme of the whole discourse. JFB reads v63 as meaning "mere flesh, could profit nothing," and only the Spirit gives life. All three treat Peter's answer as true faith: following Jesus may be hard, but there is no other place to go. Guzik puts it in his own memorable way: the hardest way to live, except for all the others. JFB calls Judas "the tool of that wicked one."

#### Pointing to Christ
At Passover, the feast of the lamb and the manna, Jesus feeds Israel in the wilderness, walks across the sea as only God can, and says "I am." The crowd wants another meal and a political king. Jesus offers himself: the true bread from heaven, the flesh he will give for the life of the world. Every image in the chapter points to the cross. Bread is broken, flesh is given, blood is poured out, and life comes to anyone who takes him in by faith. Many walked away because the message was too hard. Peter stayed, not because he had all the answers, but because the one with the words of eternal life was standing in front of him.
