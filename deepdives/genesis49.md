*Setting:* Jacob is 147 and on his deathbed in Egypt (47:28). He has made Joseph swear to bury him in Canaan (47:29–31) and has adopted Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh as his own (ch 48). Now he calls all twelve sons together for his last words. In the ancient world a father's dying words were weighty, and in this family they carried God's promise forward (27:27–40). This is the longest poem in Genesis, and it is prophecy as much as blessing. Jacob looks at each son's character, remembers what each has done, and sees the future of the tribe that will come from him. Some words are blessings, some are rebukes, and one, to Judah, reaches all the way to the Messiah. The poem is ancient and its Hebrew is difficult in places, so translations differ more than usual. Notes below flag the main differences.

#### Jacob Blesses His Sons (vv1–27)
- **"What shall happen to you in days to come" (v1).** The phrase is literally "in the latter days." Elsewhere it points to the far future and the age of the Messiah (Num 24:14; Isa 2:2; Hos 3:5). The Tyndale notes take it to cover both the conquest of Canaan and, beyond it, the messianic age.
- **"Listen to Israel your father" (v2).** He calls himself by both names, Jacob and Israel. The sons are about to hear the voice of the father of a nation.
- **The order.** Jacob begins with Leah's six sons (Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Zebulun, Issachar), then the four sons of the servant wives (Dan, Gad, Asher, Naphtali), and ends with Rachel's two (Joseph, Benjamin). Several sayings are built on puns on the son's name, which English cannot show.
- **Reuben: "my firstborn, my might… Unstable as water, you shall not have preeminence" (vv3–4).** Reuben had everything by birth: rank, strength, the right to lead. "Unstable" translates *paḥaz*, a rare word the lexicon gives as "recklessness, unbridled license," like water boiling over. The reason is stated bluntly: **"you went up to your father's bed"** (35:22). Reuben thought that act had been forgotten. It had not. His birthright went to Joseph's sons (1 Chr 5:1–2). Reuben's tribe never produced a judge, prophet or king, as Guzik and JFB both note, and in the time of the judges it stayed home "among the sheepfolds" while others fought (Judg 5:15–16).
- **Simeon and Levi: "weapons of violence are their swords" (vv5–7).** These two led the massacre at Shechem after their sister Dinah was raped (34:25–31). Jacob had complained then about the trouble they caused him. Here he condemns their anger itself: **"Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce."** He curses the anger, not the men. **"They hamstrung oxen"** means they crippled animals out of sheer spite. **"I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel."** Both tribes ended up scattered, in very different ways. Simeon had no territory of its own but lived in towns inside Judah's land (Josh 19:1, 9) and shrank badly in the wilderness (Num 1:23; 26:14). Levi had no territory either, but it was scattered as the priestly tribe in forty-eight cities across Israel (Josh 21), because the Levites stood with Moses at the golden calf (Exod 32:26–29). The same scattering became a curse for one and an honor for the other. Grace can turn even a judgment into a calling.
- **Judah: "your brothers shall praise you" (v8).** Judah's name sounds like the Hebrew for "praise" (29:35), and Jacob plays on it. **"Your father's sons shall bow down before you"** is surprising. That was Joseph's dream, and Isaac's blessing on Jacob (27:29). Here the bowing passes to Judah. Joseph gets the double portion; Judah gets the leadership. Given Judah's past, selling Joseph and sleeping with Tamar (37; 38), this is grace. But chapters 43–44 showed a changed man, the one who offered himself for Benjamin.
- **"Judah is a lion's cub" (v9).** The lion is the king of beasts. Judah grows from a cub to a full-grown lion who has made his kill, lies down, and dares anyone to disturb him. Matthew Henry notes that he is not pictured as "a lion rampant, always tearing, always raging," but as one at rest after victory. Balaam later uses the same picture of all Israel (Num 24:9), and Revelation calls Jesus "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" (Rev 5:5).
- **"The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler's staff from between his feet" (v10).** The scepter is the sign of a king. "From between his feet" pictures a ruler's staff planted between the feet of a seated king, and it may also mean "from his descendants," as the Tyndale notes explain. Kingship will belong to Judah's line. It came true in David, from the tribe of Judah, and in his royal line in Jerusalem (2 Sam 7:12–16).
- **"Until tribute comes to him" or "until Shiloh comes" (v10).** This is one of the most debated lines in the Old Testament, because the Hebrew letters can be read several ways. The KJV has *"until Shiloh come,"* taking Shiloh as a name or title of the Messiah. JFB suggests it means "the sent," "the seed," or "the peaceable one." The NIV and RSV read the letters as *"until he comes to whom it belongs,"* and Ezekiel seems to echo that: "until he comes, the one to whom judgment belongs" (Ezek 21:27). Guzik takes it this way. The ESV divides the letters differently, *"until tribute comes to him,"* which fits the next line, "to him shall be the obedience of the peoples." A few take Shiloh as the town where the tabernacle later stood, though that is hard to fit to the sense. All the main readings point the same way: Judah's line will rule until one comes whom the nations obey. Ancient Jewish interpreters, including the Aramaic Targums, read the verse as messianic, and so has the church. Guzik repeats an old claim that the rabbis mourned when Rome took away Judah's right to carry out the death penalty, thinking the scepter had departed without Shiloh coming. The evidence for that story is uncertain.
- **"Binding his foal to the vine… he has washed his garments in wine" (v11).** The picture is of overflowing plenty. Vines are so many that a man ties his donkey to one without caring if it eats the grapes, and wine is so plentiful he could wash his clothes in it. The prophets use the same picture for the messianic age (Amos 9:13). Christians have long noticed the details: the King of Zechariah 9:9 comes "humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey," and Jesus rode into Jerusalem on exactly that (Matt 21:2–5). The "garments" washed "in the blood of grapes" recall the Warrior of Isaiah 63:1–3 and the Rider of Revelation 19:13, "clothed in a robe dipped in blood."
- **"His eyes are darker than wine, and his teeth whiter than milk" (v12).** Some translate "eyes red with wine." Either way it describes a vigorous, healthy ruler in a land flowing with good things. The Tyndale notes connect this abundance to Jesus' first sign, turning water into wine (John 2:1–11).
- **Zebulun: "a haven for ships… his border shall be at Sidon" (v13).** When the land was divided, Zebulun's territory lay in lower Galilee, between the Sea of Galilee and the Mediterranean, but did not clearly touch either coast (Josh 19:10–16). It sat on the trade routes to the coast, and Moses' blessing says Zebulun would "draw from the abundance of the seas" (Deut 33:18–19). The Tyndale notes add that the oracle gives a general picture, not exact borders.
- **Issachar: "a strong donkey… he bowed his shoulder to bear, and became a servant at forced labor" (vv14–15).** Issachar's name sounds like the Hebrew for "hire" or "wages" (30:18). Its land was the fertile Jezreel valley, the richest farmland in Israel and the main highway for invading armies. Jacob's picture is of a strong animal that chooses comfort over freedom: it sees good land, settles down, and accepts a load. Guzik reads it as a warning that loving ease can lead to slavery. JFB reads it more kindly, as a patient, hardworking farming tribe.
- **Dan: "Dan shall judge his people" (v16).** Dan means "judge" (30:6). Dan was the son of Rachel's servant Bilhah, but Jacob says he will judge "as one of the tribes of Israel," a full member. Samson, a Danite, became one of Israel's judges (Judg 13:2).
- **"Dan shall be a serpent in the way… that bites the horse's heels" (v17).** A small snake by the path can bring down a horse and rider. That fits Samson's guerrilla tactics against the Philistines, and also Dan's later history: the tribe could not hold its land, moved north by a sneak attack on a peaceful town, and set up the first tribal idol shrine (Judg 18). Jeroboam later placed one of his golden calves at the city of Dan (1 Kgs 12:29). Dan is missing from the list of tribes in Revelation 7:4–8. Some early Christian writers, Irenaeus among them, guessed that the Antichrist would come from Dan, but that is speculation.
- **"I wait for your salvation, O LORD" (v18).** In the middle of the poem Jacob stops and prays. Perhaps the thought of a serpent striking at the heel brought back the first promise of all, that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head while it struck his heel (3:15). Perhaps he was simply exhausted by the troubles he foresaw. Either way, he looks past his sons to God. "Salvation" is *yeshuʿah*, its first appearance in the Bible. The name Jesus (*Yeshua*) comes from the same root. Guzik makes the connection; Jacob, of course, could not have known the name.
- **Gad: "Raiders shall raid Gad, but he shall raid at their heels" (v19).** Counting his name, four of the six Hebrew words sound like "Gad" (*gad, gedud, yegudennu, yagud*), a rattle of sound. Gad settled east of the Jordan, exposed to raiders from the desert, and became known for tough fighters (1 Chr 5:18–22; 12:8). Attacked often, it struck back. JFB says it was "generally victorious in the close of their wars."
- **Asher: "Asher's food shall be rich, and he shall yield royal delicacies" (v20).** Asher means "happy" or "blessed" (30:13). The tribe settled in the fertile coastal plain north of Mount Carmel, olive country fit to supply a king's table (Deut 33:24).
- **Naphtali: "a doe let loose that bears beautiful fawns" (v21).** The second half is uncertain. The KJV has "he giveth goodly words," and the Hebrew could mean either. The picture is of freedom and grace. Naphtali settled in the hills west of the Sea of Galilee, and its warriors fought bravely for Deborah and Barak (Judg 4:6, 10; 5:18). Guzik points out that Jesus' ministry centered in this region, fulfilling Isaiah's promise of light to "the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali" (Matt 4:13–16).
- **Joseph: "a fruitful bough, a fruitful bough by a spring; his branches run over the wall" (v22).** "Fruitful" (*porat*) plays on the name Ephraim, "fruitful" (41:52). The picture is of a vine planted by water, growing so well it climbs over the wall. The Hebrew of this verse is also difficult, and some translate "a wild donkey." The overflowing vine fits Joseph best: a blessing that spills over onto Egypt and the nations.
- **"The archers bitterly attacked him… yet his bow remained unmoved" (vv23–24).** This is Joseph's life story in a picture: his brothers' hatred, Potiphar's wife's lies, the cupbearer's forgetfulness. They shot at him, but his bow stayed steady, because his arms were strengthened **"by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob."** Joseph's survival was God's work.
- **"From there is the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel" (v24).** Jacob piles up names for God, five in three verses: the Mighty One of Jacob, the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel, the God of your father, the Almighty. "Shepherd" repeats what Jacob said in 48:15. "Stone" is the first time God is called the rock or stone of Israel, a title the Psalms and prophets take up (Deut 32:4; Ps 18:2; Isa 28:16), and that the New Testament applies to Christ (1 Pet 2:4–8).
- **"Blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep… blessings of the breasts and of the womb" (v25).** Rain from the sky, springs from underground, and many children: everything a farming family needed. Joseph's tribes did become large and strong, producing leaders like Joshua (Ephraim) and Gideon (Manasseh).
- **"On the brow of him who was set apart from his brothers" (v26).** "Set apart" is *nazir*, the word behind "Nazirite," someone consecrated (Num 6:2). It can also mean "prince." Joseph was separated from his brothers by their cruelty and set apart by God for their rescue. Moses repeats these words in his own blessing (Deut 33:16).
- **Benjamin: "a ravenous wolf, in the morning devouring the prey and at evening dividing the spoil" (v27).** The youngest son, the family's darling, becomes a fierce tribe. Benjamin produced the left-handed warrior Ehud (Judg 3:15), slingers who never missed (Judg 20:16), and King Saul (1 Sam 9:1–2). It also nearly destroyed itself in a civil war after defending the men of Gibeah (Judg 19–20). Much later came another Benjamite named Saul, the apostle Paul (Phil 3:5). Some church fathers read the verse of him: devouring the church in the morning of his life, dividing the spoil of the gospel in its evening. That is a devotional reading, but a memorable one.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry spends most of his attention on Judah, seeing Christ in the blessing: "dying Jacob, at a great distance, saw Christ's day, and it was his comfort and support on his death-bed," and the Messiah "came exactly at the time appointed." He also turns Judah's name into a lesson: "Those that are to God for a praise shall be the praise of their brethren." Guzik and JFB both note Reuben's tribe produced no leader, and both see Levi's scattering turned into honor. Guzik reads Issachar as a warning about the cost of comfort, while JFB praises its patient industry. JFB calls Jacob in this chapter an "inspired prophet" more than a dying man, and skips several of the most-discussed verses, including v11 and v18. Guzik sees in v18 the first mention of *yeshuʿah* and links it to Jesus' name.

#### Jacob's Death and Burial (vv28–33)
- **"All these are the twelve tribes of Israel" (v28).** This is the first time the phrase "twelve tribes" appears. Sons have become tribes. **"Blessing each with the blessing suitable to him."** Even the rebukes were a kind of blessing: each son was told the truth, and each still belonged to Israel. The Tyndale notes add that these are pictures of tribes, not verdicts on every person in them. Individuals in any tribe could find God's blessing by faith.
- **"I am to be gathered to my people" (v29).** Jacob repeats to all his sons the request he made of Joseph: bury me in Canaan. He describes the cave of Machpelah in legal detail, the field, the seller, the location, the purchase (vv29–32), just as chapter 23 recorded it. It was the family's only legal property in the promised land, and Jacob wants his sons to know exactly where it is and that it is theirs.
- **"There they buried Abraham and Sarah… Isaac and Rebekah… and there I buried Leah" (v31).** This is the only place Genesis tells us where Rebekah and Leah were buried. Jacob chooses to lie beside Leah, the wife he did not love as he loved Rachel (29:30–31). Rachel lies by the road near Bethlehem (35:19). In death, Jacob takes his place beside the mother of Judah and Levi, the mother of the royal and priestly tribes.
- **"He drew up his feet into the bed and breathed his last and was gathered to his people" (v33).** He had sat up to bless (48:2). Now, his work done, he lies down and dies in peace, as God had promised (46:4). "Gathered to his people" comes before any burial, which will take place weeks later in Canaan (50:13). It speaks of joining those who went before, a hint that death is not the end. Jesus pointed out that God is still "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" long after they died, "not God of the dead, but of the living" (Matt 22:32).
- **Commentators:** JFB says Jacob's detailed description of Machpelah was his way of declaring that he died in Abraham's faith. Guzik contrasts the Bible's attitude to death with both the ancient habit of resigning oneself to it and the modern habit of denying it. Jacob does neither. He faces it calmly, arranges his burial in the land of promise, and is gathered to his people.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jacob's last words are honest about his sons' sins, but they end in hope. The brightest line points to a King from Judah, the Lion who would rule the nations and to whom "the obedience of the peoples" belongs. Jesus is that King, "the Lion of the tribe of Judah" who conquered by being "a Lamb… as though it had been slain" (Rev 5:5–6). He rode into Jerusalem on a donkey's colt and washed his garments in his own blood, not the blood of grapes. And in the middle of the poem a tired old man says what every believer still says: "I wait for your salvation, O LORD." His name, when it came, was Jesus, "for he will save his people from their sins" (Matt 1:21).
