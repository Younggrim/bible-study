*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to Jewish Christians tempted to drift back from Jesus, closed chapter 12 with "a kingdom that cannot be shaken" and a call to "offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe" (12:28). Chapter 13 shows what that worship looks like on an ordinary weekday: love for fellow believers, open homes, care for prisoners, faithful marriages, freedom from money, and trust in leaders. In the middle the writer returns one last time to his great theme, Jesus as the true sacrifice, and calls his readers to leave the old system behind and follow Jesus "outside the camp." The letter then ends the way ancient letters did, with a request for prayer, a blessing and personal news. The writer is never named. Early on, the church in the East linked the letter with Paul, while others suggested Barnabas, Luke or Apollos. Origen, around AD 230, concluded that only God knows who wrote it.

#### Sacrifices Pleasing to God (vv1–19)
- **"Let brotherly love continue" (v1).** "Brotherly love" is *philadelphia*, love between brothers and sisters, the warmth of a family. "Continue" (*menō*, "remain") echoes 12:27, where only the unshakable things "remain," and v14, where this world has "no lasting [*menousan*] city." Brotherly love is one of the things that lasts. These believers already had it (6:10; 10:33–34). The call is to keep it going under pressure.
- **"Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers" (v2).** "Hospitality" is *philoxenia*, literally "love of strangers," a deliberate echo of *philadelphia* in v1. Love for the family must stretch to outsiders. Inns in the ancient world were few and often disreputable, so traveling believers and missionaries depended on Christian homes (3 John 5–8). The early church soon had to warn against freeloaders who abused this kindness, but it never stopped practicing it.
- **"Some have entertained angels unawares" (v2).** Abraham fed three visitors (Gen 18), Lot sheltered two angels in Sodom (Gen 19), and Manoah offered a meal to the angel of the LORD (Judg 13). The point is not to hope for angels but to welcome strangers as if they might be God's messengers. Jesus goes further: "I was a stranger and you welcomed me" (Matt 25:35).
- **"Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them" (v3).** Prisons in the Roman world did not feed their inmates. Prisoners depended on family and friends for food, clothing and comfort, and visiting them meant being publicly linked with them. These readers had done this before (10:34). "Since you also are in the body" most likely means "you too are flesh and blood, and it could be you." Some read it as "you are in the one body of Christ" with them. Both lead to the same compassion.
- **"Let marriage be held in honor among all" (v4).** The Greek has no verb, so it could be a statement ("marriage is honorable") or a command (ESV). Most take it as a command. "Among all" can also mean "in every way." Some ascetic teachers were already looking down on marriage (1 Tim 4:3), while the surrounding culture treated sex outside marriage lightly. The writer resists both: marriage is good, and the marriage bed is "undefiled," but "God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous." The first word covers sexual sin in general. The second is breaking a marriage vow.
- **"Keep your life free from love of money" (v5).** "Free from love of money" is one Greek word, *aphilargyros*, literally "not silver-loving." It is a qualification for church leaders too (1 Tim 3:3). These believers had lost property for their faith (10:34), and those who have lost things are tempted either to bitterness or to grasping.
- **"Be content with what you have, for he has said" (v5).** Contentment rests on a promise, not on circumstances. **"I will never leave you nor forsake you"** gathers God's words to Jacob (Gen 28:15), to Israel and Joshua through Moses (Deut 31:6, 8) and to Joshua (Josh 1:5). The Greek piles up negatives, five in all, something like "I will never, ever leave you, nor ever, ever forsake you." What God has said makes money a poor substitute for security.
- **"The Lord is my helper; I will not fear" (v6).** From Psalm 118:6, the psalm the crowds sang at Jesus' entry into Jerusalem (Ps 118:25–26; Matt 21:9). God speaks a promise in v5 and his people answer it in v6. "What can man do to me?" is not naive. These readers knew what men could do. It means nothing man does can undo God's help.
- **"Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God" (v7).** These seem to be the church's founding leaders, now dead. "Consider the outcome of their way of life" means look at how their lives ended, quite possibly in martyrdom, and "imitate their faith." They are the church's own small chapter 11.
- **"Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" (v8).** This sentence links v7 and v9. Leaders die, but Jesus does not change. "Yesterday" covers his work in creation and on the cross (1:2; 9:26), "today" his priesthood at God's right hand (7:25), and "forever" his eternal reign (1:8, 12). Hebrews 1:12 has already applied Psalm 102:27 to him: "you are the same." Since he is the same, there is no need for a new message.
- **"Do not be led away by diverse and strange teachings… not by foods" (v9).** The Tyndale notes suggest that some Jewish groups taught that sacred meals, linked to the temple altar, gave spiritual benefit, and believers were being drawn toward them. The writer does not say exactly what the teachings were, and scholars debate it. The principle is clear: the heart is "strengthened by grace," and food rules "have not benefited those devoted to them" (compare 9:10; Col 2:16, 20–23).
- **"We have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat" (v10).** Priests ate their share of many sacrifices, but not of the sin offering whose blood was carried into the holy place on the Day of Atonement. That one was burned entirely (Lev 6:30; 16:27). Christ is that sin offering, yet his people do "eat" from it, sharing in all its benefits by faith. Those who cling to the earthly tent and its system have no share. Most Protestants take the "altar" as the cross or Christ's sacrifice. Some Catholic and Orthodox interpreters connect it with the Lord's Supper, and many Protestants agree the Supper at least points to it. The text itself does not settle that question.
- **"Burned outside the camp… Jesus also suffered outside the gate" (vv11–12).** On the Day of Atonement the bodies of the bull and goat were carried "outside the camp" and burned (Lev 16:27), the place of the unclean, of lepers and of the executed (Lev 13:46; Num 15:35–36). Jesus was crucified outside Jerusalem's walls (John 19:17, 20). The place of shame became the place where the true sin offering was made, "to sanctify the people through his own blood."
- **"Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured" (v13).** For Jewish believers this was costly. It meant leaving the safety, approval and familiar worship of the temple community and siding openly with a crucified Messiah. Moses did the same when he "considered the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt" (11:26). The same "reproach" word is used of both.
- **"For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (v14).** Jerusalem itself was not the lasting city, and within a few years of this letter (AD 70) Rome destroyed it. Like Abraham, believers look for "the city that has foundations" (11:10; 12:22).
- **"Let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God" (v15).** "Through him" matters: no altar, priest or animal is needed now, only Jesus. The "sacrifice of praise" was a kind of peace offering given in thanks (Lev 7:12). "The fruit of lips" comes from Hosea 14:2, where the Greek Old Testament reads, "we will render the fruit of our lips." "Acknowledge" (*homologeō*) means confess openly, the same root as the "confession" believers must hold fast (4:14; 10:23). Praise here is public loyalty to the name of Jesus.
- **"Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God" (v16).** "Share" is *koinōnia*, fellowship expressed in giving. With the animal sacrifices ended, Christians still have offerings to bring: praise with the lips (v15) and generosity with the hands (v16). This is the "acceptable worship" of 12:28.
- **"Obey your leaders and submit to them" (v17).** Moving from former leaders (v7) to present ones. "Obey" is *peithō* in the passive, which carries the sense of being persuaded by and trusting someone, not blind obedience. "Keeping watch" is *agrypneō*, "to lose sleep," the picture of a shepherd awake at night. Leaders "will have to give an account" to God (Jas 3:1; Acts 20:28). A church that makes its leaders' work a "groaning" harms itself.
- **"Pray for us, for we are sure that we have a clear conscience" (v18).** The writer uses "we" for himself, as he often has (5:11; 6:9), and moves to "I" in v19. His mention of a clear conscience may answer some criticism of him we cannot now recover. The great conscience theme of the sermon (9:14; 10:22) comes home to his own life.
- **"That I may be restored to you the sooner" (v19).** He is kept away somehow, by imprisonment, illness or duty. The text does not say which. He believes prayer will affect when he arrives.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The spirit of Christianity is a spirit of love." He and Guzik both note the five negatives of v5, Henry saying they are "heaped together, to confirm the promise." On v4 Henry remarks that Christ honored marriage "with his presence and first miracle," and Guzik adds that the enemy wants people to indulge in sin outside marriage and feel guilty within it. On v10, Henry and JFB both take the altar as Christ and his cross. Henry: "Not a material altar, but a personal one, and that is Christ." JFB adds that the Lord's table represents that altar, but the eating is spiritual. On v13 Henry calls believers to go out "from the ceremonial law, from sin, from the world, from ourselves," and JFB writes that "faith considers Jerusalem itself as a camp, not a city." On v17 Guzik warns against leaders who abuse authority, and JFB calls the coming account "the strongest stimulus to watchfulness."

#### Benediction (vv20–21)
- **"The God of peace" (v20).** A title Paul uses often (Rom 15:33; 1 Thess 5:23). After so many warnings, the sermon ends with peace: God himself made peace with sinners through Christ.
- **"Who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus" (v20).** This is the only direct mention of Jesus' resurrection in Hebrews. The sermon has spoken constantly of his being exalted and seated at God's right hand, which assumes the resurrection. Here it is named. The wording echoes Isaiah 63:11 in the Greek Old Testament, where God "brought up from the sea the shepherd of the sheep," meaning Moses at the Red Sea. Jesus is the greater Moses (3:1–6) brought up not from the sea but from death.
- **"The great shepherd of the sheep" (v20).** Jesus is the "good shepherd" in John 10:11 and the "chief Shepherd" in 1 Peter 5:4. Here he is "great," as he is the "great high priest" (4:14). God promised to shepherd his people himself and to give them a shepherd from David's line (Ezek 34:11–24).
- **"By the blood of the eternal covenant" (v20).** God promised an "everlasting covenant" (Isa 55:3; Jer 32:40; Ezek 37:26), and "the blood of my covenant" (Zech 9:11). This blood ratified the new covenant once for all (9:12–15; 10:29). Many read "by the blood" with the resurrection: God raised Jesus because the covenant sacrifice was complete and accepted. Some theologians also see behind it an eternal agreement between the Father and the Son, but the plain reference is the new covenant.
- **"Equip you with everything good that you may do his will" (v21).** "Equip" is *katartizō*, used of fishermen "mending" their nets (Mark 1:19), and of putting something in working order. God's will is done by people God himself makes ready. **"Working in us that which is pleasing in his sight"** completes the thought. The writer has just spoken of "sacrifices… pleasing to God" (v16). Here God works that pleasing life in his people (compare Phil 2:13).
- **"To whom be glory forever and ever. Amen" (v21).** Grammatically, "to whom" could point to God or to Jesus Christ. Either fits a letter that opened by calling the Son "the radiance of the glory of God" (1:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the God of peace the one who "found out a way for peace and reconciliation between himself and sinners," and the blood "the sanction and seal of an everlasting covenant between God and his people." JFB reads the covenant as made between Father and Son. Guzik mentions both readings and compares the benediction to the priestly blessing of Numbers 6.

#### Final Greetings (vv22–25)
- **"Bear with my word of exhortation, for I have written to you briefly" (v22).** "Word of exhortation" is the phrase used for the synagogue sermon in Acts 13:15, so many see Hebrews as a sermon sent in writing. "Briefly" may raise a smile, since the sermon takes about an hour to read aloud, but the writer has repeatedly held back from saying more (5:11; 9:5; 11:32).
- **"Our brother Timothy has been released" (v23).** This is almost certainly Paul's companion Timothy. "Released" can mean set free from prison or sent off on a journey. Most take it as release from prison, though Scripture does not record his imprisonment. The writer clearly belonged to Paul's circle, which is one reason the letter was linked with Paul.
- **"Greet all your leaders and all the saints" (v24).** The writer addresses the congregation as a whole and asks them to pass greetings to their leaders, which may suggest a house church within a larger city church.
- **"Those who come from Italy send you greetings" (v24).** The Greek can mean people in Italy, or people from Italy now living elsewhere. Most scholars take the second: Italian believers away from home send greetings back, which would place the readers in Rome. That fits the earliest known use of Hebrews, by Clement of Rome around AD 96. Others read it the other way round, with the letter written from Italy.
- **"Grace be with all of you" (v25).** The sermon that warned so seriously ends on grace, the one thing that strengthens the heart (v9).
- **Commentators:** JFB assumes Paul wrote the letter and calls v25 his signature closing. Most modern scholars, and many older ones, think the style and the writer's statement that the message "was attested to us by those who heard" (2:3) point to someone other than Paul. Henry says Timothy "had been a prisoner, doubtless for the gospel." Guzik observes that these hints place the readers probably in Rome and show the writer knew Timothy, but they do not identify the author.

#### Pointing to Christ
Even the practical commands of this chapter rest on Jesus. Brotherly love, open homes, faithful marriages and freedom from money all lean on one promise, "I will never leave you," and on the Savior who "is the same yesterday and today and forever." The heart of the chapter is the sin offering burned outside the camp, Jesus suffering outside the gate, bearing the shame of the unclean so that his people might be made holy. Following him there costs something, but he is the great Shepherd brought back from the dead, and he is still equipping his people to do what pleases God.
