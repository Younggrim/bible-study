*Setting:* After Babylon, Assyria and Philistia, Isaiah's oracles against the nations turn to Moab, Judah's neighbor across the Dead Sea. Moab lay on a high plateau east of the Dead Sea, in what is now Jordan, a land of grain fields, vineyards and great flocks of sheep. The Moabites were relatives. They descended from Lot, Abraham's nephew (Gen 19:37), and God told Israel not to take their land (Deut 2:9). They were also old enemies: King Balak hired Balaam to curse Israel (Num 22), and Eglon oppressed Israel in the judges' days (Judg 3). Ruth, David's great-grandmother, was a Moabite, and David once sheltered his parents with Moab's king (1 Sam 22:3–4). David later conquered Moab, and after Solomon it passed to the northern kingdom, until King Mesha rebelled around 850 BC (2 Kgs 3). Mesha's own victory monument, the Moabite Stone, was found at Dibon in 1868, and it names many of the towns in this chapter. Chapters 15–16 are one oracle, a lament over Moab's sudden ruin. The invader is not named. The likeliest candidate is Assyria, which pressed Moab hard in Isaiah's lifetime (16:14).

#### An Oracle Concerning Moab (vv1–9)
- **"Ar of Moab is laid waste in a night… Kir of Moab is laid waste in a night" (v1).** Ar was a major city near the Arnon gorge, Moab's northern border. Kir is most likely Kir-hareseth (16:7, 11), the fortress capital in the south, today's Kerak, perched on a cliff. Two great cities, north and south, fall overnight. Henry: "Here are two cities lost in a night, though that is the time of quietness." The Tyndale notes stress how sudden and decisive the blow is. "Moab is undone" is literally "Moab is silenced," cut off.
- **"He has gone up to the temple, and to Dibon, to the high places to weep" (v2).** "The temple" is literally "the house," probably the shrine of Chemosh, Moab's national god (Num 21:29; 1 Kgs 11:7). Dibon was Mesha's capital, where his stone was found; it says he built a high place for Chemosh there. In disaster, Moab runs to its god. Henry notes they went to "where they worshipped their idols, there to make their complaints." Later the prophet will say the prayers do not help (16:12).
- **"Over Nebo and over Medeba Moab wails" (v2).** Nebo was a town named after the mountain where Moses saw the promised land and died (Deut 34:1). Mesha boasts on his stone that he took Nebo from Israel and devoted its 7,000 people to destruction for his god. Medeba is modern Madaba, famous today for a Byzantine mosaic map of the Holy Land.
- **"On every head is baldness; every beard is shorn" (v2).** Shaving the head and cutting off the beard were signs of mourning (Jer 48:37; Amos 8:10). Sackcloth, a coarse goat-hair cloth, was worn against the skin as a sign of grief (v3). People wail "on the housetops," the flat roofs where families gathered and where pagan worship was sometimes offered (Jer 48:38).
- **"Heshbon and Elealeh cry out… as far as Jahaz" (v4).** Heshbon had been the capital of Sihon, king of the Amorites, whom Israel defeated under Moses (Num 21:25–26). It was given to the tribes of Reuben and Gad, but Moab had pushed back into this land over the centuries. Mesha says he captured Jahaz from Israel. Guzik points out that these towns were once Israelite land. "The armed men of Moab cry aloud" means even the soldiers lose their nerve.
- **"My heart cries out for Moab" (v5).** This is the striking line of the chapter. Isaiah is announcing God's judgment on an enemy, and he weeps. There is no gloating. The Tyndale notes point out that the prophet joins in the lament to show how great Moab's pain is. JFB draws the lesson that those who warn of God's wrath should do it with "tender sorrow, not with exultation." God himself takes "no pleasure in the death of the wicked" (Ezek 33:11).
- **"Her fugitives flee to Zoar, to Eglath-shelishiyah" (v5).** Zoar was at the southern end of the Dead Sea, the little town where Lot fled when Sodom burned (Gen 19:22–23). Moab's story began in Zoar, and now its refugees run back there. "Eglath-shelishiyah" may be a place name or may mean "a three-year-old heifer," a young, strong animal never yet yoked; older versions translate it that way.
- **"At the ascent of Luhith… on the road to Horonaim" (v5).** These were roads climbing up from the Dead Sea valley. Horonaim also appears on the Moabite Stone. The refugees stream along the main routes weeping, and Jeremiah later borrows these exact lines (Jer 48:5).
- **"The waters of Nimrim are a desolation" (v6).** The Tyndale notes say the location is uncertain; many place it at a spring-fed stream near the southern Dead Sea. The grass withers and the green is gone. Whether by drought or because invaders stopped up the springs (as Israel did to Moab in 2 Kgs 3:25), the land that fed Moab's flocks dies.
- **"They carry away over the Brook of the Willows" (v7).** Refugees carry what they can. The brook may be the Zered, Moab's southern border with Edom. Everything Moab saved up is now a bundle on someone's back.
- **"Her wailing reaches to Eglaim… to Beer-elim" (v8).** These places are uncertain, probably at opposite edges of the land. The cry fills Moab from border to border.
- **"The waters of Dibon are full of blood" (v9).** Here the standard Hebrew text reads "Dimon," which sounds like *dam*, "blood." The ESV follows the Dead Sea Isaiah Scroll and the Latin, which read "Dibon." The Tyndale notes take "Dimon" as a deliberate play on "blood." Either way the picture is the same: the town's water runs red with the slain.
- **"I will bring upon Dibon even more, a lion for those of Moab who escape" (v9).** Now God speaks in the first person. Those who survive the attack will face a lion. Lions were a real danger in emptied, overgrown land (2 Kgs 17:25–26), and they are also a picture of further attackers. JFB takes them as fresh calamities and notes that the word is plural, against the view that they stand for Nebuchadnezzar.
- **A name map.** The chapter is almost a list of towns, more than a dozen, and the Tyndale notes observe that Isaiah clearly knew Moab well. The effect is like a news report naming town after town hit by a disaster. Each name is a real community with families in it. Jeremiah 48 reuses much of Isaiah 15–16, showing this oracle was well known and still relevant a century later.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the chapter in two parts: "great lamentation made by the Moabites, and by the prophet himself for them," and then the calamities that justify it. He reminds readers that the Moabites "were descended from Lot, Abraham's kinsman," and warns that "great changes and very dismal ones may be made in a very little time." JFB follows older scholars in dating the prophecy to early in Hezekiah's reign and suggests Assyria struck Moab for allying with Israel and Syria. Guzik traces Moab's family ties to Israel through Lot and Ruth and sees that history behind Isaiah's grief. He draws on Jeremiah 48:11, where Moab is like wine left too long "on its dregs," never poured from vessel to vessel, to warn that a life never shaken by trouble can grow stale.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah weeps over the people he warns. That is the heart of the God he speaks for, who does not delight in judgment. It is seen most clearly in Jesus, who wept over Jerusalem even as he announced its coming destruction (Luke 19:41–44). Moab ran to the high place of Chemosh and found no help. The next chapter points to the only real refuge, a throne in "the tent of David" established in steadfast love (16:5), and the line of the Moabite Ruth runs straight to that throne (Matt 1:5).
