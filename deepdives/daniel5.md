*Setting:* The story jumps forward more than twenty years from chapter 4. Nebuchadnezzar died in 562 BC, and Babylon went through a run of short, violent reigns. The last king, Nabonidus (556–539 BC), spent about ten years at the desert oasis of Tema in Arabia and left his eldest son Belshazzar ruling in Babylon. The night of this chapter is in October 539 BC. The army of Cyrus the Persian has already beaten the Babylonians in the field and is outside the city. Daniel is now about eighty and seems to have dropped out of the royal circle. The temple vessels taken from Jerusalem in 605 BC (1:2) are still in Babylon's treasury. Chapter 4 showed a proud king humbled and restored. Chapter 5 shows a proud king who learned nothing from that and was not given a second chance.

#### The Handwriting on the Wall (vv1–12)
- **"King Belshazzar" (v1).** For a long time critics said Belshazzar never existed, because Greek historians name Nabonidus as Babylon's last king and say nothing of him. Then in the nineteenth century cuneiform texts turned up naming Belshazzar as Nabonidus's eldest son, to whom his father "entrusted the kingship" while he lived at Tema. His name means "Bel, protect the king." That explains why he can offer only the rank of **"third ruler"** (v7): his father was first and he was second.
- **"A great feast for a thousand of his lords" (v1).** A banquet of this size was a show of confidence. Babylon's walls were famous and its stores could last years. Two Greek historians, Herodotus and Xenophon, both say the city fell while a festival was going on. A party with an enemy army at the gates was either bravado or denial.
- **"Nebuchadnezzar his father" (v2).** Aramaic had no word for "grandfather" or "ancestor," so "father" can mean forefather or royal predecessor (compare "Jesus Christ, the son of David," Matt 1:1). Belshazzar may have been Nebuchadnezzar's grandson through his mother, or "father" may simply mean the founder of his royal line. Either way, the text is not making a mistake about family trees.
- **The temple vessels (vv2–3).** These were the gold and silver cups and bowls from the house of God in Jerusalem (1:2; 2 Kgs 25:14–15). Nebuchadnezzar had put them in his god's treasury as trophies, but there is no record that anyone drank from them. Belshazzar does it "when he tasted the wine," which is a polite way of saying once he was drunk. Verse 3 repeats the description in full, "the house of God in Jerusalem," so the reader feels the offence.
- **"Praised the gods of gold and silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone" (v4).** The list of materials echoes the statue of chapter 2 (gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay). He drinks to idols from vessels made for the living God. This is no longer careless wickedness. It is deliberate mockery of the God who had humbled Nebuchadnezzar.
- **"Immediately the fingers of a human hand appeared" (v5).** The writing came at once, as if in answer. The hand wrote "on the plaster of the wall… opposite the lampstand," where the light fell most clearly. Archaeologists working in Babylon found a large throne room with white-plastered walls, which fits the scene, though no one can say it is the very room.
- **"His limbs gave way, and his knees knocked together" (v6).** The Aramaic says literally "the knots of his loins were loosed," which probably means his legs gave way, or possibly that he lost control of his bladder. The man who wanted to look fearless is reduced to shaking before he even knows what the words say. His own conscience seems to be reading the message before anyone else.
- **Purple, a gold chain and "third ruler" (v7).** Purple was the colour of royalty, and a gold chain was a mark of high office (compare Joseph in Gen 41:42). Belshazzar offers everything he can, and none of it helps.
- **"They could not read the writing" (v8).** This is the third time Babylon's wise men fail (2:10–11; 4:7). The words were Aramaic, the court language, so why could they not read them? It may have been written without vowels as a string of letters with no word breaks, or in a way only God would let someone understand. The text does not say, and both suggestions are guesses.
- **"The queen… came into the banqueting hall" (v10).** She was not at the feast, since the king's wives were already there (v2). She is almost certainly the queen mother, possibly Nabonidus's wife and, some think, a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar. She speaks with the confidence of an older woman who remembers better days. **"O king, live forever!"** was the standard greeting, and it carries a grim irony here, since he will not live through the night (v30).
- **"There is a man in your kingdom" (vv11–12).** She remembers what Belshazzar had forgotten or never cared to know. Her description repeats Nebuchadnezzar's own words about Daniel (4:8–9, 18): "the spirit of the holy gods," light, understanding, wisdom. **"Solve problems"** is literally "untie knots," a phrase used of solving riddles and sometimes of undoing spells. The same Aramaic word for "knots" was used in v6 of the king's loosened limbs, a wordplay many commentators notice. Daniel is the man who can untie what has undone the king.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the feast a moment when Belshazzar "filled up the measure of his iniquity," and says that using the temple vessels "bade defiance to his sanctuary." JFB calls the party a "heaven-sent infatuation" with the city under siege, and reads "father" as "forefather," likely a grandson. Guzik pictures Belshazzar trusting his walls while Cyrus waited outside. He thinks the king's terror before he knew the meaning shows a conscience already accusing him, and suggests Daniel had been out of public office for a time. All three, with the Tyndale notes, take the queen to be the queen mother. JFB names her Nitocris, following Herodotus, which is possible but not certain.

#### Daniel Interprets the Handwriting (vv13–31)
- **"You are that Daniel, one of the exiles of Judah" (v13).** Belshazzar knows who Daniel is but introduces him by his lowest title: a captive. The repeated "I have heard" (vv14, 16) suggests he had never bothered to meet him.
- **"Let your gifts be for yourself" (v17).** Daniel had accepted honours from Nebuchadnezzar (2:48). Here he refuses them, though they are pressed on him anyway (v29). He will not let anyone think God's message was bought, and he knows the gifts are worth nothing by morning. Compare Abram refusing the king of Sodom's goods (Gen 14:22–23) and Elisha refusing Naaman's gifts (2 Kgs 5:16).
- **No respectful opening.** With Nebuchadnezzar, Daniel was distressed and wished the dream were for the king's enemies (4:19). With Belshazzar there is no "live forever" and no softening. A prophet's tone changes with the hearer. One king was proud but teachable. This one is mocking God at a drunken party.
- **The lesson of Nebuchadnezzar (vv18–21).** Daniel first retells chapter 4. God gave Nebuchadnezzar his greatness: "whom he would, he killed, and whom he would, he kept alive" (v19). When his heart "was lifted up," he was driven out to live like a beast "until he knew that the Most High God rules" (v21). This is the sentence the whole book keeps coming back to (4:17, 25, 32).
- **"Though you knew all this" (v22).** This is the heart of the charge. Belshazzar's sin was not ignorance. He knew what happened to his predecessor and did the opposite. The more light a person has, the more serious the refusal (Luke 12:47–48).
- **"Gods… which do not see or hear or know" (v23).** Daniel uses the language of the Psalms and prophets against idols (Ps 115:4–8; Isa 44:9–20). Then comes the contrast: **"the God in whose hand is your breath, and whose are all your ways, you have not honored."** Belshazzar praised lifeless gods and ignored the God who was keeping him alive at that moment. That God was about to withdraw his breath.
- **"MENE, MENE, TEKEL, and PARSIN" (v25).** These are Aramaic words, and on their face they are units of weight or money. A *mene* is a mina, about 60 shekels. *Tekel* is the Aramaic form of "shekel." *Parsin* is the plural (or dual) of *peres*, which the BDB lexicon gives as "half-mina," with the *u* in the KJV's "UPHARSIN" meaning "and." Read as nouns, they make a string of coin names, a riddle. Read as verbs, they say something else, and Daniel reads them as verbs.
- **MENE: "numbered" (v26).** From the verb *mena*, "to count." "God has numbered the days of your kingdom and brought it to an end." The word is written twice, which may stress certainty (compare Pharaoh's doubled dream, Gen 41:32).
- **TEKEL: "weighed" (v27).** From *teqal*, "to weigh." "You have been weighed in the balances and found wanting." Scripture often pictures God weighing people and deeds (1 Sam 2:3; Job 31:6; Prov 16:2). Egyptian art showed the dead person's heart weighed on a balance, and the picture would be familiar in the ancient world. Belshazzar is too light. There is nothing of substance on his side of the scale.
- **PERES: "divided" (v28).** From *peras*, "to divide," with a pun on *Paras*, "Persia." The kingdom "is divided and given to the Medes and Persians." Daniel does not say the kingdom will be split in half. It is handed over, broken off from Belshazzar and given to a joint Medo-Persian power, the silver chest and arms of the statue (2:32, 39).
- **"Daniel was clothed with purple" (v29).** Belshazzar keeps his word but does not repent. There is no prayer and no confession, only a quick ceremony. Daniel holds his office for a few hours at most, a sharp picture of how short-lived worldly honour is.
- **"That very night Belshazzar the Chaldean king was killed" (v30).** The Babylonian Chronicle, a cuneiform record of the period, says Cyrus's army under the general Ugbaru (or Gubaru) entered Babylon "without battle" in October 539 BC. Herodotus says the Persians diverted the Euphrates and walked into the city along the riverbed while the Babylonians were celebrating. Details differ between the ancient sources, but all agree the city fell quickly and with little fighting. Isaiah had named Cyrus as the one who would open Babylon's gates (Isa 44:28–45:2), and Jeremiah foresaw the city's leaders made drunk and sleeping "a perpetual sleep" (Jer 51:39, 57).
- **"Darius the Mede received the kingdom" (v31).** No other ancient source names a "Darius the Mede," and this is one of the hard questions in Daniel. The main views: he was Gubaru, a governor Cyrus put over Babylon; he was Cyaxares II, a Median king mentioned by Xenophon; or he was Cyrus himself under a second title (6:28 can be read "the reign of Darius, *even* the reign of Cyrus"). Each view has supporters among careful scholars. The text's main point is plain: the kingdom passed that night, just as the writing said. In the Aramaic text this verse is 6:1.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Daniel "is not one of those that divine for money," and quotes Daniel's charge against the king, "thou hast not humbled thy heart, though thou knewest all this." He also turns the three words into a warning for every reader, applying them to death, judgment and the life after. JFB reads MENE as God having "fixed the number of years of thine empire," reads TEKEL as "too light before God," and identifies Darius as Cyaxares II, with everything "done in the name of Darius" though Cyrus led the attack. Guzik prefers Gubaru, and the Tyndale notes lean towards Darius being Cyrus, so the commentators here represent three of the main views. Guzik's main point is accountability for what one already knows: Belshazzar had more light than Nebuchadnezzar and fell further. He also argues that Babylon fell morally before it fell militarily.

#### Pointing to Christ
Belshazzar lifted the vessels of God's house to praise dead gods and was weighed and found wanting. No one placed on God's balance by themselves comes out heavy enough (Rom 3:23). The gospel does not tip the scale by adding weight to the sinner's side. It puts Christ's own righteousness there (2 Cor 5:21; Phil 3:9). And the God "in whose hand is your breath" took on flesh and breath himself. At another meal, on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took a cup and gave it to his disciples, not as a mockery of holy things but as the cup of the new covenant in his blood (Luke 22:20).
