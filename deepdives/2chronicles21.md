*Setting:* Judah, about 848–841 BC. Jehoshaphat, one of the best kings of Judah, has died after a reign that mixed real devotion to God with one ruinous habit: alliances with Ahab's house in the north. He fought beside Ahab at Ramoth-gilead (ch 18), built ships with Ahab's son (20:35–37), and, worst of all, married his heir Jehoram to Athaliah, the daughter of Ahab and Jezebel (18:1; 21:6; 22:2). Chapter 21 shows the harvest of that marriage. Jehoram is the first king of David's line whom the Chronicler judges entirely bad, with not one good word. The parallel account in 2 Kings 8:16–24 is short. Chronicles adds the murder of his brothers, Elijah's letter, the raid that carried off his family and the details of his death. Behind it all stands one question: will God's promise to David survive a king like this?

#### Jehoram Reigns in Judah (vv1–20)
- **"Jehoshaphat slept with his fathers… and Jehoram his son reigned in his place" (v1).** "Slept with his fathers" is the Bible's gentle way of saying a king died a natural death and joined his ancestors. The Tyndale notes date Jehoram's sole reign 848–841 BC, after about three years as co-regent with his father. Kings regularly crowned an heir while still alive to secure the succession, and that overlap explains why the dates in Kings sometimes seem to collide.
- **Six brothers, "sons of Jehoshaphat king of Israel" (v2).** Two brothers are both called Azariah in the Hebrew. The names are almost all built on God's name: Azariah ("the LORD has helped"), Jehiel ("God lives"), Zechariah ("the LORD remembers"), Michael ("who is like God?"), Shephatiah ("the LORD has judged"). These were sons raised in a godly house. Calling a king of Judah "king of Israel" is not a slip. The Chronicler often uses "Israel" for the true people of God, and in his eyes Judah is what remains of all Israel (compare 12:6; 28:19).
- **"Great gifts… together with fortified cities" (v3).** Jehoshaphat followed his grandfather Rehoboam's wise practice of giving younger sons wealth and posts in the provincial strongholds (11:23). It gave them honor and work, and it kept them away from the capital and from palace intrigue. The kingdom itself went to Jehoram "because he was the firstborn."
- **"When Jehoram had… established" himself, "he killed all his brothers" (v4).** "Established" here means he had made his grip firm. As soon as he felt secure he removed every possible rival, along with "some of the princes of Israel," probably leaders loyal to his father. Murdering a dead king's other sons was common in the ancient Near East, but it had never happened in David's line before. It was also an attack on the dynasty itself: the fewer heirs, the closer David's house came to dying out. Josephus, writing much later, blames Athaliah's influence. Scripture does not say that, but the next verse points in the same direction.
- **"For the daughter of Ahab was his wife" (v6).** This is the Chronicler's explanation in one clause. Jehoram "walked in the way of the kings of Israel," meaning the northern kings, with their golden calves and, under Ahab, open Baal worship. The pattern of Ahab's household repeats in Jerusalem: a weak king, a strong queen, a foreign god and royal murder (compare Naboth in 1 Kings 21). Within a few years Athaliah will try to wipe out the entire royal family herself (22:10).
- **"Yet the LORD was not willing to destroy the house of David" (v7).** This is the key verse. By every human measure the house of David deserved to end here. God spared it "because of the covenant that he had made with David" (2 Sam 7:12–16). The Chronicler sharpens the wording of 2 Kings 8:19. Kings says God would not destroy *Judah* for David's sake. Chronicles says he would not destroy *the house of David* "because of the covenant." The promise is the reason, not anything in Jehoram.
- **"To give a lamp to him and to his sons forever" (v7).** The Hebrew *nir*, "lamp," is a picture used only of David's line (1 Kings 11:36; 15:4; 2 Kings 8:19). A lamp kept burning in a house meant someone still lived there. A dark house was an empty one. As long as a son of David sat on the throne, the light was still on. The ESV's "lamp" is right, and the lexicon explains it exactly this way: "that his family may remain on the throne."
- **"In his days Edom revolted… and set up a king of their own" (v8).** Edom, the land southeast of the Dead Sea, had been under Judah's control since David (2 Sam 8:14), with breaks. Under Jehoshaphat it was ruled by a deputy rather than a king (1 Kings 22:47). Now it breaks free for good. Isaac had told Esau, Edom's ancestor, that one day "you shall break his yoke from your neck" (Gen 27:40). Losing Edom also meant losing the road to the Red Sea port at Ezion-geber and the trade with Arabia that flowed through it.
- **"He rose by night and struck the Edomites who had surrounded him" (v9).** The fuller account in 2 Kings 8:21 says that "his army fled home." Jehoram was surrounded, broke out at night and escaped, but he could not win the war. The words "to this day" (v10) mean Edom was still independent when the Chronicler's source was written. Amaziah later wins a battle against Edom (25:11–12), but Judah never fully regains it.
- **"Libnah also revolted… because he had forsaken the LORD" (v10).** Libnah was a town in the lowlands west of Judah near the Philistine border, and one of the cities given to the priests (Josh 21:13). Its revolt shows the kingdom fraying from the inside, on the west as well as the southeast. Here, as everywhere in Chronicles, the writer states the cause plainly: political collapse follows spiritual collapse. The phrase "because he had forsaken the LORD" is the Chronicler's own explanation and is not in Kings.
- **"He made high places… and led the inhabitants of Jerusalem into whoredom" (v11).** High places were hilltop shrines, often for idols, and in any case outside the one place God had chosen for worship. Asa and Jehoshaphat had worked to remove them (14:3; 17:6). Jehoram builds new ones. "Whoredom" is the prophets' word for idolatry: Israel was bound to the LORD like a wife, so turning to other gods was unfaithfulness in marriage (Hos 1–3; Jer 3). The verbs show a king actively drawing his people away, not merely tolerating sin.
- **"A letter came to him from Elijah the prophet" (v12).** This is Elijah's only appearance in Chronicles, and the only letter from him anywhere. It raises a real question. 2 Kings 3 shows Elisha already serving as the leading prophet during Jehoshaphat's lifetime, which seems to mean Elijah had been taken up to heaven before Jehoram's reign. There are three main explanations. (1) Elijah was taken up later than the order of 2 Kings suggests. 2 Kings 1:17 dates the start of the northern Jehoram's reign to "the second year of Jehoram the son of Jehoshaphat," while Elijah was clearly still active, so Jehoram of Judah was already reigning as co-regent in Elijah's lifetime. Kings does not always tell its stories in time order. (2) Elijah wrote the letter before he was taken, foreseeing what Jehoram would do, and it was delivered later. (3) The name was changed in copying from Elisha, which is JFB's view, though no manuscript supports it. The first explanation is simple and needs no change to the text, and many scholars hold it.
- **"The God of David your father" (v12).** Elijah's opening is pointed. He measures Jehoram not against the kings of the north but against David, and then against "Jehoshaphat your father" and "Asa king of Judah," his father and grandfather. Jehoram has two godly generations right behind him and has turned his back on both.
- **"Your brothers… who were better than you" (v13).** The letter names two sins: idolatry, modeled on Ahab, and murder. In Chronicles the seriousness of a sin is often measured by the goodness of what it destroys.
- **"A great plague on your people, your children, your wives… and you yourself… a disease of your bowels" (vv14–15).** The judgment is fitted to the sin. A man who destroyed his father's household will lose his own. A man who struck others will be struck from the inside. "Plague" here means a heavy blow, not necessarily a disease. It is fulfilled in vv16–17 by an invasion.
- **"The Philistines and… the Arabians who are near the Ethiopians" (v16).** "The LORD stirred up" their anger. God is in charge of the nations even when they act from their own motives (compare 1 Chr 5:26; Isa 10:5–7). The Philistines lived on the coastal plain to the west. "Arabians" were desert tribes to the south. The ESV's "Ethiopians" translates *Cushites*. This is probably not distant Ethiopia but a people living in the south near Arabia or the Negev, as perhaps with Zerah's army in 14:9. Under Jehoshaphat, Philistines and Arabians had brought him tribute (17:11). Now they come as raiders.
- **"Carried away all the possessions… and also his sons and his wives" (v17).** The raiders reached the royal household, which probably means they plundered Jerusalem itself or the king's estates. 22:1 adds that they killed the older sons. Some scholars connect this raid with Joel 3:4–6 and Amos 1:6, where Philistines sell captives from Judah as slaves, and Obadiah 11–14 has also been read against this time, when foreigners "entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem." Those links are possible but not certain.
- **"No son was left to him except Jehoahaz, his youngest son" (v17).** Jehoahaz is the same man called Ahaziah in 22:1. The two names use the same two parts in reverse order: *Jeho-ahaz* and *Ahaz-iah*, both meaning "the LORD has grasped." Here is the irony of the chapter. Jehoram killed his brothers to make his line secure, and ended with one son. Yet that one son is the lamp of v7. God keeps the light of David's house burning by the thinnest of threads.
- **"After all this the LORD struck him in his bowels with an incurable disease" (v18).** Jehoram endured the loss of his family and still did not repent, so the second half of the prophecy fell. What the disease was is not known. Suggestions range from severe dysentery to cancer of the bowel. The text is concerned with the meaning, not the diagnosis.
- **"At the end of two years… he died in great agony" (v19).** The Hebrew reads literally "at the time of the going out of the end, of days two." "Days" can stand for years in Hebrew, and most translations read "two years." Some scholars read "after two days" for the final crisis. Either way the suffering was long and the end terrible, exactly as Elijah's letter had said: "day by day" (v15).
- **"His people made no fire in his honor" (v19).** This is not cremation. Israel buried its dead. A royal funeral included a great burning of spices and perfumes in the king's honor, as for Asa (16:14) and as Jeremiah promised Zedekiah (Jer 34:5). For Jehoram, nothing.
- **"He departed with no one's regret" (v20).** The Hebrew is literally "he went without desire." The lexicon explains it as "he lived as no one desired." No one wanted him to stay, and no one missed him when he went. He was buried in the city of David, "but not in the tombs of the kings." Even in death Jehoram is placed outside the honored line he had tried so hard to secure by murder. Jehoshaphat's life closed with "he walked in the way of Asa his father" (20:32). Jehoram's closes with a closed tomb and no mourners.
- **The Chronicler's pattern.** In Chronicles, faithfulness brings rest, building and victory, and unfaithfulness brings revolt, invasion and disease. That is not a promise that every illness is a punishment (Jesus rejects that reading in John 9:1–3). It is the writer's way of showing that kings are accountable to God. For the first readers, back from exile in Babylon, the message was urgent: the same God who judged Jehoram also kept his promise to David, and he was still keeping it for them.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says Jehoshaphat "portioned his brethren and disposed of them so as that they would be easy and give him no disturbance," and suggests Jehoram hated his brothers because their goodness shamed him: they were "better than" he (v13). On v7, Henry writes that "God was tender of his covenant with the house of David." Guzik calls the Ahab marriage one of Jehoshaphat's worst decisions, and he notes that the man who began by killing his brothers ended by losing his own sons and wives. On Elijah's letter, Guzik lays out the possibilities without settling on one. Henry supposes Elijah's "departure was at hand, so that he could not go in person," and so wrote. JFB thinks a copyist put "Elijah" in place of "Elisha," which is the weakest of the options since no manuscript has it. On the funeral, JFB explains that Hebrews gave kings funeral honors "according to the good or bad characters of their reign," and Henry sums up the whole life in one line: he "departed without being desired." The Tyndale notes add that violence against David's family nearly ended the dynasty three more times in the next few years (21:17; 22:8–9; 22:10–11).

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is dark, but its brightest line is v7: God would not let David's lamp go out, "because of the covenant." A murderer sat on David's throne, his heirs were killed, and only one son was left, yet the promise held. Nine centuries later the lamp was still burning, in a carpenter's family in Nazareth, when the angel told Mary that God would give her son "the throne of his father David" (Luke 1:32). Jehoram tried to make his own line safe by killing his brothers, and lost it. Jesus, the true Son of David, secured his line by laying down his life for his brothers, and he "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Heb 2:11).
