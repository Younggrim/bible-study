*Setting:* Isaiah 12 is the shortest chapter in the opening part of the book and its finale. Chapters 7–12 began with King Ahaz refusing to trust God when Syria and Israel threatened Jerusalem (about 735 BC), and they have moved through judgment by Assyria to the promise of a child, a King from the stump of Jesse, and a second exodus in which God gathers his scattered people home (11:10–16). Chapter 11 ended with a highway out of Assyria "as there was for Israel when they came up from the land of Egypt" (11:16). After the first exodus, Moses and Miriam sang by the Red Sea (Exod 15). So after this greater exodus, God's people sing again. Chapter 12 is that song, a short psalm of praise that closes chapters 1–12 before the oracles against the nations begin in chapter 13.

#### The LORD Is My Strength and My Song (vv1–6)
- **The shape of the song.** Twice Isaiah says, "You will say in that day" (vv1, 4). The first time "you" is singular in Hebrew: one rescued person gives thanks (vv1–3). The second time "you" is plural: the whole people call each other to tell the world (vv4–6). Praise starts in one heart and spreads to the nations.
- **"In that day" (v1).** This phrase ties the song to the day described in 11:10–11, when the Root of Jesse stands as a signal to the peoples and the Lord gathers the remnant "a second time." This is the song of people who have actually been brought home.
- **"Though you were angry with me, your anger turned away" (v1).** The whole of chapters 1–11 has been about God's righteous anger at a sinful nation (5:25; 9:12, 17, 21; 10:4: "for all this his anger has not turned away"). That refrain is now answered. The same verb that kept saying anger had *not* turned away now says it *has*. God does not pretend sin did not matter. His anger was real, and it ends because he chooses mercy.
- **"That you might comfort me" (v1).** "Comfort" (*nacham*) is the word that opens the second half of the book: "Comfort, comfort my people, says your God" (40:1). The Tyndale notes point out that God's comfort becomes the main subject of chapters 40–66. This little song is a preview of that whole message.
- **"Behold, God is my salvation" (v2).** "Salvation" is *yeshu'ah*, rescue or deliverance, and in songs like this one it carries the sense of victory won by God for his people. The word appears three times in two verses (vv2–3). Isaiah's own name, *Yesha'yahu*, means "the LORD is salvation," and the name Jesus (*Yeshua*) comes from the same root. Notice that God is not just the one who *gives* salvation. He *is* the salvation.
- **"I will trust, and will not be afraid" (v2).** This is the answer to chapter 7. There Ahaz and the people shook "as the trees of the forest shake before the wind" (7:2), and Isaiah told them, "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (7:9). The rescued remnant now does what Ahaz would not. Trust drives out fear (compare 8:12–13).
- **"The LORD GOD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation" (v2).** This line is quoted almost word for word from the Song of the Sea (Exod 15:2), and it appears again in Psalm 118:14. Isaiah deliberately reuses Israel's first great salvation song for the greater rescue to come. The Hebrew name here is doubled, *Yah Yahweh*. *Yah* is a short form of God's personal name (as in "Hallelu-*jah*," "praise Yah"). The Tyndale notes explain it simply as an abbreviation followed by the full name. "My song" means God is not only the one who gives a reason to sing; he is himself the theme of the song.
- **"With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation" (v3).** In a dry land, a well meant life. Drawing water was hard daily work, usually done by women carrying jars. Here it becomes joyful. The picture is of a supply that does not run out (compare 41:18; 55:1, "Come, everyone who thirsts, come to the waters").
- **Water and the Feast of Booths.** By Jesus' day, this verse was tied to a ceremony at the Feast of Booths (Tabernacles). Each morning of the feast, a priest drew water from the Pool of Siloam in a golden pitcher and poured it out at the altar while the people sang. The Mishnah, a Jewish law collection from around AD 200, says that whoever had not seen the joy of that water-drawing had never seen joy in his life, and the Talmud links the rite to Isaiah 12:3. It was on "the last day of the feast, the great day," that Jesus stood and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37–39). He was claiming to be the well.
- **"Give thanks to the LORD, call upon his name" (v4).** These phrases closely match the psalm David's singers used when the ark came to Jerusalem (1 Chr 16:8; Ps 105:1). "Call upon his name" means to pray and worship, relying on who God has revealed himself to be (Gen 4:26; 12:8).
- **"Make known his deeds among the peoples" (v4).** The song does not stay inside Israel. Chapter 11 said the Root of Jesse would be a signal "of the peoples," and "of him shall the nations inquire" (11:10). Here the rescued are the ones who tell them. This is mission language centuries before Matthew 28.
- **"Proclaim that his name is exalted" (v4).** "Exalted" (*nisgab*) means set high, out of reach. The same word appears in 2:11, 17: "the LORD alone will be exalted in that day." Every proud thing has been brought low, and only God's name stands high.
- **"Sing praises to the LORD, for he has done gloriously" (v5).** This echoes Miriam's refrain at the Red Sea: "Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously" (Exod 15:21). The exodus language runs through the whole chapter.
- **"Shout, and sing for joy, O inhabitant of Zion" (v6).** "Inhabitant" is feminine in Hebrew, picturing Zion as a woman, much like "daughter of Zion" (1:8; 10:32). Earlier the daughter of Zion was "left like a booth in a vineyard" (1:8). Now she shouts for joy. Zephaniah, writing about a century later, uses very similar words: "Sing aloud, O daughter of Zion… The LORD, your God, is in your midst" (Zeph 3:14–17).
- **"For great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel" (v6).** "The Holy One of Israel" is Isaiah's signature title for God, used about 25 times in the book and only a handful of times in the rest of the Old Testament. Isaiah heard the seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy" (6:3), and that vision shapes everything he writes. In chapter 6 the holy God's presence made Isaiah cry, "Woe is me!" (6:5). Now the same Holy One lives in the middle of his people, and the response is joy, not terror. What makes the difference is the cleansing he provides (6:7). This is the "Immanuel," "God with us," promised in 7:14.
- **Commentators:** All three read the chapter as a new Song of Moses. JFB calls it the thanksgiving hymn of restored Israel, "just as Miriam, after the deliverance of the Red Sea… celebrated it with an ode of praise," and Henry likewise sets it beside Exodus 15. Henry and Guzik both see v1 as describing God's fatherly discipline of his own children rather than final wrath. Henry: "Even God's frowns must not put us out of tune for praising him," for his anger "endures but for a moment." Guzik adds that, for believers, the full weight of God's wrath was spent on Christ at the cross. On v2 Henry draws out the wording: God is "not only my Saviour, by whom I am saved, but my salvation, in whom I am safe." On v3, JFB describes the Siloam water rite and its link to John 7, and notes that wells are "not mere streams, which may run dry, but ever-flowing fountains." Guzik ties the wells instead to Jesus' words to the Samaritan woman about living water (John 4:14), and stresses that there are many wells but one source. Henry sees the two halves of the song as private and public praise: thanksgiving is first "closet-work," then shared in the congregation.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah 12 is the song of people who have been through God's anger and come out on the other side, and the New Testament shows how that happens. The anger turned away because it fell on the Son (53:5–6). The one who "has become my salvation" bears the name *Yeshua*, "the LORD saves." He stood in the temple at the water feast and offered himself as the well that never runs dry. And the Holy One who lives in the middle of his people took on flesh and "dwelt among us" (John 1:14). The response the song calls for is the one the church still gives: thanks, trust without fear, and telling the nations what he has done.
