*Setting:* Chapter 6 ended in 515 BC with the second temple finished and the Passover kept with joy. Chapter 7 opens with "Now after this," but almost sixty years pass in those three words. The events of Esther fall in that gap, under King Xerxes. His son Artaxerxes I (reigned 465–424 BC) now sits on the Persian throne, and in his seventh year, 458 BC, a priest and scholar named Ezra leads a second wave of exiles from Babylon to Jerusalem. From here to the end of the book, Ezra tells his own story, and in places he writes in the first person (7:27–9:15). The temple stands. What the community lacks is a people shaped by God's word, and that is what Ezra comes to give. The king's letter (vv12–26) is in Aramaic, the official language of the Persian empire, like the documents in chapters 4–6.

#### Ezra Sent to Teach the People (vv1–28)
- **"In the reign of Artaxerxes king of Persia" (v1).** Most scholars take this as Artaxerxes I, called *Longimanus*, "long-handed," by Roman writers, which puts Ezra's arrival in 458 BC and Nehemiah's in 445 BC under the same king. A minority view dates Ezra to the seventh year of Artaxerxes II (398 BC), after Nehemiah. The traditional order fits the text best: Nehemiah 8 and 12:26, 36 place Ezra and Nehemiah in Jerusalem together.
- **"Ezra the son of Seraiah" (v1).** Seraiah was the high priest Nebuchadnezzar executed at Riblah in 586 BC (2 Kgs 25:18–21), more than 120 years earlier. So "son" here means descendant, as often in Hebrew genealogies. The name Ezra is a short form of Azariah, "the LORD helps," fitting for a man who keeps saying God's hand was on him.
- **Back to "Aaron the chief priest" (vv1–5).** The list runs through Zadok, Solomon's priest (1 Kgs 2:35), Phinehas, whose zeal stopped a plague (Num 25:7–13), and Eleazar to Aaron. It is shortened. Comparing 1 Chronicles 6:3–14 shows six generations skipped between Meraioth and Azariah. The point is not a complete record but credentials: Ezra stands in the true priestly line. In a book where some priests could not prove their descent (2:61–63), that matters.
- **"A scribe skilled in the Law of Moses" (v6).** A scribe (Hebrew *sofer*) could be a royal secretary, and some scholars think Ezra held a post like "secretary for Jewish affairs" at the Persian court. But the text puts the weight on his learning in the Law. "Skilled" is *mahir*, "quick, ready, skilled." Psalm 45:1 uses it of "the pen of a ready scribe," and Proverbs 22:29 of a man "skillful in his work" who will stand before kings. Ezra moved easily and surely through the Scriptures, and he did stand before a king.
- **"That the LORD, the God of Israel, had given" (v6).** Ezra treats the Law of Moses as something received from God, not something he compiled or edited. Jewish tradition remembers him as a second Moses, the man who more than anyone made Israel a people of the book.
- **"The king granted him all that he asked, for the hand of the LORD his God was on him" (v6).** Here is the chapter's key phrase. It comes again in v9 ("the good hand of his God") and v28, and four more times in chapter 8 (8:18, 22, 31). Ezra had real access at court. He knew who opened the door.
- **"Some of the people of Israel, and some of the priests and Levites" (v7).** "Some" is the honest word. Most Jews in Babylon had settled down and stayed. Chapter 8 will count the group, and it will show how hard it was to recruit Levites. The list of groups (priests, Levites, singers, gatekeepers, temple servants) repeats 2:70. This return, like the first one, is organized around worship.
- **"On the first day of the first month… on the first day of the fifth month" (vv8–9).** They set out at the Jewish new year (Nisan, March–April) and arrived in Ab (July–August), four months on the road. Babylon to Jerusalem is about 500 miles in a straight line, but the desert forced travelers up the Euphrates and down through Syria, nearer 900 miles. With families, livestock and treasure, that meant slow, short stages. Chapter 8 tells the full story, including an eleven-day delay to find Levites (8:15, 31).
- **"For Ezra had set his heart to study the Law of the LORD, and to do it and to teach" (v10).** The "for" explains *why* God's hand was on him. "Set his heart" is the language of preparation, fixing the whole inner person on something (2 Chr 19:3; 30:19). "Study" is *darash*, "to seek, inquire," the verb used for seeking God himself. The order is the lesson: first study, then do, then teach. He did not teach what he had not first obeyed. James makes the same demand: "be doers of the word, and not hearers only" (Jas 1:22).
- **"A copy of the letter" (v11).** The narrator introduces the decree and gives Ezra two titles: priest and scribe. The scribal role is spelled out twice over, "learned in matters of the commandments of the LORD and his statutes." The Persians may have thought of him as an official. The narrator wants the reader to see a teacher.
- **"Artaxerxes, king of kings" (v12).** The title was real Persian royal style. Darius and Xerxes use it in their inscriptions, and Babylonian and Assyrian kings used it before them (compare Ezek 26:7; Dan 2:37). The Bible later gives the title its proper owner (Rev 19:16). The Aramaic word after "Peace" is uncertain. It may mean "perfect" or "complete," a greeting formula, or it may simply mark the end of the address. The ESV renders it "Peace. And now."
- **"Who freely offers to go" (v13).** No one is forced. Like Cyrus's decree (1:3), this one opens the road but does not drive people down it. The word for willing offering runs through the letter: the king "freely offered" (v15), the people's "freewill offerings… vowed willingly" (v16).
- **"Sent by the king and his seven counselors to make inquiries" (v14).** Esther 1:14 names "the seven princes of Persia and Media" who saw the king's face, and the Greek historian Xenophon mentions this inner council too. Ezra goes as an official inspector, "according to the Law of your God, which is in your hand." That is probably literal. He carried a scroll of the Law, and it was to be the standard for his inquiry. The Tyndale notes point out that the letter's word for *law* is the Aramaic *dat*, a Persian word for royal law, a natural choice for a Persian writer.
- **"The God of Israel, whose dwelling is in Jerusalem" (v15).** To Artaxerxes, the God of Israel was the god of one region who lived in one house. Solomon knew better: "heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you" (1 Kgs 8:27). God used the king's limited theology anyway.
- **"All the silver and gold that you shall find in the whole province of Babylonia" (v16).** Ezra could collect gifts from Babylonian Jews and perhaps their neighbors, echoing the first return, when neighbors gave silver and gold (1:4, 6) and the exodus, when Israel left Egypt with Egyptian silver (Exod 12:35–36). The amounts are listed in 8:26–27.
- **"With all diligence buy bulls, rams, and lambs" (v17).** The first use of the money is sacrifice: burnt offerings with their grain and drink offerings, as the Law prescribed (Num 15:1–10). The rest is left to Ezra's judgment, "according to the will of your God" (v18). The king trusts Ezra with money and freedom.
- **"The vessels… deliver before the God of Jerusalem" (v19).** These may be temple vessels missed when Cyrus returned the plunder (1:7–11), or new gifts. Ezra 8:25–27 suggests new ones, including bronze "as precious as gold."
- **"Out of the king's treasury" (v20).** Beyond the gifts, Ezra could charge further temple costs to the royal accounts. This was Persian policy toward temples across the empire, now applied to Jerusalem.
- **"Up to 100 talents of silver" (vv21–22).** The order goes to the treasurers of the province "Beyond the River," the lands west of the Euphrates, including Judah. A talent was about 75 pounds, so 100 talents was roughly three and three-quarter tons of silver. Pharaoh Neco once fined the whole kingdom of Judah 100 talents of silver (2 Kgs 23:33). A cor was a dry measure of perhaps 6 bushels, a bath a liquid measure of about 6 gallons. The wheat, wine and oil were for offerings, and salt, which seasoned every offering (Lev 2:13), had no limit at all.
- **"Lest his wrath be against the realm of the king and his sons" (v23).** Here is the king's motive in plain words. Persian rulers wanted every god in their empire on their side. Egypt had revolted around 460 BC, and Persian armies were marching through Judah's region to put it down, so the king had reason to want both divine favor and a loyal province. God used a ruler's mixed motives to serve his own purpose.
- **"Tribute, custom, or toll" (v24).** Temple personnel were exempt from all three kinds of tax. Persian kings granted similar exemptions elsewhere. Darius I, in a letter preserved in a Greek inscription, rebuked a governor for taxing the sacred gardeners of Apollo's shrine. The exemption made temple service sustainable for the Levites and others.
- **"Appoint magistrates and judges… And those who do not know them, you shall teach" (v25).** Ezra's authority extends to the Jews of the whole province, "all such as know the laws of your God." This is the heart of his mission. He is to set up courts that judge by God's law and to teach those who do not know it. The king's letter ends where v10 ended, with teaching.
- **"Whether for death or for banishment or for confiscation of his goods or for imprisonment" (v26).** These were real civil penalties, backed by Persian power. "The law of your God and the law of the king" stand side by side. "Banishment" is literally "rooting out," perhaps exclusion from the community. Chapter 10 shows one such penalty being applied: forfeit of property and expulsion from the assembly (10:8). There is no record that Ezra ever used the death penalty.
- **The letter switches back to Hebrew (v27).** The Aramaic decree ends, and Ezra's own voice begins in Hebrew. For the first time the book says "me" and "I."
- **"Who put such a thing as this into the heart of the king" (v27).** Ezra does not thank the king. He thanks God, who moved the king, as God moved Cyrus (1:1) and "turned the heart of the king of Assyria" in 6:22. "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will" (Prov 21:1).
- **"To beautify the house of the LORD" (v27).** The verb is *pa'ar*, "to beautify, glorify." Isaiah uses it of the same temple: "I will beautify my beautiful house" (Isa 60:7), and nations bringing their wealth "to beautify the place of my sanctuary" (Isa 60:13). Ezra may well have heard the echo. A Persian king's gold looked like the first small fulfilment of that promise.
- **"Extended to me his steadfast love" (v28).** "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's covenant loyalty. Ezra saw the king's favor as an expression of God's faithfulness to his people.
- **"I took courage, for the hand of the LORD my God was on me" (v28).** Ezra was a scholar, not a soldier, and the task was daunting. The courage came from knowing God was with him. He then "gathered leading men from Israel" (v28), which leads straight into the list of chapter 8.
- **Commentators:** Guzik and JFB both insist that *scribe* means far more than a copyist. JFB calls Ezra "a rabbi, or doctor, learned in the Mosaic law." Guzik lists a scribe's three duties as preserving, teaching and applying God's word, and he notes how far the scribes of Jesus' day had fallen from Ezra's pattern. On v10 Guzik makes seek, do and teach a pattern for anyone who handles Scripture, and JFB says Ezra "devoted himself, as the business of his life, to the work of instructing, reforming, and edifying others." JFB suggests Esther's influence at court lay behind the king's favor. That is a guess, and it depends on JFB's identification of Artaxerxes with the Ahasuerus of Esther, which most scholars now reject: Ahasuerus is Xerxes, Artaxerxes' father. Guzik reads v23 as the king hedging his bets with the gods of conquered peoples. Henry says such generosity from a pagan king will "rise up in judgment against the covetousness of wealthy professed Christians." On vv27–28 Henry writes, "If God give us his hand, we are bold and cheerful; if he withdraw it, we are weak as water."

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezra is a priest from Aaron's line who comes to God's people with the Law in his hand, sent with authority to teach and to set things right. He points ahead to a greater priest and teacher. Jesus did not only study and do and teach the law. He fulfilled it (Matt 5:17), and the crowds noticed that "he was teaching them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (Matt 7:29). The king's gold beautified the house of God for a season. Through Christ, God builds a temple of living stones (1 Pet 2:5) and writes his law on hearts (Jer 31:33), which no royal decree could do. And the good hand of God that carried Ezra 900 miles is the same hand that holds every believer: "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28).
