*Setting:* Chapter 6 ended the first half of Ecclesiastes with two questions: "Who knows what is good for man while he lives?" and "Who can tell man what will be after him?" (6:12). Chapter 7 begins to answer the first. It switches from first-person story to a chain of proverbs, many of them using the word "better" (*tov*), which links back to "what is good" in 6:12. These are some of the most surprising "better" sayings in the Bible: the day of death better than the day of birth, the house of mourning better than the house of feasting, sorrow better than laughter. Then the chapter turns harder. The Preacher faces the righteous dying young and the wicked living long (vv15–18), confesses that wisdom is beyond him (vv23–24), and ends with a bitter reflection on women and men and a single bright line: "God made man upright" (v29). For the question of who wrote the book, see the Setting of chapter 1.

#### The Contrast of Wisdom and Folly (vv1–29)
- **"A good name is better than precious ointment" (v1).** In Hebrew this is a wordplay: *tov shem mishshemen tov*, "good is a name more than good oil." *Shem* (name) and *shemen* (oil) sound almost the same. JFB and Guzik note the pun. Perfumed oil was a costly luxury, worn at feasts. Its scent fades in hours. A good name, meaning a person's character and reputation, lasts. Proverbs 22:1 says the same. JFB stresses that "name" means character before God, not just reputation with people.
- **"And the day of death than the day of birth" (v1).** The second half jolts the reader, as Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes. At birth no one knows how a life will go. At death the record is complete and the name is made. The Tyndale notes add that death ends life's troubles (4:2). For the believer, Paul says it even more strongly: "to depart and be with Christ… is far better" (Phil 1:23).
- **"It is better to go to the house of mourning than to go to the house of feasting… the living will lay it to heart" (v2).** A funeral teaches what a party never will: "this is the end of all mankind." Everyone dies, and seeing it makes the living think. Mourning in Israel lasted seven days (Gen 50:10; 1 Sam 31:13), with friends coming to sit with the family. Moses prayed, "Teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (Ps 90:12).
- **"Sorrow is better than laughter, for by sadness of face the heart is made glad" (v3).** A sad face can go with a heart that is being made better. "Made glad" (*yitav*) is literally "made good." Grief faced honestly can deepen and heal a person. Laughter used to avoid reality cannot. Paul speaks of a "godly grief" that "produces a repentance that leads to salvation" (2 Cor 7:10). This does not contradict the book's calls to enjoy life (2:24; 9:7). It is about not running from what is true.
- **"The heart of the wise is in the house of mourning, but the heart of fools is in the house of mirth" (v4).** Where a person's heart naturally goes shows what they are.
- **"Better… to hear the rebuke of the wise than to hear the song of fools" (v5).** Correction stings but helps. Flattery, and the empty songs of the party, do nothing. Proverbs: "Faithful are the wounds of a friend" (Prov 27:6). The Tyndale notes point to Proverbs 17:10.
- **"As the crackling of thorns under a pot, so is the laughter of the fools" (v6).** Another wordplay: *sirim* (thorns) under the *sir* (pot). The Hebrew sounds like the crackling fire it describes. Thorn branches flare up fast and loud, and burn out before the pot boils. JFB notes that slow-burning dried dung was the usual cooking fuel, which makes the quick, noisy thorn fire all the more vivid. The fool's laughter is noise, not heat.
- **"Surely oppression drives the wise into madness, and a bribe corrupts the heart" (v7).** Even the wise are not safe. "Oppression" (*'osheq*) can mean extortion, and the line may mean that taking unjust gain makes a wise man foolish. The second half says a bribe destroys judgment, as the Law warned: "a bribe blinds the clear-sighted" (Exod 23:8; Deut 16:19).
- **"Better is the end of a thing than its beginning, and the patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit" (v8).** Anyone can start. It takes patience to finish. Literally "long of spirit" against "high of spirit." The proud man is quick to start, quick to boast and quick to quit. The Tyndale notes put it plainly: finishing with patience is better than starting with big talk (1 Kgs 20:11).
- **"Be not quick in your spirit to become angry, for anger lodges in the heart of fools" (v9).** "Lodges" means rests or settles, as in the lap. Anger makes its home in a fool. James says the same: "slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (Jas 1:19–20; Prov 14:29).
- **"Say not, 'Why were the former days better than these?' For it is not from wisdom that you ask this" (v10).** Nostalgia is not wisdom. The past was never as golden as memory makes it, and every age has its evils (1:9–10). Israel in the wilderness remembered the "meat pots" of Egypt and forgot the slavery (Exod 16:3; Num 11:5). Guzik warns, with Kidner, against romanticizing the past.
- **"Wisdom is good with an inheritance, an advantage to those who see the sun" (v11).** "With" may mean "along with" (wisdom plus money is best) or "as good as" (wisdom is worth as much as an inheritance). "Those who see the sun" means the living.
- **"The protection of wisdom is like the protection of money… wisdom preserves the life of him who has it" (v12).** "Protection" is literally "shade" (*tsel*), welcome shelter in the hot sun. Money shelters a person from many troubles. Wisdom does too, and does more: it keeps a person alive. JFB adds that money only helps in good times, while wisdom also protects in bad ones.
- **"Consider the work of God: who can make straight what he has made crooked?" (v13).** An echo of 1:15. There it was a sad observation. Here God is named as the one who made it so. Some things in life are bent by God's own hand, and they cannot be straightened by complaint or effort. The Tyndale notes say trying to resist God's plans is pointless.
- **"In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity consider: God has made the one as well as the other" (v14).** This is one of the most practical verses in the book. When times are good, enjoy them. When times are bad, stop and think, and remember who sends both. Job said the same: "Shall we receive good from God, and shall we not receive evil?" (Job 2:10). The Tyndale notes connect it to Paul's contentment in plenty and in want (Phil 4:11–13). "So that man may not find out anything that will be after him" means we cannot predict what is coming next, so we must trust the God who knows. Guzik hears something near fatalism in vv13–14 from under-the-sun eyes, but the verse also leads to trust.
- **"There is a righteous man who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked man who prolongs his life in his evildoing" (v15).** This contradicts the simple rule that the good live long and the wicked die young. Proverbs states the rule (Prov 3:1–2; 10:27). Ecclesiastes notes the exceptions. Abel, the first man to die, was righteous and was murdered (Gen 4:8). Trapp, cited by Guzik, makes that point. Psalm 73 wrestles with the same problem.
- **"Be not overly righteous, and do not make yourself too wise. Why should you destroy yourself?" (v16).** This verse puzzles many readers. Surely no one can be too righteous? There are several readings. (1) The most common: it warns against self-righteousness, a show of strict religion that goes beyond what God asks, like the Pharisees' added rules. JFB reads it this way: "a fanatical, pharisaical righteousness." (2) It warns against thinking one's righteousness will guarantee a long life, given v15. (3) The Tyndale notes take a practical view: there is always more to do, and one can wear oneself out trying. (4) Some think the Preacher is giving worldly advice, the calculating middle path of under-the-sun thinking, which he does not finally endorse. Morgan, cited by Guzik, reads it this way, and Guzik adds that Jesus and Paul seemed unbalanced to many in their day. "Destroy yourself" may mean "be ruined" or "be appalled," left stunned when life does not reward goodness the way one expected.
- **"Be not overly wicked, neither be a fool. Why should you die before your time?" (v17).** The other side. This does not mean "a little wickedness is fine." Since "there is not a righteous man on earth who… never sins" (v20), all are somewhat wicked. The warning is not to throw oneself into evil. Folly kills (Prov 10:27). Nabal is the Tyndale notes' example, dead within days of his foolish insult to David (1 Sam 25). JFB adds that the verse implies "not wicked at all."
- **"The one who fears God shall come out from both of them" (v18).** Here is the real answer. The fear of God, not a calculated balance between too good and too bad, is the way through. The one who fears God will avoid both self-righteous pride and reckless sin.
- **"Wisdom gives strength to the wise man more than ten rulers who are in a city" (v19).** Ten rulers would be a city's full council of power. The Tyndale notes point to the wise woman of Abel who saved her city from Joab's army (2 Sam 20:15–22), and to the poor wise man of 9:14–15.
- **"Surely there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and never sins" (v20).** One of the clearest Old Testament statements of universal sin. Solomon said nearly the same in his prayer at the temple's dedication: "there is no one who does not sin" (1 Kgs 8:46). Paul gathers this with similar verses in Romans 3:10–12, 23. It explains vv16–17: no one is truly too righteous, and everyone needs mercy.
- **"Do not take to heart all the things that people say, lest you hear your servant cursing you. Your heart knows that many times you yourself have cursed others" (vv21–22).** Practical humility. Do not eavesdrop on every comment made about you. If you go looking for criticism, you will find it, even from your own staff. And be honest: you have said the same about others. The Tyndale notes see a call to be gracious with others' faults. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, advises having one blind eye and one deaf ear.
- **"I said, 'I will be wise,' but it was far from me. That which has been is far off, and deep, very deep" (vv23–24).** The wisest man in the world admits he could not reach the wisdom he sought. "Deep, very deep" (*'amoq 'amoq*) repeats the word for emphasis. Job 28 asks "where shall wisdom be found?" and answers, "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom" (Job 28:12, 28). Kidner, cited by Guzik, says that honestly admitting failure is a good path to the start of real wisdom. Paul: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!" (Rom 11:33).
- **"I turned my heart to know and to search out and to seek wisdom and the scheme of things" (v25).** "Scheme of things" (*cheshbon*) is a word for a sum, an account, the reckoning that adds things up. It appears again in v27 and leads to a pun in v29.
- **"I find something more bitter than death: the woman whose heart is snares and nets" (v26).** Proverbs warns again and again against the seductive woman (Prov 5:3–6; 7:6–27), and the Tyndale notes point there. The Preacher does not say all women are like this. He describes one kind, whose love is a trap. "He who pleases God escapes her," like Joseph fleeing Potiphar's wife (Gen 39:12), as JFB notes. For Solomon, this was painfully personal: "his wives turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kgs 11:4). Guzik notes that whether the old Solomon writes this as a repentant man is not something the text settles.
- **"One man among a thousand I found, but a woman among all these I have not found" (v28).** This is one of the most debated verses in the book. "Man" here is *adam*, which can mean a person of either sex. Several things are worth noticing. The "thousand" recalls Solomon's seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (1 Kgs 11:3), and JFB draws the link: Solomon's harem was the last place to look for a trustworthy companion. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says the failed search may say more about the searcher than about women. The Tyndale notes read it as the rarity of true virtue in his experience: he found very few good men and no good women. JFB insists this is "not an unqualified condemnation of the sex." Elsewhere Solomon praises the good wife as a gift from the LORD (Prov 18:22; 19:14; 31:10–31), and Ecclesiastes itself says "enjoy life with the wife whom you love" (9:9).
- **"See, this alone I found, that God made man upright, but they have sought out many schemes" (v29).** The chapter's conclusion, and it applies to both sexes equally. "Man" again is *adam*, humanity, and the verse looks back to creation: God made Adam and Eve upright (Gen 1:31). "They," the whole race, went looking for "many schemes" (*chishshevonot*), a play on the *cheshbon*, "scheme of things," that the Preacher was seeking (vv25, 27). He was trying to add up the sum of life. Humanity instead invented its own sums. The problem is not that God made people badly, but that people turned away. The Tyndale notes connect it with the fall and with Romans 3:10–18.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as rules for wise living: guard your reputation (v1), be serious (vv2–6), keep calm (vv7–10), act with prudence (vv11–12), submit to God's will (vv13–15), avoid dangerous extremes (vv16–18) and be gentle with those who wrong you (vv19–22). In vv23–29, he hears Solomon lamenting his own sin, "that mystery of iniquity, the having of many wives." JFB reads v28 messianically: Christ alone fully realizes what "man" was meant to be. Guzik passes on Eaton's remark that man was created neither sinful nor neutral, and Kidner's line that since futility was not the first word about our world, it need not be the last.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Surely there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and never sins" (v20). Ecclesiastes leaves no exceptions, and the New Testament agrees (Rom 3:23). Then it points to the one exception. Jesus "committed no sin, neither was deceit found in his mouth" (1 Pet 2:22). He is the "one man among a thousand" (v28), the true *adam* who stayed upright where the first Adam fell (v29; Rom 5:18–19). The "righteous man who perishes in his righteousness" (v15) was never more true than at the cross. And it was for the many who "sought out many schemes" that he died, "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (1 Pet 3:18). Because of him, the believer can say with Paul that the day of death really is better than the day of birth (v1): "to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Phil 1:21).
