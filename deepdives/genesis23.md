*Setting:* Some years have passed since Abraham took Isaac up Mount Moriah and came back down with him (ch 22). Sarah dies at 127, and since she bore Isaac at 90 (17:17), Isaac is now about 37 and Abraham about 137. The family had been living at Beersheba (22:19), but Sarah dies at Hebron, near the oaks of Mamre where the LORD once ate at Abraham's tent (ch 18). Chapter 22 closed with news from the old country: Abraham's brother Nahor has a growing family back in Mesopotamia, including a granddaughter named Rebekah (22:20–24). That sets up chapter 24, where Isaac will need a wife. Between those two family scenes stands this one, a funeral and a land deal. More than sixty years after God said "To your offspring I will give this land" (12:7), Abraham still owns none of it. This chapter is about how he gets the first piece, and why a grave is what he buys.

#### Sarah's Death and Burial (vv1–20)
- **One heading for the whole chapter.** The ESV gives the chapter a single heading. The story moves in three steps: Sarah's death and Abraham's grief (vv1–2), the long public negotiation with the Hittites and Ephron (vv3–16), and the legal transfer and burial (vv17–20).
- **"Sarah lived 127 years" (v1).** Sarah is the only woman in the Bible whose age at death is recorded. The Hebrew literally says "the life of Sarah was a hundred years and twenty years and seven years," and Jewish tradition names this whole section of Genesis *Chayei Sarah*, "the life of Sarah," after its opening words. The woman who laughed at the promise (18:12) lived to see her son grown. Later Scripture holds her up as a mother of God's people: "look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who bore you" (Isa 51:2), and Peter names her as a model of trust in God (1 Pet 3:5–6).
- **"Kiriath-arba (that is, Hebron)" (v2).** Kiriath-arba means "city of Arba," named after a famous figure among the Anakim (Josh 14:15). The narrator adds the later name so readers would recognize it. Hebron sits high in the hill country of Judah, about 20 miles south of Jerusalem, and will matter again: Caleb claims it (Josh 14:13–14), and David reigns there for seven years before moving to Jerusalem (2 Sam 2:11).
- **"Abraham went in to mourn for Sarah and to weep for her" (v2).** "Went in" may mean he came to her tent, or that he came from Beersheba, since 22:19 left him there. The text doesn't say why they were apart. A later Jewish tradition connects Sarah's death with the shock of hearing what nearly happened to Isaac on Moriah, but that is tradition, not the text. The two verbs matter. "Mourn" describes the public, formal rites of grief, and "weep" is his own tears. The man of faith who could raise the knife at God's word also weeps at his wife's side. Faith does not make grief unnecessary. Paul says believers grieve, but not "as others do who have no hope" (1 Thess 4:13).
- **"Abraham rose up from before his dead" (v3).** He had been sitting or lying beside her body. Now he gets up because there is work to do. The verb "rose" (*qum*) comes back in vv7 and 17, where the ESV says the field was "made over," literally that it "stood" or "was established" to Abraham. The man who rises to act ends up with land that is legally "made to stand" as his.
- **"The Hittites" (v3).** The Hebrew is "the sons of Heth" (ESV footnote), and Heth is listed among the sons of Canaan in the table of nations (10:15). Who were they? The Tyndale notes suggest they were a group that had migrated south from the great Hittite empire in Asia Minor. Other scholars note that the names here (Ephron, Zohar) are Semitic, and think the "sons of Heth" were a local Canaanite people who should not be confused with the empire. Either way, they hold the land and Abraham does not.
- **"I am a sojourner and foreigner among you" (v4).** The two words are *ger* and *toshav*. The lexicon defines a *ger* as a temporary dweller or newcomer with "no inherited rights," and a *toshav* as a sojourner of an even more temporary and dependent kind. Abraham is describing his legal position honestly: a resident outsider who cannot simply claim a plot of ground. The same pair later describes Israel's relationship to God's land: "the land is mine. For you are strangers and sojourners with me" (Lev 25:23), and David prays, "we are strangers before you and sojourners, as all our fathers were" (1 Chr 29:15). Hebrews reads Abraham's whole life this way. He "went to live in the land of promise, as in a foreign land, living in tents" (Heb 11:9), and the patriarchs "acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (Heb 11:13).
- **"Give me property among you for a burying place" (v4).** "Property" is *achuzzah*, "possession," the same word God used when he promised "all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession" (17:8). Abraham asks for a "possession" of a grave. The irony is gentle but real. The man promised the whole land as a possession has to ask strangers for enough of it to bury his wife. Stephen says God gave Abraham "no inheritance in it, not even a foot's length" (Acts 7:5).
- **"You are a prince of God among us" (v6).** "Prince" is *nasi*, which the lexicon explains as "one lifted up," a chief. "Of God" (*Elohim*) can be taken literally, a prince whom God has blessed, or as a Hebrew way of saying "mighty," which is why the ESV footnote offers "a mighty prince." Either way, the Hittites see Abraham as a man of weight. God had promised to "make your name great" (12:2), and here is that promise showing up in a neighbor's mouth.
- **"Bury your dead in the choicest of our tombs" (v6).** The offer sounds generous, and it may partly be. But the Tyndale notes point out that it would let Abraham bury Sarah without owning anything, as a guest in someone else's tomb. That would leave him obligated to them and still landless. Abraham politely does not take it.
- **"Abraham rose and bowed to the Hittites" (v7).** The whole chapter is full of bowing, "my lord," and "hear me." This is the courtesy language of public business in the ancient Near East. Abraham is rich and respected, but he does not throw his weight around. He negotiates as a guest, with dignity and respect for the people whose land it is.
- **"The cave of Machpelah" (v9).** Abraham knows exactly what he wants: a particular cave "at the end" of Ephron's field. The meaning of the name Machpelah is uncertain. The old Greek translation took it as "the double" (cave), and many have followed that, though the lexicon calls the meaning "dubious." Abraham asks the town to act as go-between, and he says from the start, "for the full price." He wants a purchase, not a favor.
- **"Ephron was sitting among the Hittites… of all who went in at the gate of his city" (v10).** The city gate was the town's courthouse and marketplace, where elders sat and deals became binding in front of witnesses (compare Ruth 4:1–11). "All who went in at the gate" means the citizens with standing in the town. Ephron happens to be sitting right there, so the negotiation is public from the first word.
- **"I give you the field, and I give you the cave" (v11).** Ephron offers a gift, and offers more than was asked: the whole field, not just the cave. Readers have long seen this as the polite opening move of Near Eastern bargaining, not a real gift. The Tyndale notes add that Ephron likely wanted to unload the whole field and get the highest price. Some scholars have suggested that owning a whole field could carry duties to the local ruler that owning only a cave might not, and have compared ancient legal texts on land sales. That is scholarly reconstruction. The text itself only shows the field growing from "the end of his field" (v9) to the whole field with its trees (v17).
- **"I give the price of the field" (v13).** Abraham answers "give" with "give." Ephron says "I give you the field," and Abraham says, in effect, "I give you the money." He will not be in anyone's debt for this ground. It echoes what he told the king of Sodom: "I would not take a thread or a sandal strap… lest you should say, 'I have made Abram rich'" (14:23). David later says the same to Araunah: "I will not offer burnt offerings to the LORD my God that cost me nothing" (2 Sam 24:24).
- **"Four hundred shekels of silver, what is that between you and me?" (v15).** Ephron names his price while pretending it is nothing. A shekel was a weight, not a coin, roughly 11 to 12 grams, so 400 shekels is around 4.5 kilograms (about 10 pounds) of silver. The Tyndale notes call it a very high price. For comparison, Jeremiah bought a field for seventeen shekels (Jer 32:9), and David paid fifty shekels of silver for Araunah's threshing floor and oxen (2 Sam 24:24). Prices varied across centuries, but 400 shekels for a field and a cave looks steep.
- **"Abraham weighed out for Ephron the silver" (v16).** Abraham doesn't haggle. He pays the full asking price on the spot, in public, "in the hearing of the Hittites." Coins did not appear until many centuries later, so silver was weighed on scales. "According to the weights current among the merchants" means by the standard trade weights everyone accepted, so no one could later say he was shortchanged. Abraham wanted this title to be beyond question, and he was willing to overpay to get it.
- **"Made over to Abraham as a possession" (vv17–18).** The description reads like a legal deed. It names the field, its location ("east of Mamre"), the cave, "all the trees that were in the field, throughout its whole area," and the witnesses, "before all who went in at the gate of his city." Every detail closes a loophole. For the first time, a piece of Canaan legally belongs to Abraham's family.
- **"After this, Abraham buried Sarah his wife" (v19).** The burial itself gets one verse. The narrator spends sixteen verses on the purchase because the purchase is the point. The Tyndale notes observe that people were normally buried in their ancestral homeland. By burying Sarah in Canaan, not back in Mesopotamia where his relatives still lived (22:20–24), Abraham shows where he believes his family's future lies.
- **The family tomb.** Machpelah becomes the burial place of the patriarchs. Isaac and Ishmael bury Abraham here (25:9). Isaac, Rebekah and Leah are buried here, and Jacob, dying in Egypt, insists on being carried back to this cave and names its history in detail (49:29–32; 50:13). Rachel is the exception, buried on the road to Bethlehem (35:19). Stephen's summary in Acts 7:16 mentions a tomb "that Abraham had bought… from the sons of Hamor in Shechem," which seems to blend this purchase with Jacob's purchase at Shechem (33:19). Interpreters handle this in different ways, most often as Stephen compressing two burial purchases into one sentence. Today a large stone enclosure built in the time of Herod the Great stands over the traditional site of the cave in Hebron, revered by Jews, Christians and Muslims.
- **"The field and the cave… were made over to Abraham as property for a burying place by the Hittites" (v20).** The chapter ends by repeating the legal result. The only land Abraham ever owned in Canaan was a grave. That is not a sign the promise failed. It is a sign he believed it. A man who expects his family to inherit the land buries his dead in it, and stakes his claim in the one place death cannot take from him. The promise is not yet fulfilled (Acts 7:5), but this field is a down payment on it.
- **Commentators:** All three agree that Sarah's death and burial are recorded to honor her. JFB says she "is the only woman in Scripture whose age, death, and burial are mentioned," and Guzik makes the same point and adds that Scripture twice holds her up as a model (Isa 51:1–2; 1 Pet 3:3–6). On Abraham's grief, Henry insists "his sorrow was not counterfeit, but real," and adds that there must also be "a time of standing up from before their dead," since "Weeping must not hinder sowing." Guzik draws the same balance from 1 Thessalonians 4:13. On v4 Henry says, "The death of our relations should effectually remind us that we are not at home in this world." On the bargaining they differ in tone. JFB is blunt that Ephron's generosity was "only a show" and that his indifference about the price "was mere affectation," which matches the Tyndale notes and Guzik's description of a customary opening offer followed by a high price. Henry reads Ephron far more kindly, praising his "great contempt of worldly wealth" and the "noble generosity of these Canaanites." Henry also gives two reasons Abraham insisted on paying: justice, because he could afford it, and prudence, so that Ephron or his heirs could never take the field back or say "I have made Abraham rich." On v7 Henry writes, "Religion teaches good manners," and Guzik likewise holds up Abraham's courtesy and fairness as a model for how believers should do business. JFB says Machpelah means "the 'double cave,'" which follows the old Greek translation. The lexicon accepts that as one reading but calls the meaning of the name uncertain. On the burial, Henry notes "a burying-place was the first spot of ground Abraham possessed in Canaan," and reads it as a sign of resurrection hope: Abraham "said plainly that he sought a better country." He also observes that the cave was at the end of the field, "for, whatever our possessions are, there is a sepulchre at the end of them." JFB comments on vv1–3, 9–11, 15–16 and 19, with no separate notes on vv4–8, 12–14, 17–18 or 20.

#### Pointing to Christ
Abraham died owning only a grave in the land God promised him. Hebrews says he and the others "died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar," because they were looking for "a better country, that is, a heavenly one" (Heb 11:13, 16). The burial at Machpelah was an act of hope that God would keep his word beyond death. Jesus, Abraham's offspring, also had no place of his own to lay his head, and was buried in a borrowed tomb in a garden (Matt 8:20; John 19:41–42). His grave did not hold him. The God who promised Abraham a land also raised Jesus as "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Cor 15:20), and so the graves of those who die in him are, like Machpelah, a claim staked on a promise God will keep.
