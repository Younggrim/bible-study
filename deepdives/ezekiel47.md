*Setting:* The temple vision of chapters 40–48 is nearing its end (573 BC). Ezekiel has watched God's glory return to the temple (ch 43) and has been given the rules for priests, prince, offerings and feasts (chs 44–46). The tour of the building ended in the kitchens of the outer court (46:19–24). Now the vision opens out from the temple to the whole land. First comes one of the most loved pictures in the prophets: a stream flowing from the temple that grows into a great river and brings life to the Dead Sea. Then God sets the borders of the land and begins to divide it among the twelve tribes, with a surprising place for foreigners. For exiles in the dry plain of Babylon, who had lost their land and their temple, both halves of the chapter were promises of a future more abundant than anything Israel had known.

#### Water Flowing from the Temple (vv1–12)
- **"Water was issuing from below the threshold of the temple toward the east" (v1).** The water rises inside the sanctuary itself, under the threshold where the glory of the LORD had paused on its way out (10:4, 18). It comes from God's house, not from any natural spring.
- **"South of the altar" (v1).** The Tyndale notes point out that in Solomon's temple this spot was where the great bronze "Sea" stood, a huge basin of water for the priests' washing (1 Kings 7:23–26, 39). In Ezekiel's temple there is no bronze Sea. In its place is a living, flowing river.
- **Jerusalem had no river.** Unlike Babylon on the Euphrates or Nineveh on the Tigris, Jerusalem depended on one small spring, the Gihon, and on cisterns. Yet the psalmist sang, "There is a river whose streams make glad the city of God" (Ps 46:4), and other prophets saw the same: "a fountain shall come forth from the house of the LORD" (Joel 3:18), and "living waters shall flow out from Jerusalem" (Zech 14:8).
- **Eden in the background.** In Genesis "a river flowed out of Eden to water the garden" (Gen 2:10). Ezekiel has already compared the restored land to "the garden of Eden" (36:35). The temple was decorated with palm trees and cherubim (41:18), the imagery of Eden. Now Eden's river flows again.
- **"The water was trickling out on the south side" (v2).** The outer east gate is shut (44:2), so the guide takes Ezekiel around by the north gate. "Trickling" is *pakah*, a verb that appears only here. It is related to the word for a small flask, so the picture is water dribbling as from a jar. The river begins as almost nothing.
- **"The man measured a thousand cubits… ankle-deep" (v3).** A thousand long cubits is about a third of a mile (about 520 m). Every third of a mile the water is deeper: ankle-deep, knee-deep, waist-deep (v4), and then **"a river that I could not pass through… deep enough to swim in"** (v5). No tributaries join it. It grows by itself, which is impossible for a natural stream. The guide who measured walls in reeds now measures a river that cannot be contained.
- **"Son of man, have you seen this?" (v6).** The guide's only question in the whole vision. It is an invitation to wonder, and to take in the lesson the Tyndale notes draw out: God's work often starts very small and grows beyond all expectation (compare Zech 4:10; Matt 13:31–33).
- **"Very many trees on the one side and on the other" (v7).** Ezekiel is brought back to the bank and sees what he missed while wading. The land between Jerusalem and the Dead Sea is the Judean wilderness, bare hills where almost nothing grows. Now it is lined with trees.
- **"This water flows toward the eastern region and goes down into the Arabah, and enters the sea" (v8).** The Arabah is the great rift valley that runs from the Sea of Galilee through the Jordan valley and the Dead Sea south toward the Gulf of Aqaba. The "sea" is the Dead Sea, the lowest place on the earth's land surface, about 1,400 feet (430 m) below sea level. Its water is around a third salt and minerals, several times saltier than the ocean, and nothing but tiny microbes lives in it.
- **"The water will become fresh" (v8).** The Hebrew is "will be healed" (*rapha'*), the same word used for healing sickness. Elisha "healed" the bad water of Jericho, a few miles north, with a bowl of salt (2 Kings 2:19–22). Here the whole sea of death is healed.
- **"Wherever the river goes, every living creature that swarms will live" (v9).** "Swarms" recalls the creation account, where the waters "swarm with swarms of living creatures" (Gen 1:20–21). This is new creation language. "River" here is literally a dual form, "two rivers," which some connect with Zechariah's waters flowing both east and west (Zech 14:8). The ESV takes it as one river, as do most translations. The last phrase states the theme of the whole section: **"everything will live where the river goes."**
- **"Fishermen will stand beside the sea. From Engedi to Eneglaim" (v10).** En-gedi, "spring of the young goat," is the oasis on the Dead Sea's western shore where David hid from Saul (1 Sam 24:1). Eneglaim, "spring of two calves," is uncertain. The Tyndale notes place it on the eastern shore, while many scholars place it at the north end near Ain Feshkha, close to Qumran, where the Dead Sea Scrolls were found. Either way, a shore where no fisherman ever cast a net becomes a place for drying nets. **"Like the fish of the Great Sea"** means as many and as varied as in the Mediterranean.
- **"Its swamps and marshes will not become fresh; they are to be left for salt" (v11).** This is a realistic and kindly detail. Salt was precious: it preserved food, seasoned offerings (43:24; Lev 2:13), and was mined along the Dead Sea for thousands of years. God's renewal does not destroy what is useful. Some readers, like JFB and Henry, also take the untouched marshes as a warning that places that refuse the river stay barren.
- **"Their leaves will not wither, nor their fruit fail, but they will bear fresh fruit every month" (v12).** This echoes Psalm 1:3, the righteous man "like a tree planted by streams of water… its leaf does not wither." Fruit every month, all year, goes beyond anything in nature. **"Because the water for them flows from the sanctuary"** gives the reason: the source is God himself.
- **"Their fruit will be for food, and their leaves for healing" (v12).** John's vision of the new Jerusalem takes this up directly: "the river of the water of life, bright as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb," with the tree of life on either side, yielding "its fruit each month," and "the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" (Rev 22:1–2). John places the river in a city with no temple at all, because God and the Lamb are its temple (Rev 21:22).
- **Readings of the river.** Those who expect a literal millennial temple, like Guzik, look for a real river and a healed Dead Sea, connected with the reshaping of the land in Zechariah 14:4–8. Henry, JFB and many others read it as the gospel and the Spirit flowing out from Jerusalem to the world. Jesus himself used this picture: "Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.' Now this he said about the Spirit" (John 7:38–39). He said it at the Feast of Booths, when, according to Jewish tradition recorded in the Mishnah, priests poured out water at the altar each day. Many think he had Ezekiel 47 in mind.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "these waters signify the gospel of Christ, which went forth from Jerusalem," and on the depths urges believers "to go to the bottom of them as far as we can." On v11 he reads the marshes as those who will not be healed: "They shall be given to salt, to perpetual barrenness." On v12, "The leaf shall be for medicine, and it shall not fade." JFB applies the waters directly to Christ: "from His pierced side flow the living waters" (compare John 19:34). He reads the deepening water as Scripture, with some truths easy to grasp and others "of which we can only adore the depth." He links the many fish to the 153 fish of John 21, and notes the plural "trees," not one tree of life as in Eden. Guzik takes the river as literal and future, and also applies its depths to Christian growth, citing F.B. Meyer and a saying often traced to Gregory the Great and passed on by Trapp: Scripture has shallows where a lamb can wade and depths where an elephant must swim.

#### Division of the Land (vv13–23)
- **"This is the boundary by which you shall divide the land for inheritance among the twelve tribes of Israel" (v13).** The northern tribes had been scattered by Assyria about 150 years earlier (2 Kings 17), and many thought of them as lost. Ezekiel had already acted out the reunion of Judah and Joseph as one stick in God's hand (37:15–22). Here all twelve tribes receive land again.
- **"Joseph shall have two portions" (v13).** Jacob adopted Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh as his own (Gen 48:5), giving Joseph the double share of the firstborn that Reuben lost (1 Chr 5:1–2). Levi receives no tribal land, because the priests and Levites live in the holy district (45:4–5; 44:28), so Joseph's two portions keep the count at twelve.
- **"You shall divide equally what I swore to give to your fathers" (v14).** In Joshua's day the larger tribes got larger territories (Num 26:54). Here every tribe gets an equal strip (48:1–29). **"What I swore"** is literally "I lifted my hand," the oath gesture (20:5–6, 28). The promise to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Gen 12:7; 15:18; 26:3; 28:13) still stands after the exile.
- **The boundaries (vv15–20).** The borders follow closely those God gave Moses in Numbers 34:1–12. The north runs from the Mediterranean ("the Great Sea") inland past Hethlon and **Lebo-hamath**, "the entrance of Hamath," probably a pass in the Lebanon range toward the city of Hamath in Syria (vv15–17). Several towns on the list, like Zedad, Berothah, Sibraim and Hazer-hatticon, cannot be located with certainty. **Hauran** is the plateau east of Galilee (v18).
- **"Along the Jordan between Gilead and the land of Israel" (v18).** The east border is the Jordan River. The Transjordan, where Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh had settled (Num 32), lies outside. The Tyndale notes explain that those lands were never part of the original promise to Moses, though Israel had long lived there.
- **"From Tamar as far as the waters of Meribah-kadesh, from there along the Brook of Egypt" (v19).** Tamar was probably south of the Dead Sea. Meribah-kadesh is Kadesh-barnea, the oasis where Israel camped in the wilderness and Moses struck the rock (Num 20:1–13; 27:14). The "Brook of Egypt" is usually identified with the Wadi el-Arish, the dry riverbed in the northern Sinai.
- **"The Great Sea shall be the boundary" (v20).** The Mediterranean forms the west. The Tyndale notes observe that Israel had never fully held all this land, even under David and Solomon, though Solomon's sphere of rule touched these limits (1 Kings 8:65). The promise is finally to be complete.
- **"Divide this land among you according to the tribes of Israel" (v21).** "Divide" here is to share out by lot, as under Joshua.
- **"For the sojourners who reside among you and have had children among you" (vv22–23).** A "sojourner" (*ger*) was a resident foreigner. The law of Moses already told Israel to love the sojourner as themselves (Lev 19:33–34) and gave him the same law (Num 15:15–16). But it never gave him land. Land stayed in Israelite families. Here foreigners who have settled and raised families in Israel are to receive an inheritance **"as native-born children of Israel,"** within whichever tribe they live. JFB calls this "altogether unprecedented under the old covenant." The Tyndale notes understand it of foreigners who had joined Israel's faith, like Rahab or Ruth (Ruth 1:16), and Isaiah had promised a welcome to foreigners who hold fast to God's covenant (Isa 56:3–7).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees God's oath to the fathers as the security of the whole division, and says the strangers "shall have inheritance among the tribes, as if they had been native Israelites," which he takes as a sign of Gentiles being brought into the church. JFB explains Joseph's two portions by Reuben's lost birthright and Levi's exclusion from the count, and reads vv22–23 as a picture of spiritual inclusion. On the boundaries he names Tamar as "the last town of Judea, by the Dead Sea," a location others debate. Guzik treats the borders as literal but says many sites cannot be fixed, partly because he expects the land itself to change. Citing Vawter and Hoppe, he notes that the gift of land to foreigners goes beyond anything the Torah required.

#### Pointing to Christ
The river begins as a trickle under the threshold of God's house and becomes a flood that heals the sea of death: "everything will live where the river goes." Jesus stood in the temple courts and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37), and he called himself the temple (John 2:19–21). From him the Spirit flows outward to Judea, Samaria and the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8), making dead places live. The chapter ends with foreigners given an inheritance as if they were native-born, and that is the gospel Paul preached: "you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Eph 2:19). The river's last appearance in Scripture flows from "the throne of God and of the Lamb," with leaves "for the healing of the nations" (Rev 22:1–2).
