*Setting:* Babylonia, around 592 BC, among the exiles by the Chebar canal. Chapters 4–5 acted out and explained the fall of Jerusalem. Chapter 6 widens the view from the city to the whole land. Ezekiel is told to turn and face west, across the desert, toward "the mountains of Israel," and to speak to them directly. The hill country of Judah and Israel had been dotted for centuries with "high places," hilltop shrines where Canaanite worship mixed with or replaced the worship of the LORD. Kings Hezekiah and Josiah had torn many of them down (2 Kings 18:4; 23:8–20), but after Josiah's death in 609 BC they came back. The chapter has two oracles. The first (vv1–10) announces judgment on the shrines and then, unexpectedly, a remnant who will remember God. The second (vv11–14) returns to judgment with a lament. A phrase from the book's great refrain, "you shall know that I am the LORD," rings four times in fourteen verses (vv7, 10, 13, 14).

#### Judgment Against Idolatry (vv1–14)
- **"Set your face toward the mountains of Israel" (v2).** "Setting the face" toward someone is Ezekiel's posture for a hostile prophecy (13:17; 21:2; 25:2). The phrase "mountains of Israel" is almost unique to Ezekiel, who uses it about seventeen times. Here they are a target. In chapter 36 the same mountains will hear a promise: "you, O mountains of Israel, shall shoot forth your branches and yield your fruit to my people Israel" (36:8). The land that is judged here is the land that will be restored.
- **"To the mountains and the hills, to the ravines and the valleys" (v3).** The whole landscape is addressed, high and low. Idolatry happened on the hilltops, but also down in the gullies. The Valley of Hinnom just outside Jerusalem was where children were burned as offerings to Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31). The "ravines" (*'aphiqim*) are stream channels, and Isaiah condemns those who "slaughter your children in the valleys, under the clefts of the rocks" (Isa 57:5).
- **"Behold, I, even I, will bring a sword upon you, and I will destroy your high places" (v3).** "I, even I" repeats the dread phrase of 5:8. A "high place" (*bamah*) was an open-air shrine, often on a hill, with an altar, a standing stone and a wooden pole or tree for the goddess Asherah. Before the temple was built, Israel's own worship sometimes happened at such places (1 Sam 9:12; 1 Kings 3:2). But the law had told Israel to destroy the Canaanite shrines and worship only where God chose (Deut 12:2–5), and in practice the high places pulled Israel toward the gods of the land. The irony is that God would use Babylon, itself an idol-worshiping power, to wipe out Israel's idols.
- **"Your incense altars shall be broken" (v4).** The word *ḥammanim* was once translated "images" or "sun images" (KJV). Inscriptions found since then show it was a small altar or stand for burning incense, and modern translations follow that. Several have been excavated in Israel, small stone pillars with a shallow bowl on top.
- **"I will cast down your slain before your idols" (v4).** This is the first use in the book of Ezekiel's favorite word for idols, *gillulim*, which appears in Ezekiel nearly forty times, far more than in the rest of the Old Testament put together. It is a word of contempt. It seems to come from *gel*, "dung," shaped to sound like *shiqquts*, "detestable thing." It is something like "dung-gods" or "dung pellets." A priest like Ezekiel could hardly have chosen a more insulting word.
- **"I will scatter your bones around your altars" (v5).** Nothing defiled a holy place like human bones. When Josiah wanted to put the altar at Bethel permanently out of use, he burned human bones on it (2 Kings 23:14–16, fulfilling 1 Kings 13:2). Here the worshipers' own bodies become the pollution of the shrines they loved. The gods they trusted lie silent while their worshipers lie dead before them. Jeremiah describes the bones of Jerusalem's leaders spread out "before the sun and the moon and all the host of heaven, which they have loved and served" (Jer 8:1–2).
- **"Your works wiped out" (v6).** The idols were just that, "works," things people made with their hands (Isa 44:9–20). Verse 6 piles up the destruction: cities waste, high places ruined, altars waste, idols broken, incense altars cut down.
- **"And you shall know that I am the LORD" (v7).** This is the first full use of the refrain that runs through the book, some seventy times in all. The idea goes back to the exodus, when Israel and Egypt would "know that I am the LORD" by what God did (Exod 6:7; 7:5). In the exodus they learned it through rescue. Now they will learn it through judgment. Later in the book they will learn it again through restoration (36:11; 37:6, 13).
- **"Yet I will leave some of you alive" (v8).** The turn is sudden. This is the few hairs tied into the robe of 5:3. Some will escape the sword, not by staying in Judah but by being "scattered through the countries." The exile itself becomes the place where God preserves a people.
- **"Those of you who escape will remember me" (v9).** Remembering, in the Bible, is more than recalling a fact. It means turning the mind and heart back toward someone. Israel had forgotten God in the land of plenty, as Moses warned it would (Deut 8:11–14). In the land of captivity it will remember him. Moses had foreseen this too: "from there you will seek the LORD your God and you will find him, if you search after him with all your heart" (Deut 4:29).
- **"How I have been broken over their whoring heart" (v9).** This is one of the most striking lines in Ezekiel. God speaks of himself as a husband who has been crushed by an unfaithful wife. The verb *nishbarti*, "I have been broken," is passive in the Hebrew text. Some scholars and early versions read it as active instead, "I broke their whoring heart," which would mean God shattered their pride through the exile. The ESV keeps the Hebrew as it stands, and either way the verse shows that sin is not just lawbreaking but betrayal of a relationship. "Whoring" (*zanah*) is the language of prostitution, which the prophets used for idolatry because Israel was bound to the LORD as a wife (Hos 2; Jer 3). Ezekiel will develop it at painful length in chapters 16 and 23.
- **"Their eyes that go whoring after their idols" (v9).** Unfaithfulness starts with the heart and shows itself through the eyes. The law used the same picture: "not to follow after your own heart and your own eyes, which you are inclined to whore after" (Num 15:39).
- **"They will be loathsome in their own sight" (v9).** The remnant will finally see their sin as God sees it, and be disgusted with themselves. This self-loathing comes after they remember God, not before. It is not despair. It is the beginning of repentance. Ezekiel returns to it in the promises of restoration: "you shall loathe yourselves for your iniquities" when God has cleansed them and given them a new heart (36:31; see 20:43).
- **"I have not said in vain that I would do this evil to them" (v10).** "Evil" here (*ra'ah*) means disaster or calamity, not moral wrong. God's warnings were not empty threats. When they come true, the survivors will know that the God who spoke them is real.
- **"Clap your hands and stamp your foot and say, Alas" (v11).** These are dramatic gestures. Elsewhere Israel's enemies clap and stamp in malicious glee at its ruin (25:6). Here the prophet does it, and **"Alas"** (*'aḥ*) makes it a cry of grief. It is probably both: an emphatic, almost violent gesture that grabs attention, expressing God's anger and the prophet's sorrow together. Ezekiel claps again when the sword of judgment is announced (21:14, 17).
- **"By the sword, by famine, and by pestilence" (v11).** The three judgments of 5:12 come back, now spread over the whole land.
- **"He who is far off… he who is near… he who is left" (v12).** No distance offers safety. Those far from the fighting die of plague. Those near it fall by the sword. Those who survive in the besieged city starve. **"Thus I will spend my fury upon them"** echoes 5:13.
- **"On every high hill, on all the mountaintops, under every green tree, and under every leafy oak" (v13).** This phrase is close to a formula. It comes from Deuteronomy 12:2, the command to destroy the Canaanite shrines "on the high mountains and on the hills and under every green tree," and the prophets used it again and again to describe Israel's idolatry (1 Kings 14:23; 2 Kings 17:10; Jer 2:20; Hos 4:13). Sacred trees were linked with the fertility goddess. **"Wherever they offered pleasing aroma to all their idols."** "Pleasing aroma" is the phrase the law uses for sacrifices that please the LORD (Lev 1:9). Israel offered God's own worship to dung-gods.
- **"From the wilderness to Riblah" (v14).** The Hebrew text reads "Diblah," a name found nowhere else. In Hebrew the letters *d* (ד) and *r* (ר) look very much alike, and most scholars, with the ESV, read Riblah. The wilderness was the desert of the far south, and Riblah lay in the far north on the Orontes River in Syria, near the old northern edge of David's kingdom. The phrase means the whole land, like "from Dan to Beersheba." There is a dark irony in it. Riblah was where Nebuchadnezzar set up his headquarters, and where Zedekiah's sons were killed before his eyes and Judah's leaders were executed after Jerusalem fell (2 Kings 25:6–7, 20–21).
- **"Then they will know that I am the LORD" (v14).** The chapter ends on the refrain.
- **Commentators:** Guzik describes the typical high place with its altar, standing stone and Asherah pole, and notes that the word *bamah* could mean any open-air shrine, not only one on a hilltop. He also points out that after the exile Israel never returned to idol worship in the way it had before. Henry: "God sometimes ruins idolatries even by the hands of idolaters." He calls the idols "dunghill-deities," catching the sense of *gillulim*. JFB says the corpses "pollute the idols" the people thought sacred. JFB still mentions the old rendering "sun images" for v4, which later discoveries have overtaken. Henry calls idolatry spiritual adultery and writes, "Nothing grieves a true penitent so much as to think that his sin has been a grief to God." He adds that true repentance includes loathing both sin and oneself. Guzik ties the image of God crushed by an adulterous heart to Hosea's marriage, and draws a three-step pattern from vv8–10: remembering God, then repentance, then a restored relationship. JFB mentions Fairbairn's reading "I will break" but prefers the English Version's "I am broken," and says the penitent remnant "shall not wait for men to condemn them," because they condemn themselves. The Tyndale notes say that Israel first learned that God is the LORD through the exodus rescue, forgot, and would learn it again through judgment. Henry: "The sins of sinners are the sorrows of God's faithful servants," taking the gestures of v11 as grief. Guzik weighs readings of the gestures as either lament or defiance and sees them as meant above all to wake up a people who took idolatry lightly. On v14 Guzik and the ESV read Riblah, explaining *Diblah* as a slip between two similar Hebrew letters. JFB keeps the Hebrew and identifies Diblath with Diblathaim in Moab, east of the Dead Sea. Henry also keeps "Diblath." Most modern scholars prefer Riblah because it gives a north–south boundary that makes sense.

#### Pointing to Christ
"I have been broken over their whoring heart." Ezekiel lets us glimpse a God who is not unmoved by betrayal but wounded by it. That glimpse comes into full view at the cross. There God's grief over sin and his judgment on sin meet in one person. Jesus was broken, literally, for a people whose hearts had gone after other gods (1 Cor 11:24; Isa 53:5). The remnant in exile would remember God, loathe their sin, and know he is the LORD. In the gospel this happens to the "remnant, chosen by grace" (Rom 11:5): through the Spirit they look on the one they pierced, and they mourn (Zech 12:10; John 19:37). And Ezekiel's high places, where Israel worshiped "on every high hill," give way to what Jesus told the Samaritan woman: the true worshipers would worship the Father not on this mountain or that, but "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24).
