*Setting:* About 959 BC, in Jerusalem. The temple was finished in the eighth month of Solomon's eleventh year (6:38), and chapters 6–7 described its building and its bronze and gold furnishings. Now comes the day the building becomes the house of God. The ark of the covenant, which David had brought to Jerusalem and kept in a tent in the City of David (2 Sam 6:17), is carried up the hill into the Most Holy Place. God's glory fills the house, and Solomon blesses the people, prays the longest prayer in the historical books, blesses the people again and leads a national feast. Second Chronicles 5–7 tells the same story and adds the singers, the trumpets and fire falling from heaven. This chapter is the high point of Solomon's reign, and its prayer looks far ahead, all the way to exile.

#### The Ark Brought into the Temple (vv1–11)
- **"Solomon assembled the elders of Israel and all the heads of the tribes" (v1).** This is a national event, not a palace ceremony. The ark was moving only a short distance, from the City of David, the old Jebusite ridge called Zion, up to the temple mount just north of it. But it was moving from a temporary tent to a permanent house, and the whole nation was summoned to watch.
- **"At the feast in the month Ethanim, which is the seventh month" (v2).** The temple was finished in the eighth month (6:38), so Solomon waited about eleven months to dedicate it. He chose the Feast of Booths (Tabernacles), the autumn harvest festival, when Israel lived in shelters to remember the wilderness years (Lev 23:33–43). It was fitting: the people who once followed a portable tent were now seeing God's house set down in the land. Deuteronomy 31:10–13 also commanded the law to be read to all Israel at this feast, and the Tyndale notes suggest Solomon was leading a covenant renewal. Ethanim is an old Canaanite month name, usually explained as "ever-flowing streams," the season when only the year-round streams still ran before the autumn rains.
- **"The priests took up the ark" (v3).** This time the ark was carried the right way, on the shoulders of Levites and priests with its poles (Num 4:15). Readers remember the first attempt to bring it to Jerusalem on a cart, when Uzzah died (2 Sam 6:3–7). David learned the lesson (1 Chr 15:13–15), and Solomon kept it.
- **"The tent of meeting, and all the holy vessels" (v4).** The old tabernacle from Moses' day, which had stood at Gibeon (3:4; 2 Chr 1:3), was brought up too, probably to be stored in the temple. The old worship was not thrown away. It was gathered into the new.
- **"So many sheep and oxen that they could not be counted" (v5).** Sacrifices lined the route, as David had sacrificed every six steps when the ark first came to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6:13).
- **"Underneath the wings of the cherubim" (vv6–7).** The two great olive-wood cherubim, ten cubits (15 feet) tall, stood in the inner sanctuary with wings touching wall to wall (6:23–28). The ark, with its own small cherubim on the lid, now sat beneath them. The picture is God enthroned above the cherubim (1 Sam 4:4; Ps 80:1), with the ark as his footstool (1 Chr 28:2).
- **"The ends of the poles were seen from the Holy Place" (v8).** The law said the poles were never to be removed (Exod 25:15). They were pulled forward so that their ends could be seen, or made a bulge in the curtain, from the Holy Place, but not from outside. **"They are there to this day"** shows that the writer's source was written while the temple still stood, before 586 BC.
- **"Nothing in the ark except the two tablets of stone" (v9).** Hebrews 9:4 says the ark also held the jar of manna and Aaron's staff. Exodus says those were placed "before" the testimony (Exod 16:33–34; Num 17:10), so they may have been kept beside it, or they had been lost by Solomon's time, perhaps while the Philistines held the ark (1 Sam 5–6). Either way, what remained at the heart of the temple was the covenant law, God's words written by his own finger at Horeb (Sinai).
- **"A cloud filled the house of the LORD" (v10).** This is the same thing that happened when Moses finished the tabernacle: "the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exod 40:34–35). Moses could not enter then, and the priests **"could not stand to minister"** now (v11). The cloud was God's own seal of approval. Second Chronicles 5:13–14 says it came just as the singers and 120 trumpeters praised God with "For he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever."
- **The glory's later story.** Ezekiel would see this glory leave the temple before the Babylonians destroyed it (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:22–23), and he saw it return in a vision of a new temple (Ezek 43:1–5). John says the Word "became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14), using a verb that means "pitched his tent."
- **Commentators:** Henry: without the ark the temple "was like a body without a soul." He reads the cloud's darkness as a reminder of how dim our sight of God is in this life compared with heaven. JFB and Guzik both suggest the year may have been a jubilee, which the text does not say. JFB, following Hebrews, says the other sacred items were placed "by" the ark, not "in" it. Guzik notes the priests' collapse fits every biblical encounter with God's holiness, from Isaiah (Isa 6:5) to John (Rev 1:17).

#### Solomon Blesses the LORD (vv12–21)
- **"The LORD has said that he would dwell in thick darkness" (v12).** The word is *ʿărāpel*, the "thick darkness" Moses entered on Sinai, "where God was" (Exod 20:21; Deut 5:22). Solomon recognises the cloud. It is the same God who met Moses, and his hiddenness is part of his holiness. No one sees God directly and lives (Exod 33:20).
- **"I have indeed built you an exalted house, a place for you to dwell in forever" (v13).** The word translated "exalted house" (*bêt zĕbul*) means a lofty or princely dwelling, a phrase also found in Canaanite texts for a god's palace. "Forever" was sincere, but the house lasted under four centuries. Verse 27 will correct any idea that God can be boxed in.
- **"The king turned around and blessed all the assembly" (v14).** Solomon had been facing the temple, and now he faces the people. He acts here as Israel's leader in worship, much as David did (2 Sam 6:18).
- **"Who with his hand has fulfilled what he promised with his mouth" (v15).** This phrase frames the speech (repeated in v24). God's mouth and God's hand do not disagree. What he says, he does.
- **"I chose no city… But I chose David" (v16).** The fuller form in 2 Chronicles 6:5–6 says God chose both Jerusalem and David. For centuries the tabernacle moved, at Shiloh, Nob and Gibeon. The choice of one king and one city went together.
- **"You did well that it was in your heart" (v18).** David wanted to build the temple, and God said no (2 Sam 7:1–13). But God did not scold the desire. He honored it and gave the work to David's son. Elsewhere David was told the reason: he was a man of war who had shed much blood (1 Chr 22:8).
- **"The ark, in which is the covenant of the LORD" (v21).** The ark is called by what it holds. The building exists to house the covenant, and the covenant ties Israel back to the Exodus.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads v12 as Solomon reassuring the people, who might have been alarmed by the dark cloud, that it was the sign of approval. Henry observes that God accepted David's good intention though he did not let him carry it out, which Henry applies widely: God values a sincere purpose even when he closes the door. Guzik stresses that Solomon gives the credit to God, not to his father or himself.

#### Solomon's Prayer of Dedication (vv22–53)
- **"Stood before the altar… and spread out his hands toward heaven" (v22).** Open, lifted hands were the common posture of prayer in Israel (Exod 9:29; Ps 28:2). Verse 54 shows he knelt as he went on. Second Chronicles 6:13 adds that he stood on a bronze platform about seven and a half feet square and four and a half feet high, so the crowd could see. He stays in the courtyard, at the altar. As king, not priest, he does not enter the sanctuary.
- **"There is no God like you… keeping covenant and showing steadfast love" (v23).** The prayer opens with who God is before it asks for anything. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. The phrase echoes Deuteronomy 7:9.
- **"Keep for your servant David… what you have promised" (vv25–26).** Solomon prays God's promise back to him (2 Sam 7:12–16). Notice the condition he repeats: **"if only your sons pay close attention to their way."** The promise of an everlasting house was unconditional (2 Sam 7:15–16), but each king's enjoyment of it depended on obedience. The next chapter repeats that warning (9:4–9), and chapter 11 shows Solomon himself failing it.
- **"Heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (v27).** This is the theological heart of the chapter. Ancient temples were seen as a god's actual home, where the god was fed and housed. Solomon builds the grandest building Israel has ever seen and in the same breath says it cannot hold God. The temple is not where God is confined. It is where God has chosen to put his **"name"** (v29), the place he promises to meet his people and hear them. Isaiah later presses the same point (Isa 66:1), and so does Stephen (Acts 7:47–50).
- **"Listen in heaven your dwelling place, and when you hear, forgive" (v30).** This is the refrain of the whole prayer. God hears *from heaven*, and the request Solomon makes most often is not for blessing but for forgiveness. Israel prays "toward this place," and that is where Daniel's habit of praying toward Jerusalem from Babylon comes from (Dan 6:10).
- **Seven cases (vv31–53).** Solomon walks through situations where Israel will need God. Many of them echo the covenant curses of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28. Solomon knows the law and expects the people to need mercy.
  - **A disputed oath (vv31–32).** When there were no witnesses, the accused swore an oath at the altar and left the verdict to God (Exod 22:8–11). Solomon asks God to judge truly, condemning the guilty and clearing the innocent.
  - **Defeat in battle (vv33–34).** Defeat "because they have sinned" is a covenant curse (Deut 28:25). If they "turn again," God should forgive and "bring them again to the land," which hints at captives taken in war.
  - **Drought (vv35–36).** "When heaven is shut up" (Deut 11:17). Note the phrase **"when you teach them the good way."** The trouble is meant to teach, not only punish. Elijah's three-year drought (ch 17–18) will be exactly this case.
  - **Famine, plague and siege (vv37–40).** A long list of disasters, and then a personal note: **"each knowing the affliction of his own heart."** The Hebrew is literally "the plague of his own heart," a striking phrase. Behind each outward disaster is an inward one. **"You, you only, know the hearts"** (v39) means God's forgiveness is never a formality.
  - **The foreigner (vv41–43).** This is one of the most remarkable petitions in the Old Testament. A non-Israelite who hears of God's "great name" and comes to pray is to be heard too, "in order that all the peoples of the earth may know your name." It reaches back to the promise that all nations would be blessed through Abraham (Gen 12:3). Isaiah picks it up: "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isa 56:7), and Jesus quotes that line when he clears the traders out of the court of the Gentiles (Mark 11:17). The Queen of Sheba will be the first such visitor (ch 10).
  - **Battle God sends them to (vv44–45).** **"By whatever way you shall send them"** limits this to wars God has directed. Soldiers far from the temple pray toward it.
  - **Exile (vv46–53).** The longest petition, and the most sobering. **"For there is no one who does not sin"** (v46; compare Eccl 7:20; Rom 3:23). Solomon expects that Israel may one day be carried off. If they "turn their heart" in the land of their captors and confess, "We have sinned and have acted perversely and wickedly" (v47), God should forgive and give them compassion before their captors. The Hebrew for "turn their heart… repent" plays on *shuv*, to turn or return: they turn back in their hearts, and God returns them to the land. Daniel prays exactly this kind of prayer in Babylon (Dan 9:4–19), and Nehemiah too (Neh 1:5–11). The reader of Kings, who was probably living in exile, would have heard this petition as written for them.
- **"Your heritage, which you brought out of Egypt, from the midst of the iron furnace" (vv51, 53).** "Iron furnace" is the smelting furnace image from Deuteronomy 4:20. Solomon's final appeal is not Israel's merit but God's choice: you made them your own, so hear them.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls claiming God's promises in vv25–26 a key to effective prayer, and quotes Spurgeon that God sent the promise on purpose to be used. Henry sees the temple as a type of the gospel church, "a house of prayer for all people," and on v38 writes, "Sin is the plague of our own heart." Henry also stresses that the petitions assume repentance, not just prayer. JFB summarises the prayer as asking deliverance from the curses of the law in seven cases, and makes the point that a king, not a priest, led this consecration. Guzik reads v46 as the Old Testament form of Romans 3:23, and the Tyndale notes connect the exile petition to Daniel praying toward Jerusalem.

#### Solomon's Benediction (vv54–61)
- **"He arose from before the altar… where he had knelt" (v54).** He began standing (v22) and ended on his knees, a picture of the prayer itself.
- **"Not one word has failed of all his good promise" (v56).** Solomon echoes Joshua at the end of the conquest (Josh 21:45; 23:14). The "rest" promised through Moses (Deut 12:9–10) has arrived. It is a high moment, and Israel will never again be this united or this secure. Hebrews 4 later argues that this rest was a foretaste of a deeper rest still to come in Christ.
- **"May he not leave us or forsake us, that he may incline our hearts to him" (vv57–58).** Solomon knows obedience is not natural to the human heart. He asks God to turn their hearts toward himself. That is the same longing that becomes the new covenant promise, where God writes his law on the heart (Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:26–27).
- **"As each day requires" (v59).** Literally "the matter of a day in its day," the same idea as "give us this day our daily bread."
- **"That all the peoples of the earth may know that the LORD is God; there is no other" (v60).** The missionary note sounds again (v43). Israel's blessing was always meant to make God known.
- **"Let your heart therefore be wholly true to the LORD our God" (v61).** "Wholly true" translates *shalem*, whole or complete, related to *shalom* and, by sound, to Solomon's own name, *Shlomo*. The tragedy is that this is the very phrase used against him later: "his heart was not wholly true to the LORD his God" (11:4).
- **Commentators:** JFB treats the benediction as a brief recapitulation of the prayer. Henry reads the dismissal as warm and heartfelt, and draws attention to the prayer that God would incline hearts, since we cannot keep God's law without his grace. Guzik, noting the kneeling, lists other kneelers in Scripture (Ezra, Daniel, Stephen, Jesus in Gethsemane) and concludes that kneeling is not required but fits a humble heart.

#### Solomon's Sacrifices (vv62–66)
- **"22,000 oxen and 120,000 sheep" (v63).** The numbers are enormous, and readers have long wondered how it could be done. These were mostly peace offerings, where most of the meat went back to the worshipers to eat, so this was also a fourteen-day feast for a vast crowd. Thousands of priests and Levites served (1 Chr 23–24). Some scholars think the numbers are rounded or inflated in transmission. Second Chronicles 7:5 gives the same figures.
- **"Consecrated the middle of the court" (v64).** The great bronze altar, 20 cubits square (2 Chr 4:1), was still too small, so the courtyard floor itself was set apart as a temporary altar area.
- **"From Lebo-hamath to the Brook of Egypt" (v65).** These are the ideal borders of the promised land, from the pass toward Hamath in Syria to the Wadi el-Arish in the northern Sinai (Num 34:5, 8). All Israel was there.
- **"Seven days… On the eighth day" (vv65–66).** Second Chronicles 7:8–10 makes the timing clearer: seven days of dedication, then the seven-day Feast of Booths, then a closing assembly, with the people sent home on the twenty-third day of the month. The standard Hebrew text of v65 reads "seven days and seven days, fourteen days." The ESV follows the shorter Greek reading and puts the longer one in a footnote.
- **"Joyful and glad of heart for all the goodness that the LORD had shown to David his servant" (v66).** The chapter ends not with Solomon's glory but with God's goodness to David. The people bless the king and go home happy.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the offerings exceeded the famous "hecatombs" of the pagan world and that most were peace offerings. He takes the dedication and the feast as two separate weeks with the Day of Atonement (on the tenth of the month) falling between, though Chronicles' wording suggests they ran back to back. Guzik notes the meat fed the people, and comments that Israel's joy could have lasted had the king stayed faithful. Henry calls these "Israel's golden days, days of the Son of man in type."

#### Pointing to Christ
Solomon's great question, "Will God indeed dwell on the earth?" (v27), is answered in a way he never imagined. The glory that filled the temple came to dwell in a man: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and Jesus called his own body the temple (John 2:19–21). Every petition of Solomon's prayer asks God to hear and forgive those who pray "toward this place." Now believers pray through Christ, the true meeting place of God and humanity, and foreigners from far countries come to God through him (Eph 2:13–22). Solomon's request that God "incline our hearts to him" (v58) is answered by the Spirit given to all who belong to Christ.
