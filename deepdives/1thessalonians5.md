*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth around AD 50–51 to a young church he had to leave after only a few weeks (Acts 17:1–10). Chapter 4 comforted believers grieving those who had died: when the Lord comes, the dead in Christ will rise and all his people will be gathered to him together. That raises the next question, the one every generation asks: *when?* Chapter 5 answers by refusing to give a date and calling the church to live ready instead. Then, as letters of the time often did, it closes with a string of short instructions, a prayer, and greetings. Many of these one-line commands, such as "Rejoice always" and "pray without ceasing," are among the most quoted verses Paul wrote.

#### The Day of the Lord (vv1–11)
- **"Concerning the times and the seasons" (v1).** "Times" (*chronoi*) is the ordinary word for spans of time. "Seasons" (*kairoi*) means fitting or appointed moments. Jesus used the same pair when the disciples asked if he would now restore the kingdom: "It is not for you to know times or seasons that the Father has fixed" (Acts 1:7). "Now concerning" again marks Paul answering a question the church had raised (4:9, 13).
- **"You have no need to have anything written to you" (v1).** Paul had taught them about this already. What they need is not a calendar but a reminder.
- **"The day of the Lord will come like a thief in the night" (v2).** In the Old Testament "the day of the LORD" is the day God steps in to judge the wicked and rescue his people (Isa 13:6–9; Joel 2:31; Amos 5:18–20; Zeph 1:14–16). The New Testament ties that day to Jesus' return. "Like a thief" is Jesus' own picture (Matt 24:43–44; Luke 12:39–40), and it recurs in 2 Peter 3:10 and Revelation 3:3 and 16:15. A thief does not send notice. The point is not that the day will be stealthy or secret, but that it will be unexpected.
- **"While people are saying, 'There is peace and security'" (v3).** Rome boasted of the *pax Romana*, the peace it had imposed across the Mediterranean, and Roman propaganda used words like "peace" and "security." Many scholars think Paul is echoing that slogan. The Old Testament prophets also condemned false prophets who said "Peace, peace," when there was no peace (Jer 6:14; Ezek 13:10). Human confidence is never so high as just before God's judgment arrives.
- **"As labor pains come upon a pregnant woman" (v3).** The picture holds two truths at once. A pregnant woman knows the birth is coming, but she cannot know the hour, and once labor begins there is no stopping it. Jesus called the troubles before the end "the beginning of the birth pains" (Matt 24:8). "They will not escape" is emphatic in Greek, a double negative: they will by no means escape.
- **"But you are not in darkness, brothers" (v4).** The turn is sharp. The day will surprise the world like a thief, but it need not surprise believers. They don't know the date any more than anyone else does. They are not surprised because they are ready.
- **"Children of light, children of the day" (v5).** "Sons of" is a Hebrew way of saying "belonging to" or "marked by." Believers belong to the coming day, so they already live by its light (Eph 5:8; Rom 13:12). Paul switches from "you" to "we" in the second half of the verse: he includes himself.
- **"Let us not sleep, as others do, but let us keep awake and be sober" (v6).** "Sleep" here is a different word from the one used for death in 4:13–15. In vv6–7 it means moral and spiritual drowsiness, being indifferent to God. "Keep awake" (*grēgoreō*) is Jesus' word in his teaching about his return (Matt 24:42; 25:13) and in Gethsemane (Matt 26:41). "Sober" (*nēphō*) means clear-headed and self-controlled. It does not mean gloomy.
- **"Those who get drunk, are drunk at night" (v7).** It is a plain observation from ordinary life. Night was when people slept and when people partied. Day people live differently.
- **"The breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet the hope of salvation" (v8).** Paul borrows from Isaiah 59:17, where God himself puts on "righteousness as a breastplate, and a helmet of salvation on his head." Later he will expand the picture into the full armor of God (Eph 6:13–17), assigning the pieces differently, so the details are flexible. Here the armor is the same faith, love and hope Paul thanked God for at the letter's start (1:3). Every piece is defensive: a soldier on night watch needs to stay awake and stay protected.
- **"God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain salvation" (v9).** "Wrath" is God's settled judgment against sin, the wrath Jesus "delivers us from" (1:10). Believers will not face it. Christians differ on how this applies to the end times. Pre-tribulation readers take it as a promise that the church will be removed before a period of tribulation. Others take "wrath" as the final judgment itself and note that believers do suffer persecution in this age (3:3–4), but never God's wrath. Either way, the main point is a source of deep assurance: God has set his people's course toward salvation.
- **"Who died for us so that whether we are awake or asleep we might live with him" (v10).** This is one of the earliest written statements that Jesus "died for us" in our place. "Awake or asleep" now means alive or dead, returning to the question of 4:13–18. Whether believers are living or have died when he comes, they will live with him. Death changes nothing about that.
- **"Therefore encourage one another and build one another up, just as you are doing" (v11).** This closes the section just as 4:18 closed the last one. Teaching about the end is meant to strengthen the church now. "Build up" is a building word. Every member has a share in the construction.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Paul turns from the resurrection to "the uselessness of enquiring after the particular time of Christ's coming," and that "The coming of Christ will be a great surprise to men." JFB notes that "the armor here is only defensive," with the breastplate guarding the heart and the helmet the head. On v9 JFB adds: "In the primary sense, God does the work; in the secondary sense, man does it," holding God's appointing and human obtaining together. Guzik reads "the day of the Lord" as a season of God's action rather than a single day, and he takes v9 as support for a pre-tribulation rapture. He also borrows three pictures from Spurgeon of the folly of sleeping: a doctor asleep while holding the cure, a captain asleep while the ship sinks, and a pardon left unopened.

#### Final Instructions and Benediction (vv12–28)
- **"Respect those who labor among you and are over you in the Lord and admonish you" (v12).** "Respect" is literally "know," meaning to recognize and appreciate. These are not three kinds of officers but one group described three ways: they work hard, they lead, and they warn. "Are over you" (*proistamenoi*) can mean both "lead" and "care for." The church was only months old, so these leaders were new believers themselves, and some members may have resented them. Paul does not give them titles. He points to their work.
- **"Esteem them very highly in love because of their work" (v13).** The phrase "very highly" piles up intensifiers in Greek, about as strong as Paul can make it. But the honor is "in love" and "because of their work," not because of rank.
- **"Be at peace among yourselves" (v13).** A church under pressure from outside (2:14) could not afford division inside.
- **"Admonish the idle, encourage the fainthearted, help the weak" (v14).** Pastoral care is not only the leaders' job. "Idle" (*ataktos*) was used of soldiers who broke ranks or left their post, so it means disorderly or undisciplined. In this letter that points to those who had stopped working (4:11; 2 Thess 3:6–11). "Fainthearted" (*oligopsychos*) is literally "small-souled," the timid and easily discouraged, perhaps those grieving their dead or shaken by persecution. "The weak" may be those weak in faith or in resisting temptation. Each needs a different response: a warning, encouragement, a hand to hold onto.
- **"Be patient with them all" (v14).** Different people, different needs, one attitude. Patience is how God treats his people (Rom 2:4).
- **"See that no one repays anyone evil for evil" (v15).** In Greco-Roman culture, paying back an insult was often a matter of honor. Seneca and others treated revenge as restoring one's standing. Jesus taught the opposite (Matt 5:38–48), and Paul repeats it (Rom 12:17). "To everyone" includes outsiders, even the persecutors in Thessalonica.
- **"Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances" (vv16–18).** In Greek v16 is just two words and v17 three, and they belong together. Joy rests on God, not on circumstances, so it can last when circumstances don't. "Without ceasing" (*adialeiptōs*) was used in ordinary Greek of things that keep recurring without a break, like a nagging cough. It does not mean nonstop words but a life where prayer keeps returning and never stops for good. Paul uses the same word of his own prayers for them (1:2–3; 2:13). Thanks is given *in* all circumstances, not necessarily *for* all of them.
- **"For this is the will of God in Christ Jesus for you" (v18).** As in 4:3, Paul names God's will plainly. Joy, prayer and gratitude are not personality traits for a few. They are God's will for every believer, and "in Christ Jesus" means they are possible because of him.
- **"Do not quench the Spirit" (v19).** "Quench" (*sbennymi*) is the word for putting out a fire or a lamp (Matt 12:20; 25:8). The Spirit came as fire at Pentecost (Acts 2:3). The next verse shows what Paul has in mind: in some way the church was in danger of stifling the Spirit's work, especially in prophecy.
- **"Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good" (vv20–21).** Some in Thessalonica may have been burned by bad prophecy. A false word about the end may already have been circulating (2 Thess 2:2). The temptation would be to shut down prophecy altogether. Paul refuses both extremes: don't despise it, and don't swallow it. "Test" (*dokimazō*) is used for testing metals. Prophecy is weighed against the apostles' teaching and the speaker's life (1 Cor 14:29; 1 John 4:1). An early Christian manual, the Didache, says a prophet is known by whether he has "the ways of the Lord." Christians differ over whether the gift of prophecy continues today. Continuationists see this as instruction for churches now. Cessationists see it as a gift tied to the apostolic age, with the testing principle still applying to all teaching. Both agree that everything must be measured by Scripture.
- **"Abstain from every form of evil" (v22).** The KJV's "appearance of evil" has often been taken to mean "avoid anything that might look bad to others." The word *eidos* can mean "appearance," but the lexicon gives "form, sort, kind" for this verse, and the ESV follows that. Paul is saying: whatever shape evil takes, including false prophecy dressed up as spiritual, stay away from it. Wisdom about how things look is real, but it is not this verse's point.
- **"May the God of peace himself sanctify you completely" (v23).** After a long list of commands, Paul turns to God. Holiness is finally God's work. "Completely" (*holotelēs*) joins "whole" and "end": wholly, through and through, reaching its goal. "Whole" (*holoklēros*) means complete in every part, with nothing missing.
- **"Your whole spirit and soul and body" (v23).** Some Christians read this as teaching that human beings have three parts (trichotomy). Others hold that Scripture usually speaks of two, body and soul or spirit, used interchangeably (Matt 10:28), and that Paul is piling up terms to mean the whole person, as Jesus did with "heart… soul… mind… strength" (Mark 12:30). Either way, God's holiness reaches every part of a person, body included. The body is not a shell to be discarded but something God will raise.
- **"He who calls you is faithful; he will surely do it" (v24).** Here is the foundation under every command in the letter. The God who called them will finish the work (Phil 1:6).
- **"Brothers, pray for us" (v25).** The apostle who prays for them constantly (1:2) asks for their prayers too.
- **"Greet all the brothers with a holy kiss" (v26).** A kiss on the cheek was a normal greeting between family members and close friends in that world. "Holy" marks it as a sign that believers are now one family. Paul gives the same instruction in four other letters (Rom 16:16; 1 Cor 16:20; 2 Cor 13:12; 1 Pet 5:14). By the second century it was part of the church's worship before communion. The custom is cultural, while the warmth it expressed is not.
- **"I put you under oath before the Lord to have this letter read to all the brothers" (v27).** This is a strong oath, unusual in Paul's letters. Letters were read aloud to the gathered church, since many could not read. Paul wants no one left out, including any who might have drifted, been idle, or been grieving at home. It is an early sign that his letters were meant to be read in worship alongside Scripture (Col 4:16).
- **"The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you" (v28).** The letter began with grace (1:1) and ends with it. Every Pauline letter closes this way. The KJV ends with an "Amen" and a note that the letter was written from Athens. Neither is in the oldest manuscripts, and the note is mistaken, since Paul wrote from Corinth.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If we pray without ceasing, we shall not want matter for thanksgiving in every thing." He likens the Spirit to fire that can be put out by removing its fuel or pouring water on it. JFB reads v17 as praying "without allowing prayerless gaps to intervene between the times of prayer," and it agrees that "unruly" is "literally said of soldiers who will not remain in their ranks." On v22, JFB argues the verse is not about evil appearances in ourselves but about evil forms in others, such as false prophecy, and Guzik agrees that *eidos* means a visible form of evil, not merely something that looks wrong. Henry, writing from the KJV wording, takes the older line that whoever "is not shy of the appearances of sin" will soon fall into sin. On v23 Guzik leans toward the three-part reading, while JFB stresses that the word means "with none of the integral parts wanting." Guzik also observes that more believers are undone by self-reliance than by Satan, which is why v24 matters.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul never gives a date for the day of the Lord, but he gives something better: a Savior "who died for us so that whether we are awake or asleep we might live with him." The day that brings sudden destruction to those who trust in their own peace brings salvation to those who belong to Christ. And the long list of commands ends not with effort but with a promise: "He who calls you is faithful; he will surely do it."
