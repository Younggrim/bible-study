*Setting:* Israel is back at its base camp at Gilgal near the Jordan (v6), after the victory at Ai and the covenant ceremony at Mount Ebal (ch 8). News of Jericho and Ai has spread through Canaan, and the region's kings respond in two ways. Most decide to fight. One group of towns decides to make peace by trickery. The chapter is the story of a clever deception, a hasty decision made without prayer, and an oath that Israel kept even though it was obtained by a lie. It also sets up chapter 10, where Israel's new treaty partners call for help.

#### The Gibeonite Deception (vv1–27)
- **"All the kings… heard of this" (v1).** The list covers the whole land west of the Jordan: the hill country, the lowland (the *Shephelah*, the rolling foothills between the Judean hills and the coastal plain), and the coast "toward Lebanon." Six of the seven nations of 3:10 are named. City-states that normally competed with each other "gathered together as one" (v2) against a common threat. Their alliance comes into action in chapters 10 and 11.
- **"The inhabitants of Gibeon" (v3).** Gibeon is modern el-Jib, about six miles northwest of Jerusalem and roughly 20 miles uphill from Gilgal. Archaeologists there in the 1950s found jar handles stamped with the name "Gibeon," confirming the site, and a great rock-cut water shaft and pool, perhaps "the pool of Gibeon" of 2 Samuel 2:13. Joshua 10:2 says Gibeon was "a great city, like one of the royal cities," larger than Ai and with strong fighting men. It and three nearby towns (v17) seem to have been governed by elders rather than a king (v11).
- **"They on their part acted with cunning" (v4).** "On their part" (or "they also") may hint that they were copying Israel's own trick of the ambush at Ai. The word for cunning (*'ormah*) is related to the word used of the serpent in Eden, who was "more crafty" (Gen 3:1), though it can also mean prudence in a good sense (Prov 1:4; 8:5).
- **The costume (vv4–5).** Every detail was staged: worn sacks on the donkeys, wineskins "worn-out and torn and mended," patched sandals, worn clothes, and bread "dry and crumbly." Wineskins were goat or sheep skins sewn into bags. Old ones cracked and had to be patched (compare Jesus' saying about new wine in old wineskins, Mark 2:22). The word translated "crumbly" (*niqqudim*) may mean crumbs or spotted with mold. Either way, it looked like bread from a long journey.
- **"We have come from a distant country, so now make a covenant with us" (v6).** The Gibeonites knew Israel's law. Moses had forbidden any treaty with the peoples of Canaan (Exod 23:32; 34:12; Deut 7:2), but had allowed peace terms with cities "very far from you" (Deut 20:10–15). They had done their homework.
- **"Perhaps you live among us" (v7).** The men of Israel were suspicious. The writer calls the visitors "Hivites," one of the peoples on the forbidden list. The Israelites asked the right question and then let the evidence answer it.
- **"We are your servants" (v8).** The language of a weaker party seeking a treaty. Notice they never answer Joshua's direct question, "Who are you? And where do you come from?" They keep talking about where they have been, not where they are from.
- **"Because of the name of the LORD your God" (v9).** Their speech praises Israel's God, and they mention only old news: the plagues in Egypt and the defeat of Sihon and Og east of the Jordan (v10). They carefully say nothing about Jericho or Ai, the recent victories that someone truly from far away would not yet have heard of. It was a clever touch.
- **"Here is our bread. It was still warm" (v12).** The bread, the wineskins and the sandals are offered as proof. The story rests on things that can be seen and touched.
- **"So the men took some of their provisions, but did not ask counsel from the LORD" (v14).** This is the key verse. Israel examined the evidence, perhaps even tasted the bread, and made a judgment by sight. God had given Israel a way to ask him: through the high priest and the Urim and Thummim (Num 27:21). Joshua was explicitly told to seek God's decision that way. Nobody did. It was the same failure as at Ai, where nobody prayed before the first attack (7:2–4).
- **"Joshua made peace with them… and the leaders of the congregation swore to them" (v15).** "Made peace" is *shalom*. "Made a covenant with them, to let them live" means a treaty guaranteeing their lives. The leaders sealed it with an oath in the LORD's name (v18), which was the most binding promise an Israelite could make.
- **"At the end of three days" (v16).** The truth came out quickly. Their towns were only a few days' march away, in the hills just ahead.
- **"Gibeon, Chephirah, Beeroth, and Kiriath-jearim" (v17).** Four towns on the plateau north and west of Jerusalem. Kiriath-jearim, "town of forests," is where the ark would later rest for twenty years before David brought it to Jerusalem (1 Sam 7:1–2; 2 Sam 6:2, where it is called Baale-judah).
- **"All the congregation murmured against the leaders" (v18).** "Murmured" is the word for the wilderness generation's grumbling (Exod 16:2; Num 14:2). The people wanted the treaty broken, perhaps also because they had lost the chance to take the cities' goods.
- **"We have sworn to them by the LORD, the God of Israel, and now we may not touch them" (v19).** The leaders held to their word even though it was obtained by fraud. An oath in God's name involved God's honor. Breaking it would have been taking his name in vain (Exod 20:7), and "lest wrath be upon us" (v20) shows they knew it. Psalm 15:4 praises the one "who swears to his own hurt and does not change."
- **Saul and the Gibeonites.** The oath mattered for centuries. Centuries later King Saul, "in his zeal for the people of Israel and Judah," tried to wipe out the Gibeonites. God sent a three-year famine in David's reign, and when David asked the LORD why, the answer was that Saul had broken the oath (2 Sam 21:1–9). God held Israel to a promise made in his name, even one Israel had been tricked into making.
- **"Cutters of wood and drawers of water" (v21).** Hard, menial work, the lowest jobs in a camp (Deut 29:11). Instead of being killed, the Gibeonites became servants of the whole congregation.
- **"Why did you deceive us?" (v22).** Joshua confronts them. "Now therefore you are cursed" (v23) echoes Noah's curse on Canaan, "a servant of servants shall he be" (Gen 9:25). But the sentence turns: they will serve "the house of my God," the tabernacle, and in v27 "the altar of the LORD."
- **"We feared greatly for our lives" (v24).** The Gibeonites' answer is frank. They believed the LORD had given Israel the land, and they believed he would keep his word. In that sense their reasoning was like Rahab's (2:9–11): fear of the LORD, faith that his word was true, and a decision to side with his people. Rahab hid the spies; the Gibeonites lied about where they lived. The Bible approves neither lie, but both acted on what they believed about Israel's God.
- **"Whatever seems good and right in your sight to do to us, do it" (v25).** They surrender completely, putting themselves in Israel's hands.
- **"He… delivered them out of the hand of the people of Israel" (v26).** Joshua protects them from his own people.
- **"For the altar of the LORD… in the place that he should choose" (v27).** "The place that he should choose" is Deuteronomy's phrase for the future central sanctuary, which became Jerusalem (Deut 12:5). The Gibeonites served at the tabernacle, and later Gibeon itself held the tabernacle and the great high place where Solomon worshiped and God appeared to him (1 Chr 16:39; 1 Kings 3:4–5). Many think the "temple servants" (*Nethinim*, "the given ones") who returned from exile and served at the rebuilt temple were partly descended from the Gibeonites (1 Chr 9:2; Ezra 2:43–58), and men of Gibeon helped rebuild Jerusalem's walls (Neh 3:7). Those who came by deception ended up serving close to God's presence for centuries.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "We make more haste than good speed in any business when we stay not to take God along with us." On keeping the oath: "God is not mocked, and therefore oaths are not to be jested with." He notes that the Gibeonites' curse "is turned into a blessing," since servile work "becomes honourable when it is done for the house of our God," and he sees in their admission to God's house a picture of "the admission of the Gentiles into the gospel church." JFB calls Israel's trust "excessive credulity" and says the leaders should have inquired through the Urim and Thummim. It also wonders whether God would have forbidden sparing Canaanites who renounced idolatry, and links the Gibeonites to the Nethinim. Guzik makes the central lesson plain: the failure was not being fooled, but deciding by what could be seen and touched without asking God. He ties the kept oath to Psalm 15:4 and Saul's later breach in 2 Samuel 21. The Tyndale notes add that the Gibeonites' frank answer in v24 shows real faith in the LORD, and that ancient war records rarely admit a leader's mistakes, while Joshua records two.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Gibeonites came to Israel in rags, claiming to be from far away, and were spared only because Israel's leaders had sworn in the LORD's name. Yet they ended up serving at God's altar. The New Testament says Gentiles really were "far off," strangers to the covenants, "having no hope and without God in the world," and in Christ "you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:12–13). We do not need a disguise to be received. And the promise that keeps us is more secure than any oath of Israel's leaders: God, "desiring to show more convincingly… the unchangeable character of his purpose," bound himself by an oath, so that we might have "a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul" (Heb 6:17–19).
