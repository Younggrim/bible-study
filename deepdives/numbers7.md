*Setting:* With 89 verses, this is the longest chapter in the five books of Moses, and most of it is one paragraph repeated twelve times. It also steps back in time. "The day when Moses had finished setting up the tabernacle" (v1) was the first day of the first month of the second year (Exod 40:17), a month *before* the census of chapter 1. Numbers has arranged its material by theme, not strictly by date. Having described the camp (chs 1–4) and its purity (chs 5–6), it now records how the twelve tribes, through their leaders, gave gifts to equip the tabernacle and dedicate its altar. The list is long on purpose: every tribe gave, and every gift was written down.

#### Offerings at the Tabernacle's Consecration (vv1–89)
- **"Had anointed and consecrated it… and… the altar" (v1).** Exodus 40:9–11 and Leviticus 8:10–11 describe the anointing. The altar is singled out because it was the place of sacrifice, where the people met God through atonement.
- **"The chiefs of Israel… who were over those who were listed" (v2).** These are the same twelve leaders who helped with the census (1:5–16) and who will lead each tribe on the march (10:14–27). The tribes gave through their heads, so the whole nation took part.
- **"Six wagons and twelve oxen" (v3).** Covered carts, one for every two leaders, and an ox from each. Wheeled carts were valuable equipment in the desert, and the Tyndale notes observe that this practical gift came first, before the silver and gold. Nobody had commanded it. It was a voluntary gift, and God told Moses, **"Accept these"** (v5).
- **"To each man according to his service" (vv5–8).** The Gershonites, who carried the fabrics, received **two wagons and four oxen** (v7). The Merarites, who carried the heavy frames, pillars and silver bases, received **four wagons and eight oxen** (v8). The help matched the load.
- **"But to the sons of Kohath he gave none" (v9).** The holy things "had to be carried on the shoulder" (4:15). Centuries later David forgot this and moved the ark on a new cart, copying the Philistines (1 Sam 6:7–8), and Uzzah died when the oxen stumbled (2 Sam 6:3–7). The next time, David admitted, "we did not seek him according to the rule," and the Levites carried it on their shoulders "as Moses had commanded" (1 Chr 15:13–15). The most practical method was not the right one for the holiest things.
- **"One chief each day, for the dedication of the altar" (vv10–11).** The word for "dedication" is *chanukkah*. It is used again for the dedication of Solomon's temple (2 Chr 7:9) and the rebuilt temple (Ezra 6:16–17). The Jewish festival of Hanukkah takes its name from the rededication of the altar in 164 BC (1 Macc 4:52–59), an event Jesus marked when he was in the temple at "the Feast of Dedication" (John 10:22).
- **Twelve days in camp order (vv12–83).** The tribes come in the order of chapter 2's camp, going round from east to south to west to north: **Judah** (Nahshon) first, then Issachar, Zebulun, Reuben, Simeon, Gad, Ephraim, Manasseh, Benjamin, Dan, Asher and Naphtali. Nahshon is the only one not called "chief" of his tribe in his own paragraph (v12), a small variation the rabbis noticed. Gad's leader appears as **"Eliasaph the son of Deuel"** (v42), confirming that "Reuel" in 2:14 is a copying variant.
- **What each tribe brought (vv13–17).** Every leader brought the same:
- **A silver plate of 130 shekels and a silver basin of 70 shekels** (v13), both full of fine flour mixed with oil for a grain offering. A shekel was about 11–12 grams, so the plate weighed about 1.5 kilograms (over 3 pounds) and the basin about 800 grams.
- **A golden dish of 10 shekels, full of incense** (v14), a small pan, about 4 ounces of gold.
- **A burnt offering** of one bull, one ram and one male lamb (v15): dedication of the whole self to God.
- **A sin offering** of one male goat (v16): even a gift-giving leader needed atonement.
- **Peace offerings** of two oxen, five rams, five male goats and five lambs (v17), seventeen animals. The peace offering was mostly eaten by the worshipers in a feast before God, so most of the gift turned into a celebration meal.
- **Why repeat it all twelve times?** Moses could have written one paragraph and added "and so did each of the others." Instead every tribe gets its own day and its own full record. Several things follow. No tribe outgave another, so no leader could boast. No tribe was left out or treated as an afterthought, not the smallest (Manasseh) nor the largest (Judah). And each gift was noticed and recorded by name. Ancient temple records really did list gifts item by item, and the Tyndale notes suggest this chapter may preserve the tabernacle's own ledger. Reading it slowly is like hearing twelve names called one after another at a ceremony.
- **Where did the silver and gold come from?** The Tyndale notes point back to the exodus, when the Egyptians gave Israel silver and gold on their way out (Exod 12:35–36). What God provided in Egypt came back to him in worship.
- **The totals (vv84–88).** Twelve silver plates and twelve basins came to **2,400 shekels** of silver (about 27 kilograms, or 60 pounds), and twelve gold dishes to **120 shekels** of gold (about 1.4 kilograms). The animals: **36** for burnt offerings, **12** for sin offerings, and **204** for peace offerings, **252** in all. Like the census figures, the totals check exactly.
- **"Moses… heard the voice speaking to him from above the mercy seat… from between the two cherubim" (v89).** After all the gifts, God speaks. In Exodus 25:22 God had promised, "There I will meet with you, and from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim… I will speak with you." Now that promise is kept. "Mercy seat" (*kapporet*) is the ark's gold lid, from the root of the word "atone," where the high priest sprinkled blood once a year (Lev 16:14–15). God's voice comes from the place of atonement. Moses heard a voice and saw no form, as Israel at Sinai "heard the sound of words, but saw no form" (Deut 4:12). The form of the verb in Hebrew is unusual, and Jewish interpreters suggested it means the voice was speaking "within itself" and Moses overheard it, though most translations take it simply as "speaking."
- **Why end the chapter with this verse?** The gifts are offered, the altar is dedicated, and God answers by speaking. Henry's outline makes the same connection: first the presents, then God's sign that he accepts them. The tabernacle is now not only furnished but working. It is the place where the God of Israel talks with his servant, and the commands of the next chapters come from there (8:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures the leaders coming "as tenants to their landlord," now that God has "set up house (as it were) in the midst of the camp of Israel," and he reads v89 as the way "God graciously signified his acceptance of them." JFB observes that the Kohathites' holy load was "carried by those Levites shoulder high," and that the offerings went round the camp "beginning at the east, proceeding to the south, then to the west, and closing with the north." It takes "day" in v1 loosely, since the presentation took many days. Guzik gives several reasons for the repetition: each tribe was honored on its own day, every gift mattered and was recorded, and identical gifts kept anyone from giving to be admired. He sees a people moving from a slave's habit of grabbing to the habit of giving that trusts God to provide, and he points out that v89 is one of the plainest statements that Moses heard an actual voice.

#### Pointing to Christ
Twelve times the same gift is laid before the same altar, and the chapter ends with God speaking from above the mercy seat. The New Testament uses that same word for Jesus: God "put forward" Christ "as a propitiation by his blood" (Rom 3:25), the Greek word used in Hebrews 9:5 for the mercy seat itself. In Christ, God's place of meeting and his place of atonement are one person. And where Moses heard a voice from between the cherubim, "in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:2). The long ledger of gifts is a reminder too that nothing given to him is forgotten, from the leaders' silver plates to a widow's two small coins (Mark 12:41–44).
