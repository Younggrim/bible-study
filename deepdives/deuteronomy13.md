*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life. Moses has begun the detailed laws of his second sermon (chapters 12–26), and he is applying the first two commandments: worship the LORD alone, in the way he chooses. Chapter 12 dealt with the danger from outside, the shrines and customs of the Canaanites. Chapter 13 deals with a more dangerous threat: the pull toward other gods from *inside* Israel. Moses describes three cases, each more personal or widespread than the last: a prophet who performs a real sign, a close family member who whispers in private, and a whole town that turns. In each case the answer is the same. Loyalty to the LORD comes before signs, before family, and before community. The ESV keeps the heading from 12:29, "Warning Against Idolatry," over the whole chapter. (In Hebrew Bibles, the last verse of chapter 12 is counted as 13:1, so verse numbers here run one behind the Hebrew.)

#### Warning Against Idolatry (vv1–18)
- **"If a prophet or a dreamer of dreams arises among you" (v1).** God really did speak through prophets and through dreams (Num 12:6; Gen 37; 1 Kgs 3:5). That is what makes this case dangerous. The person looks like a genuine messenger. Chapter 18 will promise that God will raise up a prophet like Moses and give a test for false ones (18:15–22).
- **"And the sign or wonder that he tells you comes to pass" (v2).** This is remarkable: the sign actually happens. Egypt's magicians copied some of Moses' signs (Exod 7:11, 22). Jesus warned that "false christs and false prophets will arise and perform great signs and wonders, so as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect" (Matt 24:24), and Paul speaks of "false signs and wonders" (2 Thess 2:9). A miracle alone does not prove a message comes from God.
- **"Let us go after other gods… which you have not known" (v2).** This is the real test: the content of the message. Any message that leads away from the God who redeemed Israel is false, whatever wonders come with it. "Which you have not known" means gods Israel had no history with. The LORD had proved himself in Egypt and the wilderness, and these gods had done nothing.
- **"For the LORD your God is testing you, to know whether you love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul" (v3).** The language is the Shema's (6:5). God permits the false sign as a test, the same way he tested Israel in the wilderness "to know what was in your heart" (8:2). The question is not whether they can be impressed, but whom they love.
- **"Walk after… fear… keep… obey… serve… hold fast" (v4).** Six verbs in one verse, a full description of loyalty. In Hebrew the sentence puts "the LORD your God" first for emphasis: *him* you shall follow, *him* you shall fear. "Hold fast" (*davaq*) is the clinging word of marriage (Gen 2:24).
- **"That prophet… shall be put to death, because he has taught rebellion" (v5).** "Rebellion" (*sarah*) means turning aside, apostasy. In Israel, where the LORD was both God and King, leading people to other gods was treason against the covenant. The Tyndale notes observe that other ancient religions didn't demand exclusive worship, so penalties like this were unknown elsewhere.
- **"So you shall purge the evil from your midst" (v5).** This phrase is a refrain in Deuteronomy, used for several of the most serious crimes (17:7; 19:19; 21:21; 22:21–24; 24:7). "Purge" (*ba'ar*) is literally "burn out." Paul quotes the formula when he tells the Corinthian church to remove an unrepentant man from fellowship: "Purge the evil person from among you" (1 Cor 5:13). The church applies it through discipline, not execution.
- **"Your brother, the son of your mother, or your son or your daughter or the wife you embrace or your friend who is as your own soul" (v6).** The list moves closer and closer. "The son of your mother" is the closest kind of brother, in a world where a man might have several wives. "The wife you embrace" is literally "the wife of your bosom." "Your friend who is as your own soul" is how Jonathan loved David (1 Sam 18:1). These are the people whose voice carries the most weight.
- **"Entices you secretly" (v6).** Unlike the prophet, this temptation happens in private. "Entices" (*sut*) means to lure or incite, the same verb used when Jezebel "incited" Ahab (1 Kgs 21:25). The pull toward other gods often comes not from strangers but from those we love.
- **"Whether near you or far off from you, from the one end of the earth to the other" (v7).** Whether a local Canaanite god or an impressive foreign one, it makes no difference.
- **"You shall not yield to him or listen to him, nor shall your eye pity him, nor shall you spare him, nor shall you conceal him" (v8).** Five negatives in a row. Love for a family member must not become a cover for treason against God.
- **"Your hand shall be first against him… and afterward the hand of all the people" (v9).** This was not a private killing. Deuteronomy requires at least two witnesses for any death sentence (17:6–7), and the witnesses throw the first stones. The person who heard the enticement must testify and carry the weight of it. JFB rightly stresses that this followed "trial and conviction." It was never a license for family vengeance.
- **"Because he sought to draw you away from the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (v10).** The reason is always tied to the Exodus (vv5, 10). The God being abandoned is the God who set them free.
- **"All Israel shall hear and fear and never again do any such wickedness" (v11).** The punishment had a public purpose: to deter. Deuteronomy repeats this phrase for several capital crimes (17:13; 19:20; 21:21).
- **How Christians read this today.** These were laws for Israel as a nation under God's direct rule, where religion and state were one. The New Testament does not give the church or the state this sword. Jesus told Peter to put away his sword (Matt 26:52), and Paul says God judges those outside the church (1 Cor 5:12–13). The church has sometimes ignored this, most notoriously when Michael Servetus was burned in Geneva in 1553 and when heretics were handed over for execution in medieval Europe. Christians across traditions now generally recognize those as grievous wrongs. What carries over is the priority this chapter sets. Jesus said, "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (Matt 10:37), and he warned against false teachers who come in sheep's clothing (Matt 7:15). The New Testament response is to test teaching, refuse it, and withdraw fellowship from those who promote it (Gal 1:8–9; 2 John 10–11).
- **"Certain worthless fellows" (v13).** Literally "sons of *beliyya'al*," older translations' "children of Belial." The word means worthlessness, literally "without profit." It describes the sons of Eli (1 Sam 2:12), the men of Gibeah (Judg 19:22) and the false witnesses against Naboth (1 Kgs 21:10). By New Testament times "Belial" had become a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15).
- **"Then you shall inquire and make search and ask diligently" (v14).** Three verbs for investigation. A rumor is not enough. The charge must be "true and certain." Israel later nearly went to war over a misunderstanding about an altar by the Jordan, and careful inquiry prevented it (Josh 22:10–34).
- **"Devoting it to destruction… as a whole burnt offering to the LORD" (vv15–16).** If an Israelite town went over to idols, it was to be treated as if it were a Canaanite city under the ban (*cherem*, 7:2). Being an Israelite town gave no protection. All the plunder was burned in the town square, so no one could profit. The phrase "whole burnt offering" (*kalil*) means everything was given up to God.
- **"It shall be a heap forever. It shall not be built again" (v16).** "Heap" is *tel*, the word for a mound of ruins. Across the Middle East today, ancient towns survive as *tels*, hills built up from layer on layer of ruined cities. A town under this judgment would stay a ruin as a warning. Jericho received a similar curse (Josh 6:26; 1 Kgs 16:34).
- **"None of the devoted things shall stick to your hand" (v17).** This prevented a "holy war" from turning into an excuse for plunder. No one could accuse a neighboring town to grab its wealth. It recalls Achan, whose theft of devoted things brought disaster (Josh 7).
- **"That the LORD may turn from the fierceness of his anger and show you mercy" (v17).** The whole nation's standing with God was at stake. The command to destroy ends in a promise of mercy and compassion, and of God keeping his oath to the fathers.
- **Did Israel ever do this?** There is no clear record of an Israelite town being destroyed under this law. The nearest example is the war against Gibeah and Benjamin after a terrible crime (Judg 19–20), which was not about idolatry. Later, whole regions turned to Baal under Ahab and to idols under Manasseh, and the law was not enforced. In the end God himself carried out the judgment through Assyria and Babylon (2 Kgs 17:7–18).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry answers the puzzle of a true sign: God "suffers you to be set upon by such a temptation to try your constancy," and "no evidence can be admitted against so clear a truth as that of the unity of the Godhead." On the enticing relative: "Satan tempted Adam by Eve and Christ by Peter." And: "Those are certainly our worst enemies that would thrust us from God, our best friend." JFB stresses that the accusation "must be lodged before a magistrate" and that execution was never to be done "hastily, or in a private manner." It also argues that Jewish leaders who used this passage against Jesus misapplied it, since he did not lead anyone to other gods. Guzik says a sign coming true proves nothing unless the message matches God's revealed word. He states plainly that the new covenant gives no command to execute false teachers and lists the New Testament alternative: mark them and avoid them (Rom 16:17). He sums up the chapter's four seductions, borrowing from G. Campbell Morgan: curiosity, signs and wonders, family affection, and failure to confront error. The BDB lexicon supports the reading of *beliyya'al* as "worthlessness" that all three commentators give.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus was accused under this very kind of law. His enemies said he cast out demons by Beelzebul (Mark 3:22) and that he was leading the people astray (John 7:12). But Deuteronomy 13's test is whether a prophet leads people to "other gods," and Jesus led people to the God of Abraham, the God who brought Israel out of Egypt. "I have come in my Father's name," he said (John 5:43). He fulfilled the chapter's demand himself: he loved the LORD with all his heart and refused every tempter, even when the temptation came through his closest friend: "Get behind me, Satan!" (Matt 16:23). And the judgment that this chapter says must fall on rebellion fell on him, outside the city, so that those who once followed other gods could be forgiven and brought home.
