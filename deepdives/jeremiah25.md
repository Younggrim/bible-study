*Setting:* 605 BC, "the fourth year of Jehoiakim… the first year of Nebuchadnezzar." This is one of the turning points of ancient history. That spring the Babylonian crown prince Nebuchadnezzar crushed Pharaoh Neco's army at Carchemish on the Euphrates (46:2). Egypt's hold on Syria and Judah was broken. Weeks later Nebuchadnezzar's father died, and he hurried home to take the throne. In that same year the first Judean hostages were taken to Babylon, Daniel among them (Dan 1:1–6), and Jeremiah dictated his collected sermons to Baruch (36:1–2). This chapter comes from that moment. Jeremiah looks back on twenty-three years of ignored warnings and names, for the first time, how long the exile will last: seventy years. Then he widens the frame to the whole known world. Every nation will drink from the cup of God's wrath, and last of all Babylon itself. In the Greek Old Testament this chapter is followed directly by the oracles against the nations (chs 46–51 in our Bibles), and many think it once introduced them.

#### Seventy Years of Captivity (vv1–14)
- **"In the fourth year of Jehoiakim" (v1).** Daniel 1:1 calls it "the third year." Judah and Babylon counted reigns differently. Babylon did not count a king's first partial year, while Judah did. Both dates point to 605 BC. This is the first time in Jeremiah that a date is linked to a Babylonian king's reign, a sign of who now ruled the region.
- **"For twenty-three years, from the thirteenth year of Josiah" (v3).** That was 627 BC, when Jeremiah was called as a young man (1:2). For twenty-three years he had seen no visible success. **"I have spoken persistently."** Literally "rising early and speaking." The same idiom is used of God, who "persistently sent… his servants the prophets" (v4; 7:13, 25). God's patience had been active and constant. It was not passive.
- **"Turn now… and dwell upon the land" (v5).** In Hebrew there is a wordplay between *shubu*, "turn," and *shebu*, "dwell." Turn and you will stay. The land was promised "from of old and forever," but staying in it depended on loyalty to its Giver (Deut 4:25–27).
- **"The work of your hands" (vv6–7).** Idols, made by human hands and then worshiped. **"To your own harm" (v7).** Idolatry did not hurt God. It hurt them.
- **"Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant" (v9).** One of the most startling phrases in the book, repeated in 27:6 and 43:10. A pagan emperor who worshiped Marduk is called the LORD's "servant," the same title given to Moses and David. He did not know it, but he was carrying out God's sentence, as Assyria had been "the rod of my anger" (Isa 10:5) and Cyrus would be God's "anointed" (Isa 45:1). The Greek version leaves out "my servant" here, perhaps because a translator found it too much.
- **"I will devote them to destruction" (v9).** The verb is *charam*. It is the word used for the "devoted things" of the conquest, the Canaanite cities given over to God's judgment (Josh 6:17–21). The judgment once carried out *by* Israel would now be carried out *on* Israel.
- **"The voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride, the grinding of the millstones and the light of the lamp" (v10).** These are the everyday sounds and sights of a living town: weddings, women grinding grain at dawn, lamps glowing in houses at night. All of them would stop (7:34; 16:9). Revelation 18:22–23 uses these same pictures for the fall of "Babylon the great."
- **"These nations shall serve the king of Babylon seventy years" (v11).** This is the first time the length of the exile is named. Jeremiah repeats it in his letter to the exiles (29:10). Later Scripture comes back to it again and again. Second Chronicles 36:21 links it to the sabbath years the land had missed (Lev 26:34–35). Daniel read it in "the books" and prayed (Dan 9:2). Zechariah mentions it (Zech 1:12; 7:5).
- **How long were the seventy years?** Christians hold several views. 1) From 605 BC, the first deportation, to 538–536 BC, when the first exiles returned under Cyrus, about seventy years. 2) From 586 BC, when the temple was destroyed, to 516 BC, when the second temple was finished, almost exactly seventy years. 3) A round number for a full lifetime (Ps 90:10), meaning no one who went into exile as an adult would come back. Isaiah gives Tyre "seventy years, like the days of one king" (Isa 23:15). Interestingly, the Assyrian king Esarhaddon wrote that the god Marduk had decreed seventy years of desolation for Babylon, so the number had a known meaning as a period of divine judgment. The views are not mutually exclusive. The period was real and roughly seventy years, and its number carried meaning too.
- **"Then after seventy years are completed, I will punish the king of Babylon" (v12).** God's servant was still accountable. Babylon would be judged "for their iniquity." Using a nation to carry out God's purpose did not excuse its cruelty (Isa 47:6; Hab 2:6–17). Babylon fell to Cyrus of Persia in October 539 BC, about sixty-six years after this word. **"An everlasting waste."** The fall came in stages. Babylon declined slowly over the following centuries, and by the early centuries AD it was largely abandoned. Today it is a ruin in Iraq.
- **"Everything written in this book, which Jeremiah prophesied against all the nations" (v13).** This line refers to a written collection of oracles against the nations. In our Bibles those are chapters 46–51. In the Greek version they come right here, after 25:13. The Greek text of Jeremiah is about one-eighth shorter than the Hebrew and arranged differently. Most scholars think two editions of the book circulated, both reflecting Jeremiah's work. Fragments of both forms have been found among the Dead Sea Scrolls.
- **"Many nations and great kings shall make slaves even of them" (v14).** The enslaver would be enslaved. The Medes and Persians, and later the Greeks and Parthians, ruled Babylon in turn.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "God keeps an account, whether we do or no, how long we have enjoyed the means of grace," and that "wrath comes without remedy against those only that sin without repentance." JFB explains the "third" and "fourth" years by a difference in how years were counted, and links the seventy years to the land's missed sabbaths. He also catches the wordplay of v5. Guzik, drawing on Feinberg, stresses that Nebuchadnezzar served God's plan without knowing it. He points out that the Greek version drops "my servant," and cites Thompson's view that a translator was uncomfortable with the title. He presents both main views of the seventy years: Harrison's round-number view, and Feinberg's precise view based on Daniel 9.

#### The Cup of the Lord's Wrath (vv15–38)
- **"Take from my hand this cup of the wine of wrath" (v15).** A cup in Scripture often stands for one's portion, what God hands a person to receive. It can be blessing (Ps 16:5; 23:5) or judgment (Ps 75:8; Isa 51:17, 22; Hab 2:16; Ezek 23:31–34). Here it is wine that makes the drinker reel and lose control. In the New Testament it becomes "the wine of God's wrath, poured full strength into the cup of his anger" (Rev 14:10).
- **"They shall drink and stagger and be crazed because of the sword" (v16).** The cup is explained: it is war. Nations under attack will act like drunks, disoriented and unable to defend themselves.
- **"So I took the cup from the LORD's hand, and made all the nations… drink it" (v17).** Jeremiah could not visit every capital. Most think this was a vision or a symbolic act, carried out by preaching. Some suggest he passed his message to foreign envoys in Jerusalem, as he does in 27:2–3.
- **"Jerusalem and the cities of Judah" first (v18).** The list starts at home. **"As at this day."** These words were probably added when the book was compiled after 586 BC, when the ruin was plain to see.
- **The order of the list (vv18–26).** It sweeps around the map. It starts with Jerusalem, goes south to Egypt, then west to the Philistines, east to Edom, Moab and Ammon, northwest to Tyre and Sidon, out into the Arabian desert, then far east to Elam and Media, then "all the kings of the north," and last of all to Babylon itself. Most of these nations have their own oracles in chapters 46–49.
- **"Pharaoh king of Egypt" (v19).** Placed right after Judah, because Judah trusted Egypt more than any other ally. **"The mixed tribes" (v20).** Probably foreign settlers and mercenaries living in Egypt.
- **"The land of Uz" (v20).** Job's homeland (Job 1:1). Lamentations 4:21 links Uz with Edom, so it was probably east or southeast of the Dead Sea.
- **"Ashkelon, Gaza, Ekron, and the remnant of Ashdod" (v20).** Four of the five Philistine cities. Gath, the fifth, had already disappeared. Ashdod is only a "remnant" because, according to the Greek historian Herodotus, Pharaoh Psammetichus had besieged it for twenty-nine years.
- **"Edom, Moab, and the sons of Ammon" (v21).** Israel's relatives: Edom descended from Esau, and Moab and Ammon from Lot. They were neighbors and frequent enemies.
- **"Tyre… Sidon, and the kings of the coastland across the sea" (v22).** The Phoenician trading cities and their colonies across the Mediterranean, probably Cyprus and beyond.
- **"Dedan, Tema, Buz, and all who cut the corners of their hair" (v23).** Arabian oasis tribes along the caravan routes. Buz was a relative of Abraham (Gen 22:21), and Elihu, Job's young friend, was a Buzite (Job 32:2). Cutting the hair at the temples was an Arab custom, forbidden to Israel (Lev 19:27; Jer 9:26).
- **"Zimri… Elam… Media" (v25).** Zimri is unknown, possibly a scribal form of another name. Elam lay east of Babylon in what is now southwest Iran, with Susa as its capital. Media lay to the north of it. These were the very peoples whose descendants would later help conquer Babylon (51:11, 28).
- **"And after them the king of Babylon shall drink" (v26).** In Hebrew the name here is "Sheshach." Most agree it is a code for Babylon, formed by a simple letter-swap cipher called *atbash*: the first letter of the alphabet is swapped with the last, the second with the second-to-last, and so on. So *b-b-l* (Babel) becomes *sh-sh-k*. Jeremiah 51:41 sets "Sheshach" in parallel with "Babylon," which confirms it. The ESV puts "the king of Babylon" in the text and the Hebrew in a footnote. Why use a code is uncertain. It may have been caution, or a kind of mocking wordplay. The point is plain either way: the cupbearer drinks last.
- **"Drink, be drunk and vomit, fall and rise no more" (v27).** The scene is ugly on purpose. Judgment stripped nations of their dignity.
- **"If they refuse to accept the cup… You must drink!" (v28).** No nation can opt out. **"I begin to work disaster at the city that is called by my name, and shall you go unpunished?" (v29).** If God did not spare Jerusalem, where his name dwelt, the nations should not expect to be spared. Peter draws the same principle: "it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God; and if it begins with us, what will be the outcome for those who do not obey the gospel?" (1 Pet 4:17).
- **"The LORD will roar from on high" (v30).** Amos opens the same way (Amos 1:2; Joel 3:16). The lion's roar comes just before it pounces. **"Against his fold."** First against his own people's land. **"Shout, like those who tread grapes."** In the harvest, people treading grapes shouted in rhythm (48:33). Isaiah 63:1–6 and Revelation 14:19–20 and 19:15 picture God's judgment as treading a winepress.
- **"The LORD has an indictment against the nations" (v31).** The word is *rib*, a lawsuit. God is bringing a legal case before "all flesh." Judgment is not blind rage. It is a verdict.
- **"A great tempest is stirring from the farthest parts of the earth" (v32).** Disaster moves "from nation to nation," like a storm system. The sixth century BC saw the Babylonian, Median and Persian conquests sweep across the region one after another.
- **"They shall not be lamented, or gathered, or buried; they shall be dung on the surface of the ground" (v33).** This is the curse Jeremiah had spoken over Judah (8:2; 16:4), now on a world scale.
- **"Wail, you shepherds… you lords of the flock" (v34).** The "shepherds" are kings and rulers of all these nations, and "lords of the flock" means the strongest rams, the leaders. **"You shall fall like a choice vessel."** A precious jar, dropped and shattered. The Greek reads "like choice rams," which would continue the flock picture. Either way, the fall is sudden and complete.
- **"No refuge will remain for the shepherds" (v35).** The rulers who sent others to fight cannot escape themselves.
- **"The peaceful folds are devastated" (v37).** Pastures that once felt safe are emptied.
- **"Like a lion he has left his lair" (v38).** In 4:7 the lion was Babylon. Here the lion is the LORD himself. **"The sword of the oppressor."** The ESV follows some Hebrew manuscripts and the Greek. The traditional Hebrew text has "the wrath of the oppressor." The same phrase "sword of the oppressor" appears in 46:16 and 50:16.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "the wrath of God in this world is but as a cup, in comparison of the full streams of it in the other world," and adds a warning: "when our neighbour's house is on fire it is time to be concerned for our own." JFB begins the list where judgment always begins, at "the house of God," and says Sheshach is Babylon, as "the parallelism in Jer 51:41 proves," formed by the *atbash* cipher. He reads v37's "peaceable habitations" as a pun on Salem (Jerusalem). That is possible, but the passage is about all nations. Guzik connects the cup to Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane, where Jesus faced this very cup of judgment in place of others. He also cites Kidner that the list covers the whole Old Testament world and then looks beyond it. Guzik reads the scene as a pointer to the final judgment of the nations.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every nation in this chapter is handed a cup it cannot refuse, starting with the city called by God's name. In Gethsemane, Jesus fell on his face before that same cup: "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). He drank it, and on the cross he took the wrath that the nations had earned. That is why Jesus can offer another cup, "the cup of blessing" (1 Cor 10:16), "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). The world's rulers, the shepherds of this chapter, wail when the lion leaves his lair. But the Lion of the tribe of Judah is also the Lamb who was slain, and he ransoms people "from every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:5–9), including Egypt, Edom, Elam and Babylon.
