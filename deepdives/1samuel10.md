*Setting:* Around 1050 BC, the morning after the feast in chapter 9. Saul, a tall young Benjaminite, set out to find his father's lost donkeys and ended up at Samuel's table in the seat of honor. At dawn, at the edge of town, Samuel sent Saul's servant ahead so he could give Saul "the word of God" in private (9:27). Chapter 10 tells how Saul became king in two stages. First comes a private anointing, confirmed by three signs, ending with the Spirit of God coming on him. Then comes a public choosing by lot before all Israel at Mizpah. At each stage there are hints of the man Saul will turn out to be.

#### Saul Anointed King (vv1–16)
- **"Samuel took a flask of oil and poured it on his head and kissed him" (v1).** Anointing with oil set a person apart for God's service. Priests were anointed (Exod 29:7; Lev 8:12), and now a king is. The Hebrew verb is *mashach*, which gives us "messiah," the "anointed one." The oil was a visible sign of God's choosing and of the Spirit's equipping that would follow (v6; compare 16:13). The kiss was a gesture of honor and loyalty (Ps 2:12). Samuel, who had objected to a king, gives the man God chose his full support.
- **"Has not the LORD anointed you to be prince over his people Israel?" (v1).** Again the word is *nagid*, "leader" or "designated ruler" (9:16), not "king." The people are "his people" and "his heritage." Saul is a steward of a people who belong to God. The ESV here follows a longer text found in the Greek translation and the Latin, which includes "you shall reign over the people of the LORD and you will save them from the hand of their surrounding enemies." The traditional Hebrew text is shorter, probably because a copyist's eye skipped from one "the LORD has anointed you" to the next.
- **The first sign: two men at Rachel's tomb (v2).** Rachel was buried "on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)" (Gen 35:19), but this verse places her tomb on the border of Benjamin, her son's tribe, north of Jerusalem (compare Jer 31:15, "Rachel weeping" at Ramah). Zelzah is unknown. The men will report that the donkeys are found, and that Kish is now worried about his son. That small detail shows Samuel's knowledge reaches all the way to a father's words at home.
- **The second sign: three men going up to Bethel (vv3–4).** They carry three young goats, three loaves and a skin of wine, offerings for worship at Bethel, where God met Jacob (Gen 28). They will give Saul two loaves. Bread meant for God's altar is handed to God's anointed. It is like a first gift of tribute to a new ruler, and Saul is to "accept" it.
- **The third sign: prophets at Gibeath-elohim (v5).** "Gibeath-elohim" means "the hill of God." It is probably Saul's own hometown of Gibeah or the nearby town of Geba, and there was a Philistine garrison there. Israel's new leader is anointed right under the enemy's nose. He will meet "a group of prophets," literally "a band" or "string" of prophets, coming down from worship with music. Music often went with prophecy and helped stir it (2 Kgs 3:15; 1 Chr 25:1).
- **"Then the Spirit of the LORD will rush upon you, and you will prophesy" (v6).** "Rush upon" (*tsalach*) is the same verb used when the Spirit came on Samson to tear apart a lion or strike down the Philistines (Judg 14:6, 19; 15:14). In the Old Testament, the Spirit came on leaders to empower them for a task. "Prophesy" here probably means ecstatic praise, inspired speech or song, more than predicting the future. "And be turned into another man." The shy farmer will be changed into someone fit to lead.
- **"Do what your hand finds to do, for God is with you" (v7).** Once the signs have come, Saul is to act. The garrison of v5 hangs over this verse. Many think Samuel is hinting that Saul's first task should be to deal with the Philistine outpost. Saul does nothing about it. His son Jonathan will be the one who strikes it (13:3).
- **"Go down before me to Gilgal… Seven days you shall wait, until I come" (v8).** This is a standing instruction: when the time comes to act, Saul is to wait for the prophet at Gilgal, and Samuel will offer sacrifices and tell him what to do. The king is under the word of God through the prophet. In chapter 13 Saul will fail exactly this test, offering the sacrifice himself when Samuel is late, and it will cost him his dynasty (13:8–14).
- **"God gave him another heart" (v9).** Literally, "God changed for him another heart." Does this mean Saul was born again in the New Testament sense? Interpreters disagree. Many think it means God gave him a new outlook and courage for the task of kingship, not necessarily saving faith. Saul's later life of jealousy, disobedience and turning to a medium (ch 28) makes many doubt that his heart was ever truly turned to God. Others think he began well and fell away. The text says only that God changed him for the work. Gifts and a dramatic experience of the Spirit are not the same as lasting faithfulness.
- **"Is Saul also among the prophets?" (vv10–11).** The people who knew him were amazed. Saul had shown no sign of religious zeal. "What has come over the son of Kish?" A local man answers, "And who is their father?" (v12). Prophets did not inherit their gift from their fathers, as priests and kings did. The question means "Where does any of them get it from?" The answer is from God. So why not Saul? The Greek translation reads "Who is *his* father?", a sneer at Saul's ordinary family. The saying became a proverb for someone suddenly found in unexpected company. Chapter 19 gives a second, darker story behind the same proverb (19:23–24).
- **"He came to the high place" (v13).** The Greek translation reads "to the hill," and many scholars think the original was "he came to Gibeah," which would lead naturally into the next verse, where he meets his uncle at home.
- **Saul's uncle (vv14–16).** He may be Ner, the father of Abner, Saul's future general (14:50). He wants to know what Samuel said. Saul tells him only about the donkeys. "About the matter of the kingdom… he did not tell him anything." This may be wise discretion until God made the choice public, or modesty, or fear. It fits the reluctance Saul shows in the next scene.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "it was only a vial of oil" Samuel used, a small flask for a modest beginning, and that by the Spirit Saul "should be turned thereby into another man." On v8 he says Saul "must wait for Samuel to come to him, and must tarry seven days." Guzik explains that *mashach* means to smear or rub with oil, and connects the anointing to the Messiah and to the anointing all believers receive (1 John 2:20). He describes the "other heart" as a Spirit-given change for service, and reads "who is their father?" as meaning God is the source of every prophet's gift. JFB says Saul's "reluctance to undertake the onerous office was overcome" by the signs. He mentions the Greek reading "Who is his father?" and notes that the first two signs are reported only briefly.

#### Saul Proclaimed King (vv17–27)
- **"Samuel called the people together to the LORD at Mizpah" (v17).** Mizpah was where Israel repented and God thundered against the Philistines (7:5–11). It was also where the tribes once gathered to make war on Benjamin (Judg 20:1). Now a Benjaminite will be made king there.
- **"But today you have rejected your God, who saves you" (vv18–19).** Before the lots are cast, Samuel delivers God's verdict on the request one more time. God brought them out of Egypt and saved them from every oppressor. Their demand for a king is a rejection of their Savior. The choosing goes ahead under that shadow.
- **"Present yourselves before the LORD by your tribes and by your thousands" (v19).** "Thousands" here means clans. The lot was cast in stages, probably using the Urim and Thummim, the sacred lots kept by the priest (14:41), or some similar method. "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33). The process, narrowing from tribe to clan to household to man, is exactly how Achan was found out in Joshua 7:16–18. It is an uncomfortable echo: the same method that exposed a guilty man now picks a king.
- **"Saul the son of Kish was taken by lot. But when they sought him, he could not be found" (v21).** God's choice confirms what Samuel had already done privately. But the chosen man is missing.
- **"Behold, he has hidden himself among the baggage" (v22).** They have to ask God again where he is. The "baggage" (*kelim*) means the gear and supplies of the assembled crowd. The future king is hiding behind the luggage. Is this humility, or fear? Some read it as becoming modesty in a man who did not seek power. Others see the first sign of the insecurity that will mark his reign, a man who feared the people (15:24). Both may be true.
- **"He was taller than any of the people from his shoulders upward" (v23).** Once pulled out, Saul literally stands head and shoulders above everyone. He looks like a king.
- **"Do you see him whom the LORD has chosen? There is none like him among all the people" (v24).** Samuel says the LORD chose him, and that is true. But "none like him" in height is exactly what the people wanted, a king "like all the nations." Some readers hear a note of irony in Samuel's words, though the text does not say so. The crowd shouts, "Long live the king!" (*yechi hammelek*). It is the first time the cry is heard in Israel.
- **"Samuel told the people the rights and duties of the kingship, and he wrote them in a book" (v25).** "Rights and duties" is *mishpat*, the same word as "the ways of the king" in 8:9–11. There Samuel warned what a king would take. Here he writes down what a king is allowed to do and what he owes, a kind of constitution. It probably drew on Deuteronomy 17:14–20, which limits the king and puts him under God's law. Samuel "laid it up before the LORD," at the sanctuary, as Joshua had done with his covenant (Josh 24:26). The king would be accountable to God's written word. The document itself has not survived.
- **"Men of valor whose hearts God had touched" (v26).** God gives Saul a loyal core of supporters. "Men of valor" is probably the better reading, following the Greek; the Hebrew has simply "the army" or "the valiant."
- **"Some worthless fellows said, 'How can this man save us?'" (v27).** "Worthless fellows" is "sons of Belial," the phrase used of Eli's sons (2:12). They refuse to bring Saul a gift, the customary sign of loyalty to a new ruler. "But he held his peace." Saul's restraint is to his credit here (compare 11:12–13).
- **A missing paragraph?** A Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel (4QSam-a) has an extra paragraph at the end of this chapter. It explains that Nahash king of the Ammonites had been oppressing the tribes of Gad and Reuben east of the Jordan, gouging out the right eye of every man, and that 7,000 men had escaped to Jabesh-gilead. The Jewish historian Josephus knew this information too. It fits chapter 11 well. Some modern translations (the NRSV, for example) include it. The ESV does not. Whether it was in the original text is still debated.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Samuel reproved the people "for casting off the government of a prophet, and desiring that of a captain." On the hiding, he thinks Saul was "so little fond" of power that he hid among the stuff. He says Samuel "settles the original contract between them, and leaves it upon record," and contrasts the faithful few whose hearts God touched with those who despised Saul. JFB thinks Saul's concealment came from "innate modesty." He also says Saul "bore the insult with a prudence and magnanimity" that did him credit. Guzik also reads the hiding as humility, but suggests Samuel's "none like him" may have had an edge of sarcasm, which is possible but not something the text says. He notes that the lot only confirmed publicly what God had already said privately. He also thinks Samuel's written charter was probably based on Deuteronomy 17, and that a lost book like this does not mean Scripture is incomplete.

#### Pointing to Christ
Saul was anointed with oil, the Spirit came on him, and he was hailed as king. Every element points ahead to the true Anointed One. At Jesus' baptism the Spirit came down on him, not in a sudden rush for a season but to "remain" on him (John 1:32–33), and the Father declared him his beloved Son. Saul hid among the baggage, and later, filled with fear, lost the Spirit (16:14). Jesus "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped," but humbled himself to the cross, and God has given him "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:6–9). Some despised Saul and asked, "How can this man save us?" Many said the same of Jesus on the cross: "He saved others; he cannot save himself" (Matt 27:42). In his case the scoffers were wrong. And the new heart that God gave Saul for a task, God gives in Christ for good: "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you" (Ezek 36:26).
