*Setting:* Babylonia, the first day of the third month of the eleventh year of the exile, about June 587 BC. Jerusalem had only weeks left before its walls were breached (2 Kings 25:2–4). Two months earlier Ezekiel had announced that Pharaoh's arm was broken (30:20–26). This fifth message against Egypt is a parable about a tree. Ezekiel asks Pharaoh, "Whom are you like?" and answers with Assyria, the empire that had ruled the Near East for some three centuries and had fallen within living memory. Its capital Nineveh was destroyed in 612 BC by the Babylonians and Medes, and its last stand at Harran collapsed in 609 BC, even though Egypt had marched north to help it. (King Josiah of Judah died trying to stop that Egyptian army at Megiddo, 2 Kings 23:29.) Egypt had watched Assyria fall. The parable asks why it imagines it will not.

#### Pharaoh to Be Slain (vv1–18)
- **"Say to Pharaoh king of Egypt and to his multitude" (v2).** "Multitude" (*hamon*) is the word that marks all the Egypt oracles: its crowds, armies and wealth (30:10). The chapter ends with the same word: "This is Pharaoh and all his multitude" (v18).
- **"Whom are you like in your greatness?" (v2).** The question is asked again in v18, framing the whole parable. Pharaoh thinks no one compares to him. God gives him a comparison.
- **"Behold, Assyria was a cedar in Lebanon" (v3).** Cedars of Lebanon were the grandest trees the Israelites knew, tall, wide-spreading and fragrant, used for palaces and the temple. Ezekiel used the same image for the house of David in chapter 17. A few scholars change one letter of "Assyria" (*'ashshur*) to read a kind of tree (*te'ashshur*, cypress), so that the whole poem describes Egypt as a tree, with no mention of Assyria. Most translations and commentators keep "Assyria," which gives the parable its force: here is a precedent Egypt cannot ignore.
- **"Its top among the clouds" (v3).** The cedar's height stands for pride as well as power, as at the tower of Babel, whose top was to be "in the heavens" (Gen 11:4). Daniel describes Nebuchadnezzar's own dream of a tree whose top "reached to heaven" (Dan 4:10–12, 20–22), cut down for the same reason.
- **"The waters nourished it; the deep made it grow tall" (v4).** Assyria's land was watered by the Tigris and the Euphrates, and its cities were famous for canals and irrigated gardens. "The deep" (*tehom*) is the water under the earth, the source of springs and rivers (Gen 7:11; 49:25). The image fits Egypt too, whose life came from the Nile (29:3).
- **"All the birds of the heavens made their nests in its boughs… under its shadow lived all great nations" (v6).** A great tree giving shade and nesting places is a picture of an empire sheltering the nations under it (17:23; Dan 4:12). Empires did offer protection, at a price. Many peoples, Judah included at times, lived under Assyria's shadow as vassals.
- **"The cedars in the garden of God could not rival it" (v8).** The poem places the tree in Eden, as chapter 28 did with the king of Tyre. **"No tree in the garden of God was its equal in beauty."** This is not a literal claim about Assyria's history. It is the poetry of greatness: among all the powers God planted on earth, none was finer.
- **"I made it beautiful in the mass of its branches" (v9).** This is the key line of the first half. The tree's beauty was God's gift. JFB: "It was all due to My free grace." Henry: "God's hand must be eyed and owned in the advancement of the great men of the earth." **"All the trees of Eden envied it."** The other nations envied Assyria's greatness.
- **"Because it towered high and set its top among the clouds, and its heart was proud of its height" (v10).** The turn comes, as with Tyre (28:2, 17). The gift became a reason for pride. Isaiah had recorded the king of Assyria boasting, "By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom" (Isa 10:13), and God answered that the axe does not boast over the one who chops with it (Isa 10:15). Henry: "When men's outward condition rises their minds commonly rise with it; and it is very rare to find a humble spirit in the midst of great advancements."
- **"I will give it into the hand of a mighty one of the nations" (v11).** "Mighty one" is *'el*, a word that can also mean "god," used here of a powerful ruler. The mighty one who felled Assyria was Babylon, under Nabopolassar and his son Nebuchadnezzar. **"He shall surely deal with it as its wickedness deserves. I have cast it out."** The human conqueror carries out God's sentence.
- **"Foreigners, the most ruthless of nations, have cut it down and left it" (v12).** The title of Babylon from 28:7 and 30:11 again. The picture is of woodcutters felling a giant tree and leaving its broken branches on the mountains and in the ravines. **"All the peoples of the earth have gone away from its shadow and left it."** No one shelters under a fallen tree.
- **"On its fallen trunk dwell all the birds of the heavens" (v13).** The birds that once nested in its branches now perch on the trunk where it lies, and wild animals walk over its branches. The image may also suggest scavengers feeding on the fallen (32:4; 39:4).
- **"All this is in order that no trees by the waters may grow to towering height" (v14).** Assyria's fall is meant as a lesson to every power: none may set its top among the clouds. **"For they are all given over to death, to the world below, among the children of man."** Empires, like people, are mortal. Guzik, citing Taylor, calls death "the great equalizer." Henry: "The falls of others, both into sin and ruin, are intended as admonitions to us not to be secure or high-minded, nor to think we stand out of danger."
- **"On the day the cedar went down to Sheol I caused mourning" (v15).** Sheol is the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead, the grave seen as a place where all the dead go. Here it is the destination of a fallen nation. All creation mourns. **"I closed the deep over it, and restrained its rivers."** The deep that fed the tree is covered as a mourner covers the head, and stops flowing. **"I clothed Lebanon in gloom for it."** The other cedars wear mourning.
- **"I made the nations quake at the sound of its fall" (v16).** A great tree's crash is heard far away. **"And all the trees of Eden… were comforted in the world below."** The kings and nations Assyria had brought down before it, already in Sheol, are consoled to see the mighty empire join them. Isaiah 14:9–11 has a similar scene, with the dead kings rising to greet the fallen king of Babylon: "You too have become as weak as we!" JFB calls it "a kind of consolation to the wretched" to see the great brought down to their level.
- **"Those who were its arm, who lived under its shadow among the nations" (v17).** Assyria's allies and supporters, its "arm," went down with it. The warning to Egypt is pointed: Egypt itself had been Assyria's ally at the end.
- **"Whom are you thus like in glory and in greatness among the trees of Eden?" (v18).** The question returns, now with the answer. You are like Assyria. **"You shall be brought down with the trees of Eden to the world below."** Assyria was greater than Egypt. If it fell, Egypt will.
- **"You shall lie among the uncircumcised, with those who are slain by the sword" (v18).** Egyptians practiced circumcision. To lie among the uncircumcised was a disgrace, a burial among outsiders. The phrase points ahead to chapter 32, where Egypt is pictured joining the nations of the dead.
- **"This is Pharaoh and all his multitude, declares the Lord GOD" (v18).** The parable closes by naming its subject. The tree is Pharaoh. This is how Ezekiel's parables often end (17:12–21; 19:14).
- **Commentators:** Henry explains the method of the chapter like a judge: "In passing sentence upon great criminals it is usual to consult precedents." Assyria is the precedent for Egypt. He adds, "Those who have power ought to use it for the protection and comfort of those whom they have power over; for to that end they are entrusted with power," and ends, "Great men and great multitudes… when God comes to contend with them, will soon become little, less than nothing." JFB says Egypt was not to cease utterly as Assyria did, but "like Assyria, to lose its prominence in the empire of the world." On v6, JFB contrasts the world's empires with "the gospel kingdom," which will gather all under its shelter "for their good and for the glory of God," citing the parable of the mustard seed (Matt 13:32). Guzik discusses the views on "Assyria" in v3, noting some older writers who thought of ancient Assyria in Nimrod's day and others who read "a tree," and concludes it is best taken as historical Assyria held up as a lesson to Egypt. He cites Wiersbe's summary of the argument: Egypt was not as great as Assyria, and Babylon conquered Assyria, so Babylon can conquer Egypt.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel 31 is the story of a tree that grew too tall and was cut down. Scripture tells it more than once: the tower of Babel, Nebuchadnezzar's tree (Dan 4), and here Assyria and Egypt. Ezekiel himself gives the other side of the story in 17:22–24: God will take "a sprig from the lofty top of the cedar" and plant it on a high mountain of Israel, and "under it will dwell every kind of bird." Jesus told the same kind of story about the kingdom of God. It begins as a mustard seed, the smallest of seeds, and grows until "the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches" (Matt 13:31–32). The empires of this chapter began great and ended small. Christ's kingdom begins small and ends great. And the reason is in the King himself: he did not grasp at height but humbled himself, and so "God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:8–9). The great tree of Assyria offered the nations shade for a price. Christ offers rest to all who come (Matt 11:28).
