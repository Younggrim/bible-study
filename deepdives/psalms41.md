*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." This is the last psalm of Book One of the Psalter (Psalms 1–41), and it ends with the doxology that closes the whole book (v13). Book One opened with "Blessed is the man" (1:1), and Psalm 41 opens with the same word, "Blessed is the one." Like Psalms 38–40, it comes from a time of illness that David links with his own sin (v4). This time the sharpest pain is not the sickness but the people around his sickbed: enemies who visit pretending sympathy and leave to spread rumors, and a close friend who has turned against him. Many think of the years of Absalom's rebellion, when David's trusted counselor Ahithophel joined the plot (2 Sam 15:12, 31). Some suggest David was ill during the four years Absalom was winning hearts at the city gate (2 Sam 15:1–6), which would explain why the king did so little to stop him. That fits well but is not stated. Jesus quoted v9 the night he was betrayed: "He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me" (John 13:18).

#### Blessed Is the One Who Considers the Poor (vv1–3)
- **"Blessed is the one who considers the poor!" (v1).** "Considers" is *sakal*, to act wisely, pay attention, think something through. It is not tossing a coin to a beggar. It is giving careful thought to someone's situation and acting wisely for them. "Poor" (*dal*) means weak, low or thin. It is used of Pharaoh's thin cows (Gen 41:19) and of Amnon, who was looking "haggard" (2 Sam 13:4). It covers the physically weak and sick as well as the poor in money. That matters here, because the psalmist is himself weak and sick. JFB: the poor "in person, position, and possessions."
- **"In the day of trouble the LORD delivers him" (v1).** The one who cares for the weak will be cared for when he becomes weak. Proverbs 19:17: "Whoever is generous to the poor lends to the LORD, and he will repay him." Jesus put it as a beatitude: "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy" (Matt 5:7).
- **"He is called blessed in the land" (v2).** People speak well of him. JFB says "the land" is the land of promise. This was the shape of covenant blessing in the Old Testament, tied to life in the land (Deut 28:1–14).
- **"You do not give him up to the will of his enemies" (v2).** "Will" is literally "soul" or "appetite," the enemy's hungry desire. God does not hand him over to it.
- **"The LORD sustains him on his sickbed" (v3).** A tender picture of God as nurse. JFB: the imagery is "drawn from the acts of a kind nurse."
- **"In his illness you restore him to full health" (v3).** Literally "all his bed you turn in his sickness." The KJV kept that: "thou wilt make all his bed in his sickness." It could mean God changes the bed of sickness into one of health, or that he turns and smooths it like a nurse fluffing a pillow. Either way, the picture is God at the bedside.
- **David is speaking of himself.** Verses 1–3 sound like a general proverb. But by v4 it is clear that David is the sick man in the bed. He has cared for the weak, and now he is the weak one, holding on to God's promise. The Tyndale notes add that the poor had special protection under the Law (Lev 19:10) and that "poor" often stands for the godly who depend on God.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God will "consider their case, and send due supplies," and that compassion for the poor "is usually recompensed with temporal blessings." Guzik passes on Kidner's point that "considers" means careful thought, not token help, which the lexicon supports (*sakal*, act with insight). Guzik also reminds readers that this is Old Covenant language tying obedience to earthly blessing, and suggests David was quietly claiming the promise for himself. JFB links the blessing to Proverbs 19:17.

#### My Enemies Say of Me in Malice (vv4–9)
- **"O LORD, be gracious to me; heal me, for I have sinned against you!" (v4).** He asks for healing and confesses sin in the same breath. He does not claim the illness is unfair. "Heal me" is literally "heal my soul." Healing the soul comes first. The confession is direct and has no excuses: "against you." David said the same after Bathsheba: "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (51:4).
- **"When will he die, and his name perish?" (v5).** His enemies are waiting for the funeral. "His name perish" means that his family line and memory would be wiped out. For a king, that meant his dynasty, which God had promised to keep (2 Sam 7:16).
- **"When one comes to see me, he utters empty words" (v6).** Visiting the sick was a duty and a kindness. This visitor says all the right things, but his heart is somewhere else. JFB: he comes "as if to spy out my case."
- **"While his heart gathers iniquity; when he goes out, he tells it abroad" (v6).** He sits by the bed collecting material, every sign of weakness, every word that might be twisted, and then goes out to spread it. Guzik likens the heart to a magnet drawing sin to itself.
- **"All who hate me whisper together about me" (v7).** The whisper campaign. "Imagine the worst" means they plot evil for him, or interpret everything in the worst light.
- **"A deadly thing is poured out on him" (v8).** Literally "a thing of *belial*," a word that means worthlessness or wickedness. It later became a name for Satan (2 Cor 6:15). JFB reads it as "a word of Belial," a slander; most modern versions read it as a deadly disease. The enemies may mean that his illness is a curse from God, proof he is wicked. "Poured out" is a liquid picture, like something that has soaked into him and cannot be wiped off.
- **"He will not rise again from where he lies" (v8).** They have written him off. In the light of the gospel the line carries irony: they said the same of Jesus, sealing his tomb so he would not rise (Matt 27:62–66).
- **"Even my close friend in whom I trusted" (v9).** Literally "the man of my peace," as JFB notes, a friend who greeted him with *shalom*. It is the deepest wound. Psalm 55:12–14 says the same: "It is not an enemy who taunts me… But it is you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend." Ahithophel was David's most trusted counselor, whose advice "was as if one consulted the word of God" (2 Sam 16:23), and he went over to Absalom.
- **"Who ate my bread" (v9).** Eating together was a bond of loyalty in the ancient world. To share someone's bread and then betray him was the worst kind of treachery. Ahithophel's end foreshadows Judas: when his plan failed, he went home and hanged himself (2 Sam 17:23; Matt 27:5).
- **"Has lifted his heel against me" (v9).** Literally "made great his heel." It may be the picture of a horse rearing to kick, or of a man raising his foot to trample someone down, or of lifting the heel to walk away in contempt. JFB: "scornful violence." Some hear an echo of Genesis 3:15, where the serpent would strike the heel of the woman's offspring.
- **Jesus and Judas (John 13:18).** At the Last Supper, Jesus quoted this verse of Judas: "I am not speaking of all of you; I know whom I have chosen. But the Scripture will be fulfilled, 'He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.'" Moments later he gave Judas a piece of bread dipped in the dish (John 13:26). Notice what Jesus leaves out. He does not quote "in whom I trusted." Jesus knew from the beginning who would betray him (John 6:64). JFB calls the verse "not distinctly prophetical": David's experience was a pattern that Jesus' betrayal fulfilled.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Sin is the sickness of the soul," so the believer should care more for its healing than for the body's. On v9: "let us not think it strange, if we receive evil from those we suppose to be friends," and then he turns it on the reader: "We eat of his bread daily, yet lift up the heel against him." Guzik notes that Jesus omitted "in whom I trusted," and he contrasts David's full confession in v4 with Saul and Judas, who admitted sin without turning to God. JFB reads "a deadly thing" as slander and "lifted up heel" as scornful violence.

#### You Have Upheld Me (vv10–12)
- **"But you, O LORD, be gracious to me, and raise me up" (v10).** He repeats the prayer of v4, "be gracious to me." His enemies said he would not rise again (v8). He asks God to raise him up.
- **"That I may repay them!" (v10).** This surprises readers. The Psalms usually leave vengeance to God, and Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes how unusual it is. But David was king. It was his God-given duty to punish rebellion and treachery (Rom 13:4). JFB defends it as lawful punishment, not personal revenge. It is worth remembering that when Absalom died, David wept, "O my son Absalom… would I had died instead of you" (2 Sam 18:33). He did not take pleasure in repaying. And Jesus, who quoted v9, did not quote v10. He prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34).
- **"By this I know that you delight in me: my enemy will not shout in triumph over me" (v11).** "Shout in triumph" is the victory shout of an army. David reads God's protection as a sign of God's favor. "Delight in" (JFB: "tenderly loves") is personal. God is pleased with him.
- **"You have upheld me because of my integrity" (v12).** He has just confessed sin (v4), so "integrity" cannot mean sinlessness. It means wholeheartedness: he is not a double-dealer like his visitors (v6) and his false friend (v9). His heart toward God is sincere.
- **"And set me in your presence forever" (v12).** The real prize is not victory over enemies. It is to stand before God's face, under his eye and care. JFB: "under thy watch and care." Psalm 16:11: "in your presence there is fullness of joy."
- **Commentators:** Henry: "When we can discern the Lord's favour in any mercy, personal or public, that doubles it," and "If the grace of God did not take constant care of us, we should not be upheld." He draws a careful line: "though we may not take pleasure in the fall of our enemies, we may take pleasure in the making vain their designs." Guzik says the greatest request is to be set before God's face forever, which outweighs any victory, and notes that the verbs in vv11–12 speak as if David already has the answer.

#### Blessed Be the LORD, the God of Israel (v13)
- **This verse closes all of Book One, not just Psalm 41.** The Psalter is divided into five books, each ending with a doxology: 41:13, 72:18–19, 89:52, 106:48, and the whole of Psalm 150 for Book Five. Jewish tradition compared the five books of Psalms to the five books of Moses. Book One is almost entirely psalms of David.
- **"Blessed be the LORD" (v13).** The psalm that began with "Blessed is the one" (v1) ends with "Blessed be the LORD." The first word is *'ashre*, happy or fortunate. The second is *baruk*, praised, spoken well of. God blesses people by doing them good; people bless God by praising him.
- **"From everlasting to everlasting!" (v13).** From the farthest past to the farthest future. Every name in this psalm will perish, the visitors, the whisperers, the false friend, even David. God does not.
- **"Amen and Amen" (v13).** *Amen* means "it is firm, it is true, so be it." Doubled, it is the congregation's full, emphatic agreement. It appears doubled only here, in 72:19 and 89:52, and in Numbers 5:22 and Nehemiah 8:6.
- **Commentators:** Henry urges readers to give "heartfelt assent to those praises" God's people offer on earth and in heaven. Guzik passes on Kidner's observation that every book of the Psalter ends in praise, whatever trouble came before. JFB notes that "blessed" used of God means praised, and that the verse closes the first book.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus is the one who most perfectly "considers the poor." He touched lepers, sat with the sick and gave his life for the weak (Rom 5:6). And he received what this psalm describes. His enemies whispered and plotted, said he would never rise again, and a friend who ate his bread lifted his heel against him. Jesus took that verse on his own lips at the table. But where David prayed to be raised up to repay his enemies, Jesus was raised up to forgive his. God did set him in his presence forever (Heb 9:24), and because of him, sinners who pray "heal me, for I have sinned against you" are set there too. Book One of the Psalms ends where the gospel ends: "Blessed be the LORD… from everlasting to everlasting! Amen and Amen."
