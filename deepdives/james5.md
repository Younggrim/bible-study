*Setting:* James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, closes his letter to Jewish believers scattered outside Judea, probably written in the 40s or 50s AD. Many of his readers were poor farm workers and small traders, pressed by wealthy landowners who dragged them into court (2:6–7). Chapter 4 ended by warning merchants who planned their year as if God did not exist. Chapter 5 goes further, with a prophet's denunciation of rich oppressors, and then turns tenderly to the oppressed: be patient until the Lord comes, speak plainly, pray in every situation, and go after the one who wanders. The letter ends abruptly, with no greetings, on the note of rescuing a brother.

#### Warning to the Rich (vv1–6)
- **"Come now, you rich, weep and howl" (v1).** The same sharp "come now" as 4:13. "Howl" is a word the Greek Old Testament uses for the cry of people facing God's judgment (Isa 13:6; 15:3; Amos 8:3). This is not a call to repent, at least not directly. There is no "turn and live" in these verses. It reads like the prophets' oracles against foreign nations, spoken partly so the oppressed would hear that God sees. Most scholars think these rich men were outside the church, though James means the warning for any believer tempted to live like them.
- **"Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten" (v2).** In the ancient world wealth was held in three main forms: stored grain and goods, fine clothing, and precious metals. James names all three and shows each one decaying. The tenses are vivid. He speaks as if the ruin has already happened, as a prophet often did. Jesus said the same: "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy" (Matt 6:19–20).
- **"Your gold and silver have corroded" (v3).** Gold does not literally rust. James is speaking in pictures. The corrosion "will be evidence against you": wealth hoarded while workers went hungry will stand up in court as a witness for the prosecution. And it "will eat your flesh like fire," turning from treasure into punishment.
- **"You have laid up treasure in the last days" (v3).** The irony is bitter. For the New Testament, the "last days" began with Jesus' coming (Acts 2:17; Heb 1:2). These men stockpiled wealth at the very moment history was heading to its end, like someone redecorating a house on fire.
- **"The wages of the laborers… kept back by fraud, are crying out against you" (v4).** Day laborers were paid at sunset because they lived hand to mouth. The law said, "You shall give him his wages on the same day, before the sun sets… lest he cry against you to the LORD, and you be guilty of sin" (Deut 24:14–15; Lev 19:13). Jesus' parable of the vineyard workers assumes this (Matt 20:8). Like Abel's blood (Gen 4:10), the stolen wages themselves "cry out."
- **"The ears of the Lord of hosts" (v4).** The Greek keeps the Hebrew word, *Sabaoth*, "armies." The Lord who commands the armies of heaven hears the cry of a farmhand nobody else will listen to. This exact phrase in Isaiah comes in a woe against greedy landowners who "join house to house" (Isa 5:7–9). The only other New Testament use of *Sabaoth* is Romans 9:29, quoting Isaiah 1:9.
- **"You have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter" (v5).** The picture is of cattle eating happily on the very day they are led to the butcher. Jeremiah prayed that the wicked be set apart "for the day of slaughter" (Jer 12:3). The rich man in Jesus' story "feasted sumptuously every day" while Lazarus starved at his gate (Luke 16:19–31).
- **"You have condemned and murdered the righteous person. He does not resist you" (v6).** The rich used the courts to ruin the poor, and taking a poor man's livelihood could mean his death. "The righteous person" is singular. Most scholars, and the Tyndale notes, take it as a type: the innocent poor man in general, who has no power to fight back. Some early writers saw a reference to Jesus, "the Righteous One" (Acts 3:14; 7:52), who did not resist those who killed him. A few have seen a grim foreshadowing of James himself, called "James the Just," who was killed by the high priest's party around AD 62. The text itself points first to the defenseless poor, but the pattern is the same one that led to the cross.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God does not forbid us to use lawful pleasures; but to live in pleasure, especially sinful pleasure, is a provoking sin," and "all idolized treasures will soon perish, except as they will rise up in judgment against their possessors." JFB thinks the coming "miseries" were first the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 and finally Christ's judgment. He reads v6 as a pattern of condemning the just "with Christ foremost." JFB also says "Lord of Sabaoth" occurs only here in the New Testament, but the lexicon lists Romans 9:29 as well. Guzik clears up an old confusion: *Sabaoth* means "armies," not "Sabbath." He stresses that Jesus had wealthy followers, so riches are not condemned in themselves, but they make many sins easier.

#### Patience in Suffering (vv7–12)
- **"Be patient, therefore, brothers" (v7).** The "therefore" links this to vv1–6. Because God will judge the oppressors, the oppressed do not have to take revenge. "Be patient" is *makrothymeō*, literally "be long-tempered": slow to boil, able to hold out a long time under provocation. It is the patience of love in 1 Corinthians 13:4.
- **"Until the coming of the Lord" (v7).** "Coming" is *parousia*, the word for a king's official arrival. It appears three times in vv7–8. James's answer to injustice is not a revolution but a return: the King is coming to set things right.
- **"The early and the late rains" (v7).** In Israel the "early" rains came in October and November, softening the hard ground for plowing and sowing. The "late" rains came in March and April, filling out the grain before harvest. Between them a farmer could only wait. The phrase is straight from the Old Testament, where these rains are God's covenant gift (Deut 11:14; Jer 5:24; Joel 2:23). James's readers, many of them farm workers, knew exactly what this waiting felt like.
- **"Establish your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is at hand" (v8).** "Establish" means to make firm or prop up. Patience is not drifting. It is a heart held steady. "At hand" is the same word Jesus used of the kingdom (Mark 1:15). Every New Testament writer lives as if the return could come soon (Rom 13:12; 1 Pet 4:7; Rev 22:20). Peter later explains the delay: God's patience gives people time to repent (2 Pet 3:8–9).
- **"Do not grumble against one another… the Judge is standing at the door" (v9).** Suffering people often turn on each other. "Grumble" is literally "groan," a muttered complaint under the breath. James warns that the coming Lord is also Judge, and believers too will answer to him. The picture of someone "at the door" echoes Jesus (Matt 24:33; Rev 3:20).
- **"Take the prophets" (v10).** Jesus pointed to them too: "so they persecuted the prophets who were before you" (Matt 5:12). Jeremiah was beaten, put in stocks and dropped in a muddy cistern (Jer 20:2; 38:6). Faithful speaking for God has always been costly.
- **"We consider those blessed who remained steadfast" (v11).** "Steadfast" is *hypomonē*, "remaining under" a load, the word James opened the letter with (1:3–4, 12). James uses two words in this section. *Makrothymia* (vv7–10) is patience with people. *Hypomonē* (v11) is endurance under circumstances.
- **"The steadfastness of Job" (v11).** Job was not quietly patient. He cursed the day of his birth and argued with God (Job 3; 23). But he never let go of God: "though he slay me, I will hope in him" (Job 13:15). That is what *hypomonē* means: not silence, but holding on.
- **"You have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful" (v11).** "Purpose" is *telos*, "end" or "outcome." Job's story ended with God restoring him "twice as much as he had before" (Job 42:10). The point is not that every sufferer gets double back in this life. It is that God's last word is mercy. "Compassionate" is *polysplanchnos*, "very tender-hearted," a word that appears only here in the New Testament. James echoes the great self-description of God in Exodus 34:6 and Psalm 103:8.
- **"But above all, my brothers, do not swear" (v12).** This is the closest James comes to quoting Jesus word for word (Matt 5:33–37). He does not mean coarse language. He means oaths: "by heaven," "by earth," formulas people used to make a statement seem binding, while keeping loopholes (Matt 23:16–22). If a person's plain word is not enough, something is wrong with their honesty.
- **Why "above all"?** It may simply mark the start of the closing section, as letters of the time often did. It may also link to the context. Suffering tempts people to rash, impatient words, including vows and curses. Either way, a believer's "yes" should mean yes.
- **Are all oaths forbidden?** Some traditions, notably Quakers and Anabaptists, take these words literally and refuse all oaths, even in court. Most Christians have understood James and Jesus to be forbidding casual and evasive swearing, since God himself swears (Heb 6:13–18) and Paul calls God as witness (Rom 1:9; 2 Cor 1:23). Both agree on the main point: speech so honest that oaths are unnecessary.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Consider him that waits for a crop of corn; and will not you wait for a crown of glory?" He adds, "Men count time long, because they measure it by their own lives; but all time is as nothing to God." Guzik and JFB part ways on the rains. Guzik insists they are simply literal farm rains and rejects reading them as two outpourings of the Spirit. JFB allows that the late rain may hint at a future Pentecost-like outpouring. The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, keep to the farming picture. JFB argues Job must have been a real man, since a made-up figure would not serve as an example, and calls Job's impatience real but his trust final.

#### The Prayer of Faith (vv13–18)
- **"Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise" (v13).** Every condition of life is meant to turn a person toward God. "Sing praise" is *psallō*, the root of "psalm." The Psalms themselves are full of both lament and praise. Paul and Silas did both at once, praying and singing hymns in a Philippian jail (Acts 16:25).
- **"Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church" (v14).** The sick person takes the initiative. "Elders" were the recognized leaders of the local congregation (Acts 14:23; 20:17). Notice that James sends for the elders, not for a person with a special healing gift. Healing prayer is part of ordinary church care.
- **"Anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (v14).** Olive oil was the ancient world's common medicine: the Good Samaritan poured oil and wine on wounds (Luke 10:34; Isa 1:6). The disciples also anointed the sick when Jesus sent them out (Mark 6:13). There are two main views. Some read the oil as medicine, so James is saying to use the best care available and pray. Others read it as a symbol, marking the person out for God's attention and picturing the Spirit's presence. "In the name of the Lord" points to the second, but the two need not be exclusive. Either way, the power is the Lord's, not the oil's.
- **Different church practices.** The Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches treat this verse as the basis of a sacrament, the Anointing of the Sick. For centuries in the West it was given mainly to the dying ("extreme unction"), though since 1972 the Catholic church again offers it to the seriously ill. Protestant churches generally see a practice, not a sacrament, and many elders still anoint and pray for the sick. James's own concern is plainly recovery, not preparation for death.
- **"The prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up" (v15).** "Sick" here is *kamnō*, "worn out," the word Hebrews uses for growing weary (Heb 12:3). "Save" (*sōzō*) in the Gospels often means "make well" (Mark 5:34). The primary sense here is healing of the body. Notice what heals: not the oil, not even the elders, but "the prayer of faith," and above all "the Lord."
- **Does God always heal?** Scripture shows he does not. Paul left Trophimus sick at Miletus (2 Tim 4:20), and Paul's own "thorn" was not removed (2 Cor 12:7–9). The Tyndale notes say the promise stands under God's will. The prayer of faith trusts God to act and leaves the outcome to him. Even when healing does not come in this life, "the Lord will raise him up" is finally true at the resurrection.
- **"If he has committed sins, he will be forgiven" (v15).** "If" matters. Not all sickness comes from sin. Jesus said of the man born blind that neither he nor his parents had sinned (John 9:2–3). But sometimes there is a link (Mark 2:5; 1 Cor 11:30). James covers both cases: the sick person's whole need, body and soul, is met.
- **"Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed" (v16).** "Therefore" widens the circle from elders to every believer. "Confess" is *exomologeō*, to acknowledge openly, from a root meaning "to say the same thing," to agree with God about what something is (compare 1 John 1:9). James means mutual confession between believers, not a formal rite. "Healed" (*iaomai*) can mean bodily healing, and it is also used of spiritual healing (1 Pet 2:24; Heb 12:13). James probably intends both.
- **"The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working" (v16).** The verb is *energeō*, from which comes "energy." The ESV takes it as describing the prayer in action. "Righteous" does not mean sinless, since the very next verse makes the point that Elijah was ordinary. It means a person in right standing with God who lives consistently with it.
- **"Elijah was a man with a nature like ours" (v17).** The phrase is used of Paul and Barnabas protesting at Lystra that they were only human (Acts 14:15). Elijah won on Mount Carmel and then fled in fear and asked to die (1 Kings 19:4). James picks the most spectacular prophet and stresses how human he was.
- **"He prayed fervently" (v17).** Literally "he prayed with prayer," a Hebrew-style way of expressing intensity. 1 Kings 17:1 records Elijah announcing the drought, not praying for it, but 1 Kings 18:42 shows him bowed on Carmel with his face between his knees, praying for rain. James draws the prayer behind both moments out of the story.
- **"For three years and six months it did not rain" (v17).** 1 Kings 18:1 says the word came "in the third year." Jesus also says three and a half years (Luke 4:25), so this was the common reckoning, likely counting from the start of the dry season before Elijah's announcement. The figure is half of seven, which later Scripture uses for a time of trial (Dan 7:25; Rev 11:2–3).
- **"Then he prayed again, and heaven gave rain" (v18).** The last word of this section is rain and fruit, the same picture James used of the patient farmer in v7. Prayer and patience belong together.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "In a time of sickness it is not cold and formal prayer that is effectual, but the prayer of faith." All three reject using v14 for last rites. JFB's line is sharp: "He does not say the oil shall save: it is but the symbol." JFB thinks the anointing went with the miraculous healing of the apostolic age. Guzik leaves the question of oil as medicine or symbol open and stresses that physical healing is in view. He notes God does not always heal and the reasons lie hidden in his will. Guzik and JFB both explain "prayed fervently" as literally "prayed with prayer," which matches the Greek. Both also read v16 as mutual confession between believers rather than confession to a priest, and Guzik adds that confession should be made with discretion.

#### Bring Back the Wandering (vv19–20)
- **"If anyone among you wanders from the truth" (v19).** "Wanders" is *planaō*, to go astray like a lost sheep, the root of "planet," the "wandering" star. "Among you" means a professing believer. "The truth" for James is not only correct ideas but a way of life (1:18; 3:14). A person can drift by doctrine or by behavior, and in James the two are never far apart.
- **"And someone brings him back" (v19).** "Brings back" (*epistrephō*) is the Bible's word for turning or conversion. Notice who does it: "someone," any believer, not just elders. Paul says the same: "you who are spiritual should restore him in a spirit of gentleness" (Gal 6:1). The letter that began with "count it all joy… when you meet trials" (1:2) ends with believers carrying one another through them.
- **"Will save his soul from death" (v20).** Whose soul? Most interpreters take it as the wanderer's: bringing him back rescues him from the "death" that sin brings forth (1:15). A few take it as the restorer's own soul, but James's focus is on the one who strayed.
- **"And will cover a multitude of sins" (v20).** An echo of Proverbs 10:12: "love covers all offenses." Peter quotes the same proverb (1 Pet 4:8). The point is not that the restorer earns credit for his own sins. When the wanderer repents, his sins are covered by God's forgiveness (Ps 32:1).
- **The abrupt ending.** No greetings, no blessing. James stops with the picture of one believer bringing another home. For a letter that has spoken sternly about faith that does nothing, it is a fitting end: real faith goes after the one who is lost.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that "it is no mark of a wise or holy man, to boast of being free from error, or to refuse to acknowledge an error," and that saving one soul is worth more than preserving many lives. JFB reads "the truth" as the gospel and says the "multitude of sins" covered are the convert's, covered "before God" through Christ's atonement, which agrees with the Tyndale notes. Guzik observes that most people who wander do not plan to, they drift, and he reads the ending as James's last call to a living, active faith.

#### Pointing to Christ
James's portrait of "the righteous person" who is condemned and "does not resist" (v6) describes Jesus perfectly, who "when he suffered… did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). He is "the Lord of hosts" who hears the cries of the oppressed, the Judge "standing at the door," and the coming King whose *parousia* the farmer-hearted church waits for. Job's story ended in mercy, and so does the whole story of Scripture, because "the Lord is compassionate and merciful." And the last picture of the letter is a small version of the gospel: the Good Shepherd went after the wandering sheep himself (Luke 15:4–7), saved our souls from death, and covered our multitude of sins with his own blood.
