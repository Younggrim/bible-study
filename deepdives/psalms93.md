*Setting:* Psalm 93 is one of the shortest psalms, five verses, and has no title in the Hebrew text. Its author and date are unknown. The Greek translation (the Septuagint, made a couple of centuries before Christ) gives it a heading that links it to the day before the Sabbath, "when the land was settled," and the Mishnah, the early collection of Jewish tradition (about AD 200), records that the Levites sang it in the temple on the sixth day of the week. That fits its theme: on the sixth day God finished making the world, and this psalm celebrates the King who made it and holds it firm. It opens a group of psalms (93–100) that ring with the cry "The LORD reigns" (93:1; 96:10; 97:1; 99:1). They follow Psalm 89, which mourned the apparent collapse of David's throne, and Psalm 90, which asked "How long?" (90:13). The answer of these psalms is that whatever has happened to Israel's kings, the LORD himself is still King. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's three natural movements.

#### The LORD Reigns (vv1–2)
- **"The LORD reigns" (v1).** The Hebrew is two words, *Yahweh malak*, and the covenant name comes first for emphasis: it is the LORD, and no other god, who is King. The verb can mean "is king" or "has become king," the cry raised when a new king took the throne (2 Sam 15:10; 1 Kgs 1:11; 2 Kgs 9:13: "Jehu is king"). So the line has the ring of a royal proclamation. God is not newly made King, as if he once was not. Rather his reign is announced and displayed afresh, as at a coronation. Scholars debate whether Israel held a yearly festival celebrating God's enthronement. The psalm does not say, and the idea remains a theory.
- **"He is robed in majesty" (v1).** Kings were known by their robes. The LORD needs no tailor: his clothing is his own majesty (*ge'ut*, "loftiness," "proud splendor"). The same word describes the sea's surging "pride" in Psalm 89:9, which God rules. Here the true majesty belongs to him. Compare Psalm 104:1–2, where God is "clothed with splendor and majesty, covering yourself with light as with a garment."
- **"He has put on strength as his belt" (v1).** A belt gathered up a long robe so a man could work, run or fight (1 Kgs 18:46; Isa 11:5). The picture is of a King not only enthroned but ready for action. Every phrase of the verse is doubled or tripled, a pattern that runs through the whole psalm and gives it a hammering, triumphant rhythm.
- **"Yes, the world is established; it shall never be moved" (v1).** The result of God's reign is a stable world. Ancient people feared that the order of things could collapse back into chaos. The psalm says it cannot, because the King holds it. The same words appear in Psalm 96:10 and 1 Chronicles 16:30. "Moved" (*mot*) is the word for shaking or tottering, used of the righteous who "shall never be moved" because God holds them (Ps 15:5; 16:8; 62:2).
- **"Your throne is established from of old" (v2).** The psalm turns from describing God to speaking to him. "From of old" (*me'az*) means from the very beginning. God's rule is not a new arrangement and cannot be overthrown, because there was never a time before it.
- **"You are from everlasting" (v2).** The throne is as old as the King, and the King has no beginning. Psalm 90:2 used the same phrase: "from everlasting to everlasting you are God." Earthly thrones, including David's, rise and fall (Ps 89:44). This one does not.
- **Commentators:** Henry organizes the psalm around a comparison: other kings have robes, thrones, enemies and honor, and so does the LORD, only greater. He calls earthly majesty beside God's "but like the glimmerings of a glow-worm compared with the brightness of the sun." He reads "clothed with strength" as armor as well as robes, a King "clad in armour," and says God's right to rule rests on his having made the world: "he that gave being to it, no doubt, may give law to it." JFB pictures God "as a King entering on His reign" who puts on his own attributes as royal robes, with "the durability of the world" as the result. Guzik stresses that the opening cry lifts Israel's covenant God over every idol and rival claim to power, and that the world stands firm unless God himself chooses to move it.

#### The Floods Have Lifted Up (vv3–4)
- **"The floods have lifted up, O LORD" (v3).** "Floods" (*neharot*) is literally "rivers" or "streams," and the word is repeated three times, each line rising like a wave. The psalmist turns to the LORD with what he hears. He does not argue with the water.
- **"The floods lift up their roaring" (v3).** The verb changes tense from "have lifted" to "lift," as though the storm is still building while he speaks. "Roaring" (*dokhi*) appears only here in the Old Testament. It seems to mean the pounding or crashing of breakers.
- **Sea and river as chaos.** In Canaanite poetry from Ugarit (on the Syrian coast, about 1300 BC), the storm god Baal fights and defeats Yam, "Prince Sea," also called "Judge River," and is then hailed as king. Psalm 93 uses the same pairing of sea and rivers and the same idea of kingship after victory, but there is no battle and no rival god. The waters are not deities. They make noise, and the LORD is simply above them. Israel's Scriptures often use the raging sea this way: God "rule[s] the raging of the sea" (Ps 89:9), "stills the roaring of the seas… the tumult of the peoples" (Ps 65:7), and sets a limit the waves cannot pass (Job 38:8–11; Jer 5:22).
- **What the waters stand for.** The image is not only about weather. Elsewhere the prophets use flooding rivers for invading empires: Assyria comes like "the waters of the River, mighty and many" (Isa 8:7), and Egypt "rises like the Nile" (Jer 46:7–8). The Tyndale notes suggest God's mastery of nature's chaos gives the psalmist confidence that God will also defeat his people's present enemies. Many readers have applied the waves to every power, outside or within, that seems unstoppable.
- **"Mightier than the thunders of many waters, mightier than the waves of the sea" (v4).** "Mightier" (*addir*) means majestic, glorious, powerful. "Waves" here (*mishberei yam*) are literally "breakers," the waves that shatter on the shore. Jonah used the same word: "all your waves and your billows passed over me" (Jonah 2:3). The psalmist piles up the most fearsome sounds he knows, and then tops them all.
- **"The LORD on high is mighty!" (v4).** "On high" places God above the storm, out of its reach. Psalm 29:10 says, "The LORD sits enthroned over the flood; the LORD sits enthroned as king forever." The flood rises, but it rises toward a throne it cannot touch.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the storm "a threatening storm supposed" and v4 "an immovable anchor cast in this storm." He observes that the power of the church's enemies "is but as the noise of many waters; there is more of sound than substance in it," and quotes Jeremiah 46:17, where Pharaoh is called "but a noise." He adds, "The church's friends are commonly more frightened than hurt." He also applies the waves to "the tumults that are sometimes in our own bosoms." JFB says God's "underived power exceeds the most sublime exhibitions of the most powerful objects in nature," citing Psalm 89:9. Guzik notes that the psalmist addresses the LORD, not the waves, and that the waters stand for whatever opposes God and frightens his people. He takes comfort that nothing is higher or stronger than the King above them.

#### Your Decrees Are Very Trustworthy (v5)
- **"Your decrees are very trustworthy" (v5).** After the thunder of the sea, the psalm ends quietly with God's word. "Decrees" (*edot*, often "testimonies") are God's solemn declarations of his will, the covenant laws he has given. "Very trustworthy" (*ne'emnu me'od*) comes from the same root as "amen" and means firm, reliable, confirmed. The same God who keeps the world steady keeps his word steady. Psalm 19:7 says "the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple."
- **Why the shift?** It can seem abrupt to move from waves to laws. But the two belong together. The King who orders creation also orders his people's life by his word. The Tyndale notes say God orders the world through his stable rule and his royal laws. The world "shall never be moved" (v1), and neither will what he has said.
- **"Holiness befits your house" (v5).** "Befits" (*na'avah*) means "is fitting" or "is lovely for." Holiness is the right adornment of the place where God lives, as majesty is his robe (v1). The "house" may be the temple in Jerusalem or God's heavenly palace, and the psalm may intend both, since the earthly temple was a copy of the heavenly one (Exod 25:9, 40; Heb 8:5). A King's palace reflects its King. A holy God's house must be holy.
- **"O LORD, forevermore" (v5).** Literally "for length of days." The psalm ends where v2 began, with time without end. The world stands, the throne stands, the word stands, and the holiness of God's house stands, all "forevermore."
- **Commentators:** Henry says that as God can protect his church, so "he is true to the promises he has made of its safety and victory." On holiness he writes, "Fashions change, and that which is becoming at one time is not so at another; but holiness always becomes God's house," and "Where there is purity there shall be peace." JFB joins the two halves: while God's power "inspires dread, His revealed will should secure our confidence," so that "fear and love combined" shape worship in his house "both earthly and heavenly." Guzik sets the certainty of God's word beside the uncertainty of human teaching, and applies the holy house to the church as God's dwelling: his people cannot adorn that house unless they are themselves holy (1 Cor 3:16–17).

#### Pointing to Christ
Jewish interpreters already read this psalm and the ones after it as songs of the age of the Messiah, as JFB notes, and Henry says plainly that "in singing this psalm we forget ourselves if we forget Christ." The Gospels show the King of Psalm 93 at work. When a storm on the Sea of Galilee threatened to swamp the boat, Jesus "rebuked the wind and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" and the disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:39–41). The answer is in this psalm: only the LORD on high is mightier than the waves. Jesus is also the one through whom the world was made and by whom it holds together (Col 1:16–17; Heb 1:3), the Word who is perfectly trustworthy (John 1:14), and the one who makes his people God's holy house (Eph 2:21–22). Revelation takes up the psalm's opening cry for the end of history: "Hallelujah! For the Lord our God the Almighty reigns" (Rev 19:6).
