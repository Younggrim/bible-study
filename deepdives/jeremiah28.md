*Setting:* The temple courts in Jerusalem, in the fifth month (July–August) of Zedekiah's fourth year, 594–593 BC. This is the same year as chapter 27. Envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon have come to plan a revolt against Babylon, and Jeremiah has been walking the streets wearing an ox yoke, telling everyone, king and envoys alike, to bow their necks and serve Nebuchadnezzar. Now another prophet answers him, in public, in the temple, with the same "Thus says the LORD." The chapter is a duel between two men who both claim to speak for God. It is the clearest picture in the Bible of what it is like to live among competing prophets, and of how God settles the question.

#### Hananiah the False Prophet (vv1–17)
- **"In that same year, at the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah… in the fifth month of the fourth year" (v1).** The phrase "beginning of the reign" sits awkwardly with "fourth year." Most think it is used loosely here for the first part of his reign, or that a scribe joined two date formulas. The Greek simply reads "in the fourth year of Zedekiah." The precise month matters, because v17 counts from it.
- **"Hananiah the son of Azzur, the prophet from Gibeon" (v1).** Hananiah means "the LORD has been gracious," which is just what he preached. Gibeon, about six miles northwest of Jerusalem, was one of the towns given to the priests (Josh 21:17), so Hananiah may have come from a priestly family. A Jaazaniah son of Azzur appears among the leaders in Jerusalem whom Ezekiel saw "devising iniquity" (Ezek 11:1–2). He may have been Hananiah's brother, but that is uncertain.
- **"In the house of the LORD, in the presence of the priests and all the people" (v1).** Hananiah chose the most public place, at a time when crowds were present, perhaps during a festival.
- **"Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel" (v2).** Hananiah uses exactly the same formula as Jeremiah (27:4, 21). He also closes with "declares the LORD" (v4). To any listener the two men sounded the same. The difference lay in the source, not the style.
- **"I have broken the yoke of the king of Babylon" (v2).** He speaks in the past tense, as prophets did of events God had already decided: it is as good as done. It is a direct answer to Jeremiah's yoke.
- **"Within two years I will bring back… all the vessels of the LORD's house" (v3).** Literally "within two years of days," two full years. Jeremiah had just said the vessels would stay in Babylon "until the day when I visit them" (27:22), and that the exile would last seventy years (25:11). Hananiah sets a date. Promising specific dates is bold: it can be checked.
- **"I will also bring back to this place Jeconiah the son of Jehoiakim" (v4).** This was politically explosive. Many in Judah still considered the exiled Jeconiah the real king (see 22:28). To promise his return was to imply that Zedekiah was only filling in. Hananiah's message flattered the exiles, the nationalists and the anti-Babylon party all at once.
- **"Amen! May the LORD do so" (v6).** Jeremiah's answer is surprising. He does not shout Hananiah down. He says "Amen," "so be it." Readers differ on his tone. Some hear irony, as if to say, "Wouldn't that be nice?" Others hear a sincere wish. Jeremiah loved his people and had begged God to spare them (14:7–9, 19–22). He would have been glad to be wrong. Both may be true. He wishes it were so, and he knows it is not.
- **"Yet hear now this word" (v7).** His argument is not "I'm right because I say so." It is an appeal to history and to the Law.
- **"The prophets who preceded you and me from ancient times prophesied war, famine, and pestilence" (v8).** Elijah, Amos, Hosea, Micah and Isaiah had all warned sinful nations of judgment. Most Hebrew manuscripts read "war, disaster, and pestilence." A few read "famine," which differs by one letter, and the ESV follows them. The point is the same either way. Warnings of judgment against an unrepentant people fit the long line of true prophets.
- **"As for the prophet who prophesies peace, when the word of that prophet comes to pass, then it will be known" (v9).** This applies Deuteronomy 18:21–22: if a prophet's word does not come true, the LORD did not speak it. A prophet of peace to a people deep in sin carries the burden of proof, because his message goes against the covenant warnings (Lev 26; Deut 28). Wait and see.
- **"Hananiah took the yoke-bars from the neck of Jeremiah the prophet and broke them" (v10).** Hananiah answers a sign with a sign. In the ancient world a symbolic act was more than theater. It was believed to set the announced event in motion. By breaking Jeremiah's yoke, Hananiah claimed to be breaking Babylon's.
- **"Even so will I break the yoke of Nebuchadnezzar… within two years" (v11).** He repeats his date in front of everyone. **"But Jeremiah the prophet went his way."** Jeremiah says nothing. He has no word from the LORD yet, and a true prophet does not invent one on the spot. It must have looked like defeat. Hananiah had the last word, the crowd and a broken yoke. Notice how often the narrator calls Jeremiah "the prophet" in this chapter (vv5, 6, 10, 11, 12, 15). The narrator knows which one is real.
- **"Sometime after… the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah" (v12).** God's answer comes in God's time. Jeremiah waits for it rather than reacting.
- **"You have broken wooden bars, but you have made in their place bars of iron" (v13).** Wood can be snapped. Iron cannot. Rejecting God's lighter discipline only brings a heavier one. Moses had warned of exactly this: "he will put a yoke of iron on your neck until he has destroyed you" (Deut 28:48). The Greek reads "I will make" rather than "you have made." Either way, Hananiah's false hope made the yoke heavier for everyone.
- **"I have put upon the neck of all these nations an iron yoke… for I have given to him even the beasts of the field" (v14).** This repeats 27:6. Nothing Hananiah did changed God's decree.
- **"Listen, Hananiah, the LORD has not sent you" (v15).** Now Jeremiah speaks with full authority. **"You have made this people trust in a lie."** That is the real harm. Hananiah's error was not just a wrong forecast. He led people to rest their hope on something false, so that they would not repent and would not submit.
- **"Behold, I will remove you from the face of the earth" (v16).** In Hebrew there is a sharp wordplay on the verb *shalach*, "send." "The LORD has not *sent* you" (v15). "Therefore… I will *send* you off the face of the earth" (v16). Hananiah was never sent to the people, so now he will be sent away from them, into death.
- **"Because you have uttered rebellion against the LORD" (v16).** These are almost the exact words of Deuteronomy 13:5, the law against a prophet who leads people away from the LORD: "he has taught rebellion against the LORD your God." The Law set death as the penalty. Here God carries out the sentence himself.
- **"In that same year, in the seventh month, the prophet Hananiah died" (v17).** Two months after the fifth month of v1. Hananiah promised that something would happen within two years, and it did not. Jeremiah said Hananiah would die within the year, and he died within two months. The test of v9 settled the case, though not in the way Hananiah expected. The seventh month was the season of the Day of Atonement and the Feast of Booths, when crowds would again fill the temple where Hananiah had spoken.
- **False prophets everywhere.** At the same time, prophets among the exiles in Babylon were promising a quick return too (29:8–9, 21–23), and Ezekiel was confronting the "prophets who prophesy from their own hearts" and say "Peace" when there is no peace (Ezek 13:2, 10). Hananiah was not unusual. He was one voice in a chorus.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Hananiah "had only the spirit of the world, not the Spirit of God," and that "it is no new thing for lies to be fathered upon the God of truth." On v6 he takes Jeremiah's "Amen" as sincere: "though he had no warrant from God to promise them peace, yet he earnestly desired it and prayed for it." JFB agrees: "Jeremiah prays for the people, though constrained to prophesy against them." Guzik, following Harrison and Ryken, hears irony instead, so the commentators divide on the tone. JFB calls the two-year promise "marvellous presumption" and the breaking of the yoke "impious audacity," and notes that Jeremiah "deigns no reply." Guzik stresses that Hananiah may well have been sincere, but sincerity does not make a message true. He cites Cundall that the people forged the iron yoke themselves by rejecting the lighter one. He also observes that the two months between the sentence and Hananiah's death were time to repent.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two prophets stood in the same courtyard using the same words. One told people what they wanted to hear, and the other told them what God had said. Jesus warned of the same danger: "Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing" (Matt 7:15), and "many false prophets will arise and lead many astray" (Matt 24:11). The way to tell them apart has not changed: the word must come true, and it must agree with what God has already said (Deut 13; 18; 1 John 4:1). Hananiah tried to break a yoke God had placed, and he only forged an iron one. Jesus is the one who truly breaks yokes, not by denying God's judgment but by bearing it. Isaiah foretold that God would shatter "the yoke of his burden" through a child born to reign on David's throne (Isa 9:4–7). That child is the true Prophet, whose words never fail (Matt 24:35). He says, "If the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36).
