*Setting:* The first four trumpets struck the natural world: land, sea, rivers and sky (ch 8). An eagle then cried three woes over "those who dwell on the earth" (8:13). Chapter 9 brings the first two woes, the fifth and sixth trumpets. Now the judgments strike people directly, and they come from the spiritual world: a horde from the bottomless pit, then a vast army from the Euphrates. It is one of the strangest chapters in the Bible. It ends with one of the saddest verses: after all of it, the survivors "did not repent."

The four approaches differ sharply here:
- **Futurists** (Guzik) see real demonic powers loosed on the earth in the coming tribulation: locusts that are demons, and a literal army of 200 million.
- **Historicists** have had their most famous readings here. Henry and JFB both report the long Protestant tradition that saw the locusts as the Arab armies of Islam's early expansion (or false teaching in the church), and the Euphrates horsemen as the Ottoman Turks, whose cannons breathed "fire and smoke and sulfur."
- **Preterists** link the scenes with Rome's wars, such as the feared Parthian cavalry beyond the Euphrates, or the Jewish war of AD 66–70.
- **Idealists** see a picture of how evil, when God lets it loose, torments and destroys the very people who serve it, in every age.

All agree that even the forces of hell work only on a leash God holds, and that his people are kept.

#### The Fifth Trumpet: Locusts from the Bottomless Pit (vv1–12)
- **"I saw a star fallen from heaven to earth, and he was given the key" (v1).** The ESV's "fallen" is right: John sees a star that *had already* fallen. Stars stand for angels in Revelation (1:20), and "he" shows this one is a person. Readers differ on who he is:
  - **A fallen angel, perhaps Satan.** Jesus said, "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (Luke 10:18), and Isaiah 14:12 speaks of the "Day Star" fallen from heaven. JFB and many others take this view.
  - **A good angel sent by God.** In 20:1 an angel comes down "holding in his hand the key to the bottomless pit" to lock Satan up.

  Either way, he "was given" the key. He doesn't own it. Christ holds the keys of Death and Hades (1:18).
- **"The shaft of the bottomless pit" (vv1–2).** "Bottomless pit" is *abyssos*, the abyss, the deep. In Jewish thought it was the prison where rebellious spirits were held (1 Enoch 10; 2 Pet 2:4; Jude 6). The demons Jesus cast out begged him not to send them there (Luke 8:31). The beast comes up from it (11:7; 17:8), and Satan is thrown into it (20:3).
- **"Smoke like the smoke of a great furnace, and the sun and the air were darkened" (v2).** The phrase recalls the smoke over Sodom (Gen 19:28) and over Sinai (Exod 19:18). The darkness echoes the ninth plague. What comes out of the pit hides the light.
- **"From the smoke came locusts on the earth… power like the power of scorpions" (v3).** Locust swarms were one of the most feared disasters in the ancient world. They could cover the sky and strip a region bare in hours, bringing famine. The eighth plague on Egypt was locusts (Exod 10:12–15). The book of Joel is built around a locust invasion pictured as an army on the day of the Lord (Joel 1–2), and this chapter borrows heavily from it. But these locusts don't eat plants. They sting people, like scorpions, whose sting causes intense pain but is seldom deadly.
- **"Only those people who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads" (v4).** This is why chapter 7 came first. God's sealed servants are untouched, as Israel was spared in Goshen during the plagues (Exod 8:22; 9:4, 26).
- **"Torment them for five months, but not to kill them" (v5).** Five months was roughly the life cycle of a locust, the length of the season from spring into late summer. It marks a limited time. The limit is God's: "they were allowed."
- **"People will seek death and will not find it" (v6).** The pain is so great that death would feel like relief, but it is withheld (Job 3:21; Jer 8:3). This is torment meant to bring people to their senses, not yet the final judgment.
- **"The locusts were like horses prepared for battle" (vv7–10).** Notice the repeated "like" (nine times in four verses). John describes something beyond ordinary experience by comparing it to familiar things. The pieces come from Joel 2:4–6: "their appearance is like the appearance of horses… like warriors." Each detail adds to the horror: gold-looking crowns (they seem to conquer), human faces (intelligent), women's hair (perhaps long antennae, or wild flowing hair), lions' teeth (Joel 1:6), iron breastplates (unstoppable), and wings that roar like chariots. The Italian word for locust, *cavalletta*, means "little horse," and the German *Heupferd* means "hay horse." Locusts' heads really do look like tiny horses' heads.
- **Making sense of the locusts.** The Tyndale notes warn against reading them as attack helicopters or modern weapons, since they come up from the abyss, not from a factory. Guzik agrees, calling such guesses speculation. He takes them as demons. Historicists saw the Arab armies of the 600s: turbaned riders with long hair, from the land of locusts. JFB raises that view but notes a problem: many true believers died in those invasions, and these locusts can't touch God's people.
- **"They have as king over them the angel of the bottomless pit. His name in Hebrew is Abaddon, and in Greek he is called Apollyon" (v11).** Natural locusts "have no king" (Prov 30:27), but these do. *Abaddon* means "destruction," and in the Old Testament it is a name for the place of the dead, paired with Sheol and death (Job 26:6; 28:22; Ps 88:11). *Apollyon* means "Destroyer." John gives both names so that Jewish and Greek readers alike would understand. Many see a jab at Rome here. The god Apollo, whose name sounds like Apollyon, had the locust among his symbols, and emperors including Domitian liked to be associated with him. The Tyndale notes mention this link. The one the emperors honored is unmasked as the destroyer. Most take the king as Satan or one of his chief demons, though the text doesn't say which.
- **"The first woe has passed; behold, two woes are still to come" (v12).** It is a short breath and a warning: it gets worse.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the star "had fallen already" and is Satan, and the pit is "the orifice of the hell where Satan and his demons dwell." On the locusts it concludes: "they are not natural locusts, for these do not sting men." Henry, as a historicist, reports that "Some understand it of Boniface the third bishop of Rome," the fallen church leader who opens the way to "the antichrist." He reads the locusts as false teaching, writing that "Heresy is a poison in the soul, working slowly and secretly." He comforts believers that God's grace "will preserve his people from total and final apostasy." Guzik argues that the key being *given* shows Satan isn't the ruler of hell but will be its prisoner. He leaves open whether the star is the same as the king of v11.

#### The Sixth Trumpet: Four Angels Released at the Euphrates (vv13–21)
- **"A voice from the four horns of the golden altar before God" (v13).** This is the altar of incense where the saints' prayers were offered (8:3–5). The horns were raised corner projections on Israelite altars; archaeologists have found altars like this. This judgment also comes as an answer to the cry of God's people. JFB notes that the voice is "one," not four.
- **"Release the four angels who are bound at the great river Euphrates" (v14).** These angels are *bound*, which suggests they are evil powers held in check (contrast the four angels "holding back" the winds in 7:1). The Euphrates was the eastern border of the promised land (Gen 15:18) and the edge of the Roman world. Beyond it lay the Parthians, mounted archers who had defeated Roman armies and were the empire's great fear. In Israel's history, the invaders came from beyond the Euphrates: Assyria and Babylon. "The river" meant the place from which judgment comes (Isa 8:7; Jer 46:10). It appears again when the sixth bowl dries it up "for the kings from the east" (16:12).
- **"Prepared for the hour, the day, the month, and the year" (v15).** There is one definite article for the whole phrase in Greek: "*the* hour and day and month and year." It means a moment set in advance by God, down to the hour, not a span of time to add up. Historicists did add it up, by the "day for a year" method. JFB gives the classic result: 391 years and one month, from the Turks' first conquests over Christian lands to their high point, while also calling such calculations uncertain. Most modern interpreters of every view read the phrase simply as God's exact timing.
- **"To kill a third of mankind" (v15).** The locusts could only torment. These may kill. The judgments are rising in strength.
- **"Twice ten thousand times ten thousand; I heard their number" (v16).** That is 200 million. Ten thousand (*myrias*, the source of "myriad") was the largest number the Greeks had a single word for, so "myriads of myriads" means a host beyond counting (compare 5:11; Ps 68:17). The Tyndale notes point out that the whole Roman army at its height was perhaps 125,000 men. Guzik weighs a symbolic number and a human army, and settles on a literal number of demonic horsemen. Some popular teachers have linked it to modern China's armed forces, which the Tyndale notes say is not a useful way to read the number.
- **"Breastplates the color of fire and of sapphire and of sulfur" (v17).** Red, dark blue and yellow match the fire, smoke and sulfur that pour from the horses' mouths. "Sulfur" (older versions: "brimstone") is the burning substance of Sodom's judgment (Gen 19:24) and of the lake of fire (19:20; 21:8). Lion heads and serpent tails make them something like the monsters of Greek myth.
- **"By these three plagues a third of mankind was killed" (v18).** Fire, smoke and sulfur are called plagues, the word used for the plagues of Egypt.
- **"Their tails are like serpents with heads, and by means of them they wound" (v19).** The serpent links this army with "that ancient serpent… the devil" (12:9). They destroy coming and going, from mouth and tail.
- **"The rest of mankind… did not repent of the works of their hands nor give up worshiping demons and idols" (v20).** This is the verse the chapter has been building toward. The purpose of the warning trumpets was repentance, and it failed, like Pharaoh hardening his heart through ten plagues. The list of idols, "gold and silver and bronze and stone and wood, which cannot see or hear or walk," echoes Psalm 115:4–7 and Daniel 5:23. Paul says that behind idols stand demons (1 Cor 10:20). People keep worshiping the very powers that have been tormenting them.
- **"Nor did they repent of their murders or their sorceries or their sexual immorality or their thefts" (v21).** False worship leads to wrong living. The list follows the second half of the Ten Commandments: murder, adultery, theft. "Sorceries" is *pharmakeia* in many manuscripts, while the critical text has the related *pharmakōn*, "drugs" or "potions." The lexicon gives the sense as the use of drugs or spells, poisoning, and then sorcery or witchcraft. In the ancient world, potions, charms and magic went together. The word is the root of "pharmacy." It doesn't mean medicine in our sense; it means trying to control the world through occult means.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the Turks in this vision: "Here the Turkish power had its rise, which seems to be the story of this vision." He passes on Mede's guess that the fire and brimstone were "a prediction of great guns." Yet he admits that "Prophetic characters of time are hardly to be understood by us." On v20 he writes that people "would not cast away their images, though they could do them no good," and he applies v21 to the church of Rome in his own day. JFB, after describing the Turkish reading and the gunpowder theory, says plainly, "All this is very doubtful," and leans toward an army that is "probably infernal, though constrained to work out God's will." On v20 it compares Pharaoh "hardening his heart against repentance notwithstanding the plagues." Guzik notes that the voice comes from the altar of prayer, as in 8:3–5. He also sees the sins of v21 as a fair description of the modern world, linking "sorceries" with drug use. The lexicon allows the drug sense alongside sorcery. The Tyndale notes add that the four angels' being *bound* shows they are evil.

#### Pointing to Christ
Chapter 9 shows what the world looks like when God lets evil off the leash: torment from the abyss and death from the Euphrates. But even here, the key is given, the angels are released only at God's hour, and the sealed servants of God cannot be touched. The destroyer has a name, and so does the Deliverer. Abaddon and Apollyon mean "destruction" and "Destroyer," while the name Jesus means "the LORD saves." He alone holds the keys of the abyss and of death (1:18; 20:1–3). The tragedy of the chapter is not that people suffer, but that they will not turn. The judgments are meant to drive the world to the Lamb, and the door stays open while the trumpets still sound.
