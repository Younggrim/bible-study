*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). From 16:18 to 18:22 Moses sets out the offices that will lead Israel in the land: judges, priests, the king and the prophet. Chapter 16 called for honest judges in every town and forbade Canaanite objects beside God's altar. Chapter 17 finishes that thought with a ban on defective sacrifices, then shows how a town must handle a charge of idolatry, where hard cases go when local judges cannot decide them, and what kind of king Israel may have if it asks for one. Israel would not have a king for another three centuries or so. Moses is preparing them for it ahead of time, and the rules he gives are aimed squarely at the temptations of power.

#### Forbidden Forms of Worship (vv1–7)
- **"An ox or a sheep in which is a blemish" (v1).** The ESV heading runs on from 16:21, so this verse belongs with the Asherah and the pillar: all three corrupt the worship at God's altar. A sick or damaged animal was worth little. Offering it would cost the worshiper almost nothing while pretending to give God something (15:21; Lev 22:17–25). **"An abomination"** (*to'evah*) is strong language, the word used for idolatry and child sacrifice (12:31). Centuries later Malachi rebuked priests who brought blind and lame animals: "Present that to your governor; will he accept you?" (Mal 1:8).
- **"A man or woman who does what is evil… in transgressing his covenant" (v2).** Idolatry is described first as covenant-breaking. Israel had bound itself to the LORD alone at Sinai, and worshiping another god was like adultery in a marriage. "Man or woman" makes clear that no one is exempt. Chapter 13 dealt with people who lure others into idolatry. This case is a person who simply practices it.
- **"The sun or the moon or any of the host of heaven, which I have forbidden" (v3).** Star worship was the great religion of Mesopotamia, and Moses had already warned against it (4:19). The Tyndale notes say "host of heaven" can mean the sun, moon and stars and also heavenly beings. Later kings of Judah set up altars to "all the host of heaven" in the temple courts (2 Kgs 21:3–5), and Josiah tore them down (2 Kgs 23:4–5).
- **"You shall inquire diligently, and if it is true and certain" (v4).** Before anyone could be punished, a rumor had to be investigated. The language repeats 13:14. "True and certain" is a high standard of proof. Mob justice and gossip were ruled out.
- **"Bring out to your gates… and you shall stone that man or woman" (v5).** The trial happened at the gate, where the elders sat, and the execution outside the town. The Tyndale notes point out that ancient towns were small and crowded, and the open space at the gate was the town's public square and courtroom.
- **"On the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses" (v6).** One witness was never enough to put a person to death. The rule is repeated for all crimes in 19:15 and for murder in Numbers 35:30. Jesus applied it to church discipline (Matt 18:16), and Paul to charges against an elder (1 Tim 5:19; 2 Cor 13:1). At Jesus' own trial the witnesses could not get their stories to agree (Mark 14:55–59), so even by this law the case against him should have collapsed.
- **"The hand of the witnesses shall be first against him" (v7).** The witnesses had to throw the first stones. Anyone who lied would carry the guilt of a death with his own hands, which was a strong check on false accusation. That is the background to Jesus' words to the men who brought the woman caught in adultery: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her" (John 8:7). **"So you shall purge the evil from your midst."** The refrain appears again and again in Deuteronomy (13:5; 19:19; 21:21). Paul quotes it when he tells the Corinthians to put an unrepentant man out of the church (1 Cor 5:13). The church does not use the sword, but it is still called to deal seriously with open sin.
- **Commentators:** All three press v1 as a call to give God the best. Guzik connects it with David's refusal to offer God what cost him nothing (2 Sam 24:24). Henry sees the unblemished sacrifice as pointing to Christ's spotless offering and warns, "Let all who in our day set up idols in their hearts, remember how God punished this crime in Israel." JFB says "no rank or sex could palliate this crime." Guzik admits that a strict two-witness rule meant some guilty people would escape human courts, leaving final justice to God, and he notes that physical evidence could also serve as a witness.

#### Legal Decisions by Priests and Judges (vv8–13)
- **"Any case… that is too difficult for you" (v8).** "Too difficult" is the verb *pala'*, which usually describes something wonderful, beyond reach (it is the root of "Wonderful" in Isa 9:6). Here it means a case beyond the local judges' ability. Moses gives three kinds of examples: **"between one kind of homicide and another"** (murder or accident, see ch 19), **"one kind of legal right and another"** (disputes over rights and property), and **"one kind of assault and another"** (injuries, literally "stroke and stroke").
- **"Go up to the place that the LORD your God will choose" (v8).** Hard cases went to a central court at the sanctuary. This develops what Moses did in the wilderness, when he heard the hard cases himself and the appointed leaders the simple ones (1:17; Exod 18:22, 26). Jehoshaphat later set up just such a court in Jerusalem, with Levites, priests and heads of families (2 Chr 19:8–11).
- **"The Levitical priests and… the judge who is in office in those days" (v9).** The court had both priests, who knew the law and taught it (33:10), and a lay judge. The Tyndale notes say either kind of official could rule on religious or civil matters. "In those days" means whoever held the office at the time, so the system did not depend on one great man like Moses.
- **"You shall not turn aside… either to the right hand or to the left" (v11).** The verdict was final. The image is a straight road, and the same phrase is used for the king in v20 and for Israel's whole life under the law (5:32; 28:14).
- **"The man who acts presumptuously… that man shall die" (v12).** "Presumptuously" (*zadon*) means with arrogance, deliberately defying authority. This is not someone who lost his case and was disappointed, but someone who openly refuses to submit to the court God set up. Without a final court, every dispute would become a blood feud. **"All the people shall hear and fear"** (v13): the punishment was meant to teach the nation that God's justice is not optional.
- **Is this a blank check for leaders?** The court's authority was not unlimited. Its rulings were to be "according to the instructions" of the law (v11), and the priests were themselves under that law (v18). The prophets later condemned priests and judges who twisted justice for money (Mic 3:11).
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws on Thompson for the link between the verb and the word for "wonderful," and connects the homicide cases with the cities of refuge (Num 35; Josh 20). Henry notes that the court's rulings were not new revelation but the judgment of wise and experienced men, backed by God's promise. JFB says the central tribunal's decision was final and that defying it was treated as a capital crime.

#### Laws Concerning Israel's Kings (vv14–20)
- **"I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me" (v14).** Moses foresees the very words Israel would use in Samuel's day: "Now appoint for us a king to judge us like all the nations" (1 Sam 8:5). Wanting to be like everyone else was the problem then, and God told Samuel, "they have rejected me from being king over them" (1 Sam 8:7).
- **"You may indeed set a king over you whom the LORD your God will choose" (v15).** So was kingship good or bad? Faithful readers have weighed it both ways. God had promised Abraham that kings would come from him (Gen 17:6, 16), and Jacob's blessing gave the scepter to Judah (Gen 49:10). The Tyndale notes take kingship as part of God's plan, with only its abuses condemned. JFB reads v14 as permission, not approval, pointing to God's displeasure in 1 Samuel 8. Both agree on the main point: the king must be God's choice. Saul was the people's choice, a tall man who looked the part (1 Sam 9:2). David was God's choice, "a man after his own heart" (1 Sam 13:14; 16:7).
- **"One from among your brothers… you may not put a foreigner over you" (v15).** The king was to be an Israelite, a brother under the same covenant, not a foreign ruler with other gods. Herod the Great, an Idumean, was hated partly because he was not truly one of them.
- **"He must not acquire many horses for himself" (v16).** Horses and chariots were the tanks of the ancient world. A king who piled them up would trust in his army instead of in God (20:1; Ps 20:7: "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God"). **"Or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses."** Egypt was famous for its horses. Buying them meant trade treaties, and treaties drew Israel back toward the land God had brought them out of. Isaiah later warned, "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses" (Isa 31:1). **"You shall never return that way again"** echoes Exodus 14:13, and 28:68 threatens a return to Egypt as the final curse.
- **"He shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away" (v17).** Kings married many wives to seal alliances with other nations. Each foreign wife brought her own gods. The Tyndale notes say the verse limits polygamy without approving or banning it outright. The warning is about the heart.
- **"Nor shall he acquire for himself excessive silver and gold" (v17).** Wealth would make the king proud and independent of God, and it usually came from heavy taxes on his people (1 Sam 8:11–17).
- **Solomon broke all three.** He had thousands of horses and imported them from Egypt (1 Kgs 4:26; 10:26–29). He received 666 talents of gold in one year and made silver "as common in Jerusalem as stone" (1 Kgs 10:14, 27). And he had 700 wives and 300 concubines, and "his wives turned away his heart after other gods" (1 Kgs 11:3–4), almost the exact words of this verse. The kingdom split in his son's day (1 Kgs 11:11–13; 12). The wisest man in Israel knew this law and still drifted from it one step at a time.
- **"He shall write for himself in a book a copy of this law" (v18).** The king was to copy the law by hand, from the priests' official copy. The phrase "a copy of this law" is *mishneh hattorah ha-zo't*. The Greek translators rendered it *to deuteronomion touto*, "this second law," and from that phrase the book got its English name, Deuteronomy. "This law" most likely means Deuteronomy itself, or at least its laws. Samuel later wrote down "the rights and duties of the kingship" in a book and laid it before the LORD (1 Sam 10:25), and at Joash's coronation the priest handed the young king "the testimony" (2 Kgs 11:12).
- **"He shall read in it all the days of his life" (v19).** Every other king of the ancient world was above the law. Israel's king was to sit under it, and to read it daily. The reading had a purpose: **"that he may learn to fear the LORD his God"** and keep it. Its fruit would be humility, **"that his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers"** (v20). The king was still a brother. When Josiah heard the book of the law read, he tore his clothes and led the nation in repentance (2 Kgs 22:8–13; 23:1–3).
- **"So that he may continue long in his kingdom, he and his children" (v20).** The promise looks ahead to a dynasty. Obedience would bring a lasting house, the promise God later made to David (2 Sam 7:12–16).
- **Commentators:** All three see horses, wives and wealth as the king's great temptations, and all three point to Solomon. Henry: "Riches, honours, and pleasures, are three great hinderances of godliness." On the copy of the law: "It is not enough to have Bibles, but we must use them, use them daily, as long as we live," and "The king's writing and reading were as nothing, if he did not practise what he wrote and read." Guzik stresses that each command is about excess, which is exactly what makes it easy to explain away, and he names power, pleasure and money as the traps that still catch leaders. JFB thinks the horses were a danger mainly because they led to foreign entanglements, especially with Egypt, and reads v20 as implying that the crown would pass from father to son unless forfeited.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every part of this chapter finds its answer in Jesus. He is the unblemished sacrifice, "a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19). He was condemned on false testimony that did not agree, and he himself said, "let him who is without sin… be the first to throw a stone." He is the king God chose, from among his brothers (Heb 2:11–12), who did not multiply horses but rode into Jerusalem on a donkey (Matt 21:5), who had nowhere to lay his head, and who did not lift his heart above his brothers but "humbled himself" to death on a cross (Phil 2:8). He is the king who did not merely copy the law but could say, "your law is within my heart" (Ps 40:8). His kingdom will not end (Luke 1:32–33).
