*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC, for Jews resettling Judah under Persian rule. Chapters 2–3 traced Judah's main line to David and David's line to the Chronicler's own day. This chapter goes back and gathers up the rest of Judah's clans (vv1–23), and then turns to Simeon (vv24–43), the small tribe whose land lay inside Judah's southern territory. Much of it is a list of family and town names whose exact links are now hard to follow, and the Chronicler himself admits that "the records are ancient" (v22). But two short stories break through the lists: the prayer of a man named Jabez, and the Simeonites' search for pasture. Both are about seeking room to live, and finding it.

#### Descendants of Judah (vv1–23)
- **"The sons of Judah: Perez, Hezron, Carmi, Hur, and Shobal" (v1).** These are not five brothers but five generations, a line of descent (2:4–5, 9, 19, 50). Hebrew "sons" can mean descendants. Carmi is likely a copying slip for Caleb (Chelubai, 2:9), since Hur was Caleb's son.
- **"The clans of the Zorathites" (v2).** The people of Zorah, Samson's hometown in the foothills west of Jerusalem (Judg 13:2).
- **"Hazzelelponi" (v3).** One of the few women named in these lists. Nothing is known of her except her name, and that is something. She was remembered.
- **"Hur, the firstborn of Ephrathah, the father of Bethlehem" (v4).** Ephrathah was the old name of the Bethlehem area: "But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah… from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel" (Mic 5:2). The town of David and of Jesus is rooted in this family. "Father of Bethlehem" means founder or chief of the town.
- **"Ashhur, the father of Tekoa, had two wives" (vv5–7).** Tekoa sits on the edge of the wilderness south of Bethlehem, later the home of the wise woman (2 Sam 14:2) and of the prophet Amos.
- **"Jabez was more honorable than his brothers" (v9).** In the middle of a list of names, the Chronicler stops to tell one man's story, in two verses. Who his family was is not even clear. He is simply set apart as "more honorable," which in Hebrew carries the sense of weight, honor, respect. There was also a town called Jabez where clans of scribes lived (2:55), possibly named for him.
- **"His mother called his name Jabez, saying, 'Because I bore him in pain'" (v9).** The name *Yabets* sounds like a scrambled form of *otseb*, "pain" or "sorrow." It is a wordplay, the letters of "pain" shuffled into a name. A name in Israel was thought to say something about a person's life, so this boy carried "pain" with him wherever he went. The Tyndale notes connect it with the pain of childbirth spoken of in Eden (Gen 3:16, the same root).
- **"Jabez called upon the God of Israel" (v10).** He prays to the God of Israel, the God who had made covenant promises to his people, not to a generic deity or a household god.
- **"Oh that you would bless me and enlarge my border" (v10).** "Border" means the boundaries of land. In a tribe whose families had each received an allotment of land (Josh 15), more land meant more room for a family to live and prosper. He asks God to do what God had promised to do for Israel: "When the LORD your God enlarges your territory, as he has promised you" (Deut 12:20).
- **"That your hand might be with me" (v10).** The hand of God means his power at work. It can be a hand of judgment (1 Sam 5:6), but Jabez asks for it to be *with* him, guiding and protecting, as "the good hand of his God was on him" is said of Ezra (Ezra 7:9) and Nehemiah (Neh 2:8). The Chronicler's readers knew those phrases.
- **"That you would keep me from harm so that it might not bring me pain!" (v10).** The last word is the same "pain" (*otseb*) that is hidden in his name. He asks God not to let his name become his life. The Hebrew could also mean "so that I might not cause pain," which the Tyndale notes and Guzik both mention. Either way, he asks to escape what his name predicted.
- **"And God granted what he asked" (v10).** The whole story ends in four words. No reason is given except that he prayed and God answered. The Chronicler's book will make this a pattern: those who seek God and call on him are heard (2 Chr 7:14; 14:11; 20:3–17; 33:12–13).
- **How to read the prayer.** Around the year 2000 the prayer of Jabez became widely popular, sometimes treated as a formula to be prayed daily for blessing and success. The text itself does not present it as a technique. It records an honorable man who asked boldly for the blessings God had already promised his people, and a God who listened. Its lesson is simpler and deeper: an unknown man with a painful start in life turned to God, and his story changed.
- **Kenaz, Othniel and the valley of craftsmen (vv13–14).** **Othniel** was Israel's first judge, who rescued Israel from a Mesopotamian king (Judg 3:9–11) and married Achsah, Caleb's daughter (Josh 15:17). **Ge-harashim**, "Valley of Craftsmen," was named for the skilled workers who lived there. It appears again after the exile as a place resettled by returning Benjaminites (Neh 11:35).
- **"Caleb the son of Jephunneh" (v15).** This is the famous Caleb, the faithful spy who said Israel could take the land (Num 13:30; 14:24) and who, at 85, asked for the hill country of Hebron (Josh 14:6–14). He was a Kenizzite, from a clan with roots outside Israel (Gen 15:19; 36:11) that had been fully joined to Judah. He is a different man from Caleb son of Hezron in chapter 2.
- **"Bithiah, the daughter of Pharaoh, whom Mered married" (v17).** An Egyptian princess, or at least a woman of the royal household, married into Judah. When this happened is not known. It may have been during Israel's centuries in Egypt, as Guzik (following Payne) suggests. Her name means "daughter of the LORD" (*Bat-yah*), which led JFB to conclude she had become a worshiper of Israel's God. That is a reasonable guess from the name. Her daughter was named **Miriam**, like Moses' sister. **"His Judahite wife"** (v18) shows Mered also had an Israelite wife. The Hebrew order of vv17–18 is jumbled, and translations rearrange it.
- **Keilah, Eshtemoa, Soco, Zanoah (vv17–19).** Towns in the foothills and hill country of Judah. **Keilah** is the town David rescued from the Philistines, which then nearly betrayed him to Saul (1 Sam 23:1–13).
- **The sons of Shelah (vv21–23).** Shelah was the third son of Judah, the one promised to Tamar (Gen 38:11). His line is listed last, after the line through Tamar's sons. His descendants were **"linen workers at Beth-ashbea"** (v21) and **"potters… in the king's service"** (v23). Skilled trades ran in families, and whole clans were known by their work.
- **"Who ruled in Moab and returned to Lehem" (v22).** The Hebrew is obscure. It may mean "who married into Moab" or "who ruled in Moab," and "Lehem" may be a shortened form of Bethlehem. Some have connected this with the family of Elimelech and Naomi in Ruth 1, who went from Bethlehem to Moab and returned. That is an attractive guess but no more. **"Now the records are ancient"** is the Chronicler's honest note: his sources were very old.
- **"These were the potters… They lived there in the king's service" (v23).** Netaim and Gederah were in the foothills (the Shephelah), good clay country. Archaeologists have found hundreds of storage jar handles from Judah, mostly from Hezekiah's reign, stamped *lmlk*, "belonging to the king." They show royal pottery production on a large scale in this very region, which fits this verse well.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees Jabez as proof that "The way to be truly great, is to seek to do God's will, and to pray earnestly." He reads the prayer as an implied vow, "Lord, if thou wilt bless me and keep me, do what thou wilt with me," and says, "Spiritual blessings are the best blessings." JFB reads the last request as "Let me not experience the grief which my name implies." Guzik notes that many people have blessings without being blessed, and that God's "hand" in Scripture is often a hand of judgment, which makes Jabez's request for it to be with him striking. Guzik also draws on Spurgeon's sermon on v23: the potters "lived there with the king for his work," and those who serve the King should stay near him. JFB thinks Bithiah "had become a convert to the worship of the God of Israel," and takes the craftsmen of v14 to be "probably… carpenters."

#### Descendants of Simeon (vv24–43)
- **"The sons of Simeon" (v24).** Simeon was Jacob's second son. With Levi he massacred the men of Shechem to avenge his sister Dinah (Gen 34:25–30), and Jacob's last words over them were a curse: "I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel" (Gen 49:7). Levi was scattered as a blessing, becoming the priestly tribe living in towns across Israel. Simeon was scattered by shrinking and being absorbed into Judah.
- **"His brothers did not have many children, nor did all their clan multiply like the men of Judah" (v27).** Simeon's fighting men numbered 59,300 at the first census (Num 1:23) and only 22,200 at the second, forty years later (Num 26:14), the steepest decline of any tribe. Many link that drop to the plague after the sin at Baal-peor, where the leader who flaunted his sin was a Simeonite prince (Num 25:6–14). Moses' final blessing on the tribes does not mention Simeon at all (Deut 33).
- **"Beersheba, Moladah, Hazar-shual… Ziklag" (vv28–31).** These towns were in the Negeb, the dry south, inside Judah's borders. Joshua 19:1–9 explains that Simeon's inheritance "was in the midst of the inheritance of the people of Judah," because Judah's share was too large for it. **"These were their cities until David reigned"** (v31) suggests that from David's time these towns were reckoned as Judah's. Ziklag, for example, was given to David by the Philistine king Achish and "has belonged to the kings of Judah to this day" (1 Sam 27:6).
- **"They kept a genealogical record" (v33).** Simeon's families had records. The Chronicler is drawing on actual documents.
- **The princes and their search for pasture (vv34–40).** Thirteen clan leaders are named, and **"their fathers' houses increased greatly."** Growing flocks needed room. They went to **"the entrance of Gedor"** and found **"rich, good pasture… broad, quiet, and peaceful."** The Greek translation reads Gerar, a region in the western Negeb known from the patriarchs (Gen 20:1; 26:1–6), and many scholars prefer that reading. **"The former inhabitants there belonged to Ham"** means they were Egyptians or Canaanites (1:8).
- **"In the days of Hezekiah, king of Judah" (v41).** Hezekiah (about 715–686 BC) defeated the Philistines "as far as Gaza" (2 Kings 18:8), and this push into the southwest may have been part of the same period. The **Meunites** were desert people, probably from the region of Maon or Ma'an southeast of the Dead Sea, whom Uzziah had also fought (2 Chr 26:7). **"Marked them for destruction"** is the language of *cherem*, the old law of devoting a people entirely to God, last famously applied to the Amalekites in Saul's day (1 Sam 15:3).
- **"Five hundred men of the Simeonites went to Mount Seir" (v42).** Seir is the mountain country of Edom, south and east of the Dead Sea. **"They defeated the remnant of the Amalekites who had escaped" (v43).** The Amalekites had attacked Israel in the wilderness, and God had sworn war against them "from generation to generation" (Exod 17:14–16). Saul failed to finish the task (1 Sam 15:9). David defeated them, but four hundred escaped on camels (1 Sam 30:17). Now, in Hezekiah's time, the last remnant is defeated by Simeon, of all tribes, the shrunken tribe from Jacob's curse.
- **"They have lived there to this day" (v43).** The Chronicler is copying from a source written before the exile. He keeps its words even though, by his time, the situation had surely changed.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads Simeon's story as one of a curse working itself out: no territory of its own, only towns inside Judah, a population that shrank, and later families driven to wander in search of pasture. He contrasts this with Levi, whose scattering became a blessing. JFB, on v27, points back to the census figures in Numbers 1 and 26, explains differences in names by noting that "some persons had more than one name," and describes the Simeonites who "effected by force a settlement on Mount Seir." The Tyndale notes connect the expansion to Hezekiah's campaign against the Philistines (2 Kings 18:8).

#### Pointing to Christ
Jabez was born under a name that meant pain, and he asked God to keep that pain from defining him. God answered. The gospel goes further. Christ took our pain on himself: "Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows" (Isa 53:4). His people are given a new name (Rev 2:17; 3:12). And in the end, God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes… neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore" (Rev 21:4).
