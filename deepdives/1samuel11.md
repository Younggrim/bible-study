*Setting:* Around 1050 BC, soon after Saul was chosen king by lot at Mizpah (10:17–27). Saul has gone back to farming at Gibeah, and Israel has no capital, no palace and no standing army. Some "worthless fellows" sneered, "How can this man save us?" (10:27). Chapter 11 answers them. The threat comes from the east, across the Jordan, where the Ammonites lived around their capital Rabbah (modern Amman in Jordan). They were distant relatives of Israel, descended from Lot (Gen 19:38), and had long claimed the land of Gilead (Judg 11:12–13). Samuel later reveals that the Ammonite threat was one reason Israel demanded a king (12:12). This is the battle that turns the reluctant king into a national leader.

#### Saul Defeats the Ammonites (vv1–11)
- **"Nahash the Ammonite went up and besieged Jabesh-gilead" (v1).** Jabesh-gilead was a town in Gilead east of the Jordan, roughly 20 miles southeast of the Sea of Galilee. "Nahash" is spelled like the Hebrew word for "serpent," though as a name it may simply mean something else. Jabesh had a painful history with Israel and a special tie to Benjamin. When the tribes nearly wiped out Benjamin after the outrage at Gibeah, Jabesh had refused to join the war, and Israel destroyed the town and gave four hundred of its young women as wives to the surviving Benjaminites (Judg 21:8–14). Many in Saul's tribe, and perhaps in his own family, may have been descended from Jabesh.
- **A Dead Sea Scroll's extra paragraph.** One Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel has an extra paragraph before this verse (see the end of chapter 10). It says Nahash had already been gouging out the right eyes of the Israelites of Gad and Reuben, and that 7,000 men had fled to Jabesh. If that is original, it explains why Nahash came to Jabesh and why his demand was so specific. Josephus also knew the tradition, and the Greek translation begins this chapter "about a month later."
- **"Make a treaty with us, and we will serve you" (v1).** Jabesh offers to surrender and become a vassal town, paying tribute. They do not expect help from the other tribes, and in the days of the judges, help rarely came.
- **"That I gouge out all your right eyes, and thus bring disgrace on all Israel" (v2).** Mutilating prisoners was a known practice in the ancient Near East. It made them unfit for battle, since a soldier holding a shield on his left covered his left eye and aimed with his right. More than that, it was meant as a public humiliation of the whole nation. The Philistines did something similar to Samson (Judg 16:21).
- **"Give us seven days' respite… if there is no one to save us" (v3).** Nahash agrees. He is so sure Israel cannot unite that he lets them advertise their helplessness throughout the land. Every messenger carries his insult to "all Israel." "No one to save us" is the language of the book of Judges, where Israel cried out and God raised up a savior (Judg 3:9, 15).
- **"When the messengers came to Gibeah of Saul… all the people wept aloud" (v4).** Did they come to Gibeah because Saul was there? The text does not say so. They tell the people, not the king, and the people only weep. JFB notes that "the appeal was made to the people, not to the prince."
- **"Saul was coming from the field behind the oxen" (v5).** Israel's new king is still plowing. He has to ask what the crying is about. It is a humble picture, like Gideon threshing wheat (Judg 6:11) or Elisha plowing when Elijah called him (1 Kgs 19:19).
- **"The Spirit of God rushed upon Saul when he heard these words, and his anger was greatly kindled" (v6).** The same verb (*tsalach*) is used for the Spirit coming on Samson (Judg 14:6, 19) and on Saul at Gibeath-elohim (10:10). Here the Spirit equips him to lead in war, just as with the judges. His anger is a righteous anger, burning at cruelty and at the disgrace being heaped on God's people, not at a personal insult.
- **"He took a yoke of oxen and cut them in pieces and sent them throughout all the territory of Israel" (v7).** This is a deliberate echo. In Judges 19:29 a Levite cut up the body of his murdered concubine and sent the pieces through Israel, after the atrocity at Gibeah, Saul's town. That call to arms led to war on Benjamin and the destruction of Jabesh. Now a man from Gibeah of Benjamin sends pieces through Israel to rescue Jabesh. The old wound is being healed. The threat is plain: whoever does not come, "so shall it be done to his oxen." Ancient treaties were sometimes sealed by cutting animals in pieces, with the understanding that a traitor would suffer the same (compare Jer 34:18).
- **"Whoever does not come out after Saul and Samuel" (v7).** Saul ties his call to Samuel's name. He is still new and needs the prophet's authority behind him.
- **"The dread of the LORD fell upon the people, and they came out as one man" (v7).** The dread is from God, not just fear of losing their oxen. "As one man" is the phrase from Judges 20:1, 8, 11, when Israel united against Benjamin. Now they unite behind a Benjaminite.
- **"At Bezek… three hundred thousand, and the men of Judah thirty thousand" (v8).** Bezek was west of the Jordan opposite Jabesh, about 14 miles away. The Hebrew word for "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a clan unit, so the actual numbers may have been smaller. The figures should not be pressed. The separate count for Judah hints at the later division of the kingdom into Israel and Judah (1 Kgs 12).
- **"Tomorrow, by the time the sun is hot, you shall have salvation" (v9).** "Salvation" (*teshu'ah*) here means rescue, deliverance in battle. The men of Jabesh "were glad."
- **"Tomorrow we will give ourselves up to you" (v10).** The men of Jabesh speak to Nahash with careful double meaning. "Give ourselves up" is literally "come out to you." Nahash hears surrender. They mean an attack. He relaxes, and is caught off guard.
- **"Saul put the people in three companies… in the morning watch" (v11).** Dividing an army into three groups was a standard tactic for a surprise attack, used by Gideon (Judg 7:16) and Abimelech (Judg 9:43). The "morning watch" was the last of the three night watches, roughly 2 a.m. to dawn. Saul must have crossed the Jordan and marched through the night. The Ammonites were "scattered, so that no two of them were left together," a complete rout. The siege is lifted before the sun gets hot, just as promised.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the loss of the right eye "would disqualify them for war," and calls it a punishment used in the East. JFB sees the small number from Judah as a sign that "disaffection to Saul was strongest in that tribe," though that is a guess. Henry notes Saul's good sense: "He numbered those that came in to him, that he might know his own strength." Guzik compares Nahash to Satan, who would rather partly blind God's people than lose them altogether, and he says Saul's anger was righteous because it was for the LORD's cause. He also calls the reply of the men of Jabesh a clever ambiguity that left Nahash unprepared. Guzik says Nahash means "serpent." The spelling matches, but as a personal name its meaning is uncertain. None of the three notices the echo of Judges 19 in the pieces of the oxen.

#### The Kingdom Is Renewed (vv12–15)
- **"Who is it that said, 'Shall Saul reign over us?' Bring the men, that we may put them to death" (v12).** Victory makes the crowd vengeful. The people turn to Samuel, not Saul, to demand that the scoffers of 10:27 be executed.
- **"Not a man shall be put to death this day, for today the LORD has worked salvation in Israel" (v13).** This is Saul at his best. He refuses to start his reign with bloodshed, and he gives the credit to the LORD. "Salvation" (*teshu'ah*) is the word of v9. Saul is the instrument, but the LORD is the Savior. Sadly, Saul will not stay like this. He will later order the massacre of the priests of Nob (22:17–19) and hunt David for years. David will show the same mercy to his own critics (2 Sam 19:22–23).
- **"Come, let us go to Gilgal and there renew the kingdom" (v14).** Gilgal, near Jericho, was where Israel first camped in the land, renewed the covenant through circumcision and Passover, and set up twelve memorial stones (Josh 4–5). "Renew" probably means confirming Saul's kingship publicly and with full support, now that he had proven himself. It may also mean renewing the covenant with God under the new form of government. Samuel's farewell speech in chapter 12 is given at this gathering.
- **"They made Saul king before the LORD in Gilgal" (v15).** This is the third stage of Saul's rise: anointed in private (10:1), chosen by lot in public (10:21), and now confirmed by the whole nation after a victory. "Before the LORD" is repeated twice in this verse. The kingdom is set up in God's presence and under his authority.
- **"There they sacrificed peace offerings… and there Saul and all the men of Israel rejoiced greatly" (v15).** Peace offerings were shared meals of fellowship with God (Lev 3; 7:11–15). It is the high point of Saul's reign. Things will never be this good for him again. The Greek translation says that Samuel too rejoiced, but the Hebrew names only Saul and the people. Perhaps that is a hint. Samuel's speech in chapter 12 is not all celebration.
- **Commentators:** Henry praises Saul's clemency: "If an enemy be made a friend, that will be more to our advantage than to have him slain," and says they gathered at Gilgal so "that God might have the praise of that which they had the comfort of." JFB says Saul showed himself "as great in clemency as in valor," and credits "the calm and sagacious counsel of Samuel" with leading the people to Gilgal. Guzik warns that victory is often the moment when division creeps in among God's people, and commends Saul for giving the glory to God. He also observes that Saul's real battles from now on would be inside him, against pride, insecurity and the desire for revenge.

#### Pointing to Christ
A cruel enemy offered a town peace on terms of permanent shame and blindness. The people had "no one to save" them. Then the Spirit came on God's anointed, he gathered his people, and he brought salvation "by the time the sun is hot." The pattern points to a greater rescue. Jesus, anointed by the Spirit, announced "recovering of sight to the blind" and "liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18), and he came to undo the work of the ancient serpent who blinds minds (2 Cor 4:4). Saul's mercy at the moment of victory is a faint picture of the King who prayed for those who mocked him (Luke 23:34). Saul said, "Today the LORD has worked salvation in Israel." Jesus said, "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9). His reign does not begin well and fade, as Saul's did. It will have no end (Luke 1:33).
