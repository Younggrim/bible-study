*Setting:* The far shore of the Red Sea, the morning after the crossing. Israel has just watched the sea close over Pharaoh's chariots and seen "the Egyptians dead on the seashore" (14:30). The people "feared the LORD, and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses" (14:31). Their first act as a free people is to sing. The Song of Moses is the first great hymn of the Bible, and most scholars think it is among the oldest poems in Hebrew. It looks back at the sea, then forward to the land and to God's dwelling among his people. Then the story moves on, and three days into the desert the song gives way to grumbling over bitter water, the first of many tests on the road to Sinai.

#### The Song of Moses (vv1–21)
- **"Then Moses and the people of Israel sang this song to the LORD" (v1).** Moses leads and the whole people joins in. The song begins, as Hebrew praise usually does, with the reason for it: **"for he has triumphed gloriously."** The Hebrew doubles the verb, "he is highly exalted," or "he has risen up in majesty." **"The horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea."** The chariot, Egypt's proudest weapon, is gone.
- **"The LORD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation" (v2).** "Song" may also mean "might" or "protection," and some translations take it that way. The line is quoted word for word in Psalm 118:14 and Isaiah 12:2, so it became one of Israel's standard confessions. "The LORD" here is the short form *Yah*, its first appearance in the Bible, the *-jah* in "hallelujah." **"Salvation"** is *yeshu'ah*, the word Moses used at the sea: "see the salvation of the LORD" (14:13).
- **"This is my God… my father's God" (v2).** The God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, known to Israel by report, is now known by experience. The Tyndale notes point out that the singular "my" runs through these opening lines: the whole people sings, but each person claims God as their own.
- **"The LORD is a man of war; the LORD is his name" (v3).** God fights for his people (14:14, 25). This is not cruelty or a love of bloodshed. He fights for the helpless against the oppressor. The same picture of God the warrior returns in the prophets (Isa 42:13; 59:16–17) and in Revelation, where Christ rides out "in righteousness he judges and makes war" (Rev 19:11).
- **"His chosen officers were sunk in the Red Sea" (v4).** "Chosen officers" is the same word as the "officers" over the chariots in 14:7. **"They went down into the depths like a stone" (v5).** Like a stone, and in v10 "like lead," Egypt's mighty army sinks without a struggle. The images grow heavier as the song goes on: stone, lead, stubble (v7).
- **"Your right hand, O LORD" (v6).** The right hand was a warrior's sword arm, the hand of strength. It is a human picture of divine power, and it runs through the Psalms (Ps 98:1; 118:15–16). Notice the repetition, "your right hand, O LORD… your right hand, O LORD." Hebrew poetry often climbs this way, saying a line and then stepping it up.
- **"You send out your fury; it consumes them like stubble" (v7).** Stubble is dry stalks left after harvest, the first thing fire eats. Egypt had made Israel gather stubble for bricks (5:12). Now Egypt is the stubble.
- **"At the blast of your nostrils the waters piled up" (v8).** The "east wind" of 14:21 is now pictured as God's own breath. The word for "blast" is *ruach*, wind, breath or spirit, the same word as the Spirit over the waters in Genesis 1:2. The poem shows what the prose only hinted at: **"the floods stood up in a heap; the deeps congealed in the heart of the sea."** "Congealed" means thickened or set, like curdled milk.
- **"The enemy said, 'I will pursue, I will overtake, I will divide the spoil…'" (v9).** Six short boasts tumble out in Hebrew, each one "I will." The poet lets us hear Pharaoh's greed: the plunder, the swords, his "desire" (literally his *nephesh*, his appetite) filling itself. **"You blew with your wind; the sea covered them" (v10).** One breath from God answers all six boasts.
- **"Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (v11).** This is the heart of the song. "Gods" is *elim*, mighty ones. The plagues were judgments "on all the gods of Egypt" (12:12), and none of them could stand. The question is not whether other gods are real rivals, but that nothing called a god can be compared with the LORD. **"Majestic in holiness"**: holiness means God's otherness, his complete moral and personal purity. The question echoes in later Scripture (Ps 86:8; Mic 7:18), and the name Michael means "Who is like God?"
- **"The earth swallowed them" (v12).** In Hebrew poetry "the earth" can stand for the underworld, the grave. The sea becomes their grave.
- **"You have led in your steadfast love the people whom you have redeemed" (v13).** The song turns from the past to the future. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal, covenant love, one of the great words of the Old Testament. "Redeemed" is *ga'al*, the word for a family member who buys back a relative from slavery or debt (Lev 25:47–49; Ruth 4). God has acted as Israel's next of kin (6:6). **"To your holy abode."** The goal of the exodus is not just escape but a home with God.
- **"The peoples have heard; they tremble" (vv14–15).** The song names the nations Israel will meet on the way: **Philistia** on the coast, **Edom** and **Moab** to the southeast, and **Canaan** itself. Their fear did come: forty years later Rahab tells the spies, "the fear of you has fallen upon us, and… all the inhabitants of the land melt away before you" (Josh 2:9–11), using the same "melt away." Edom and Moab feared Israel too (Num 20:14–21; 22:3). Mentioning the Philistines may reflect the name later readers knew for the coast; see 13:17.
- **"They are still as a stone, till your people, O LORD, pass by" (v16).** The enemies who "went down like a stone" (v5) will be frozen still like a stone while Israel passes. **"The people… whom you have purchased."** "Purchased" (*qanah*) means acquired, made your own. Israel belongs to God by redemption.
- **"You will bring them in and plant them on your own mountain" (v17).** A planting is permanent, a people rooted in their land (2 Sam 7:10; Ps 80:8). In the ancient world gods were thought to live on mountains. Here the "mountain" is the land God gives, and ultimately Mount Zion, where the temple would stand. **"The sanctuary, O Lord, which your hands have established."** God himself builds the place where he will live with his people.
- **"The LORD will reign forever and ever" (v18).** The song ends where everything ends: God is king. Pharaoh ruled for a lifetime. The LORD reigns forever. This is the first time the Bible speaks of God reigning as king.
- **"For when the horses of Pharaoh… went into the sea" (v19).** A one-verse prose summary bridges the song to Miriam's dance.
- **"Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron" (v20).** This is the first time Miriam is named. She is almost certainly the sister who watched over baby Moses at the Nile (2:4–8; Num 26:59). She is the first woman in the Bible called a "prophetess," and Micah later lists her with Moses and Aaron as leaders God sent (Mic 6:4). **"Took a tambourine in her hand, and all the women went out after her with tambourines and dancing."** Women led victory songs with hand drums and dancing (Judg 11:34; 1 Sam 18:6–7). The tambourine was a small frame drum, without the metal jingles of a modern one.
- **"And Miriam sang to them" (v21).** Her song repeats the opening line of Moses' song as a refrain. "To them" is masculine in Hebrew, so she may have been answering the men's chorus, the women and men singing back and forth.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "an ancient song, the most ancient that we know of," a holy song for God's honor. On v2 he says of God's people, "in him they have comfort, he is their song." The question of v11 "is a challenge to all other gods to compare with him," and of the coming conquest Henry says Moses "looks upon it as in effect finished already." He notes that "Miriam (or Mary, it is the same name) presided in an assembly of the women." JFB likewise calls it "the oldest poem in the world" by some centuries, says Miriam was called a prophetess chiefly for her gift of sacred song, and suggests from the masculine "them" in v21 that men and women sang in alternating choirs. JFB has no comments on vv2–19. Guzik notes that this praise was spontaneous and aimed at God, not at an audience, and that Israel sang because God had done what they could never do. He also counts the "right hand" of God as a frequent biblical image of skill and power, a human picture used to describe God.

#### Bitter Water Made Sweet (vv22–27)
- **"They went into the wilderness of Shur" (v22).** Shur means "wall," perhaps after a line of Egyptian border forts, and names the desert east of Egypt, in the northwest Sinai. Hagar fled this way (Gen 16:7). **"Three days in the wilderness and found no water."** Three days was what Moses had asked Pharaoh for (5:3), and it is about as long as people can last without water in desert heat.
- **"When they came to Marah… it was bitter" (v23).** *Marah* means "bitter." Water in the Sinai can be heavy with minerals and undrinkable. Many have identified Marah with the bitter spring called Ain Hawarah, but no site is certain. After the "sweet" song comes bitter water, a test of whether the people will sing in hardship too. Naomi later calls herself "Mara," because "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (Ruth 1:20).
- **"The people grumbled against Moses" (v24).** "Grumbled" (*lun*) becomes a key word for the wilderness years (16:2; 17:3; Num 14:2). Three days ago they sang "who is like you?" Now they ask **"What shall we drink?"** The question is fair; the grumbling is not. Moses shows the better way: **"he cried to the LORD" (v25).**
- **"The LORD showed him a log, and he threw it into the water" (v25).** The Hebrew word *'ets* means tree or wood. "Showed" is *yarah*, "taught" or "pointed out," the root behind *torah*, "instruction." God teaches Moses what to do. People have guessed at a plant that might sweeten water, but no such plant is known. The power came from God, not the wood. Elisha later heals Jericho's bad water with a bowl of salt (2 Kgs 2:19–22), another sign that the means were only signs.
- **"There the LORD made for them a statute and a rule, and there he tested them" (v25).** Before Sinai, God begins to shape Israel by his word. "Tested" (*nasah*) means proved, put to the test, as God tested Abraham (Gen 22:1). The wilderness was a school: God tested them "to know what was in your heart, whether you would keep his commandments" (Deut 8:2).
- **"If you will diligently listen to the voice of the LORD your God" (v26).** Four phrases pile up: listen, do what is right, give ear, keep his statutes. **"I will put none of the diseases on you that I put on the Egyptians."** The plagues, including the boils, are still fresh in memory. God who could strike with disease could also keep it away.
- **"For I am the LORD, your healer."** "Healer" is *rophe'*, from *rapha*, to heal, mend or restore, the word used for a physician. This is often quoted as the name *Yahweh Rophe*. It covers more than bodies; God heals the land (2 Chr 7:14) and broken hearts (Ps 147:3). Obeying God's law also protected health in practical ways, as later food and purity laws show, though the promise rests on God himself, not on hygiene. Scripture is also honest that faithful people still suffer illness (2 Kgs 13:14; 2 Cor 12:7–9). The promise is about God's character as healer, not a guarantee against every sickness.
- **"Then they came to Elim, where there were twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees" (v27).** *Elim* means "large trees" or "terebinths." After the test, rest. The numbers are striking. Twelve is the number of Israel's tribes, and seventy the number of Jacob's family who came into Egypt (1:5) and of the elders later chosen (24:1; Num 11:16). Whether or not the numbers are meant as symbols, the picture is of plenty: shade, water and rest for all. Many identify Elim with the oasis of Wadi Gharandel.
- **Commentators:** JFB is sure no known shrub sweetened the water and that God "miraculously endowed" the wood with that power, noting that a new statute here "would have been singularly inopportune if no miracle had been wrought." He places Marah at Howarah and Elim at Wady Ghurandel, "an oasis with palms." Henry's outline moves from "their discontent at the waters of Marah" to "the relief granted them" and then "their satisfaction in the waters of Elim," and he observes that God may let his people camp for a time by bitter water, but that will not always be their lot. Guzik reports scientific guesses about the tree and the water's minerals, which should be held loosely. His surer point is that God wanted to take Egypt out of Israel, not just Israel out of Egypt, and that God knows when to test and when to refresh. The Tyndale notes add that a pattern of complaining like this, if it hardens, ends in disaster, as it did for this same generation (Num 14; Heb 3:7–11).

#### Pointing to Christ
The Song of Moses is sung again at the end of the Bible. In Revelation 15:3, those who have conquered the beast stand beside a sea of glass and "sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb." Every rescue God worked for Israel is gathered up in the greater rescue Christ worked at the cross, where the true enemy, sin and death, was thrown down. And at Marah, bitter water was made sweet by a piece of wood. Many Christian writers since the early church have seen there a picture of the cross, the "tree" on which Christ bore our sins (1 Pet 2:24), which turns the bitterness of death into life. That is an application, not the passage's first meaning, but the God who names himself "your healer" here is the same God who, in Jesus, healed the sick and "by his wounds" heals his people (Isa 53:5; 1 Pet 2:24).
