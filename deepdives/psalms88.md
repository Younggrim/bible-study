*Setting:* The title is the longest in the Psalter: "A Song. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah. To the choirmaster: according to Mahalath Leannoth. A Maskil of Heman the Ezrahite." It joins a Korahite song collection with the name of one writer. Heman is probably the Levite singer whom David appointed over temple music, a grandson of the prophet Samuel (1 Chr 6:33; 15:17; 25:1–6), a man God blessed with fourteen sons and three daughters. A Heman is also named among the wisest men of Solomon's day, wise but not as wise as Solomon (1 Kgs 4:31), and "Ezrahite" may tie him to the family of Zerah in Judah (1 Chr 2:6). Whether these are one man or several is uncertain. *Mahalath* may be a tune or instrument, but it sounds like the word for "sickness," and *Leannoth* may mean "to afflict" or "for singing," so some read the title as "concerning sickness, for humbling." *Maskil* probably means a song that teaches. No event is named. Psalm 88 is often called the darkest psalm in the Bible, because it is the only lament that never turns to praise. Most laments move from complaint to confidence (Ps 13; 22; 86). This one opens with "God of my salvation" and closes on the word "darkness." It is in the Bible on purpose, and it teaches what to do when the light does not come back on.

#### I Cry Out Day and Night (vv1–2)
- **"O LORD, God of my salvation" (v1).** This is the only hopeful phrase in the psalm, and it comes first. Heman still calls the LORD by his covenant name and still calls him the God who saves, even though nothing in his life looks like rescue. Everything that follows is said to God, not about him. That alone is faith.
- **"I cry out day and night before you" (v1).** The cry is constant (compare 22:2). He is not giving up on prayer. He is praying harder.
- **"Let my prayer come before you; incline your ear to my cry!" (v2).** The word for "cry" (*rinnah*) is usually a ringing shout of joy (30:5: "joy comes with the morning"). Here the same loud voice is used for anguish. JFB points to 22:2 and 31:2 as close parallels.

#### Near to Sheol (vv3–9a)
- **"My soul is full of troubles, and my life draws near to Sheol" (v3).** "Full" is the word for being sated, as with food: he has had all he can take. *Sheol* is the realm of the dead, the grave seen as a place. Whether from illness or other trouble, Heman is close to dying, and has been for a long time (v15).
- **"I am counted among those who go down to the pit" (v4).** People already treat him as good as dead. "A man who has no strength" uses the word for a strong man (*geber*), which JFB brings out: "a stout man," whose strength is utterly gone.
- **"Like one set loose among the dead" (v5).** "Set loose" (*chophshi*) is the word for a slave set free (Exod 21:2). Job uses it for death as release from a master (Job 3:19). Here it is bitter: Heman is "free" among the dead, adrift, released from God's care. "Like those whom you remember no more, for they are cut off from your hand." To the Old Testament believer, God's "remembering" meant acting on someone's behalf (Gen 8:1). Being forgotten was the deepest fear.
- **"You have put me in the depths of the pit" (v6).** Now the hardest note sounds. Heman does not blame chance or enemies. He says God did this. The Tyndale notes call vv6–7 his first "charge" against God. That is not unbelief. It is the same conviction Job had, that nothing happens outside God's hand (Job 2:10), pushed to the point of pain.
- **"Your wrath lies heavy upon me, and you overwhelm me with all your waves" (v7).** The image is drowning, wave after wave (42:7; Jonah 2:3). Heman feels God's anger but names no sin. Unlike the penitential psalms (32; 51), there is no confession here. Sometimes suffering feels like wrath even when the Bible does not say it is punishment, as with Job. **Selah** marks a pause.
- **"You have caused my companions to shun me; you have made me a horror to them" (v8).** "Horror" is literally "abominations," the word for something people recoil from. Some think he had a disfiguring disease like the skin diseases that forced a person to live apart (Lev 13:45–46). Others take it as a picture of shame and isolation. The Tyndale notes point out that in a culture built on family and community, loneliness was itself a curse.
- **"I am shut in so that I cannot escape; my eye grows dim through sorrow" (vv8–9).** Shut in like a prisoner, worn out by weeping (6:7; 31:9). JFB: the eye "decays," fails from exhaustion.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "one of the most melancholy of all the psalms" and outlines it as pressure of spirit, the wrath of God as its cause, the unkindness of friends, prayer, and pleading with God. Guzik notes the parallels with Psalm 22 and with Job: both men suffer while still believing God is in control. He passes on Spurgeon's line on v3, "Trouble in the soul is the soul of trouble," and Spurgeon's picture of each *Selah* as a swimmer lifting his head between waves. JFB on v5: the dead are "cut off from God's care."

#### Do the Departed Rise Up to Praise You? (vv9b–12)
- **"Every day I call upon you, O LORD; I spread out my hands to you" (v9).** Open, raised hands were the posture of prayer (Exod 9:29; 1 Kgs 8:22; Ps 143:6). Heman keeps reaching up even when nothing comes down.
- **Six questions (vv10–12).** "Do you work wonders for the dead? Do the departed rise up to praise you?" The "departed" (*rephaim*) are the shades, the weak, shadowy dead. The questions expect the answer no, and they are an argument: if I die, LORD, you lose a worshiper. David argues the same way (6:5; 30:9), and so does Hezekiah (Isa 38:18–19).
- **"Your steadfast love… your faithfulness in Abaddon" (v11).** *Abaddon* means "destruction," used in the Wisdom books as a name for the depths of the grave (Job 26:6; Prov 15:11). In Revelation it becomes the name of the angel of the abyss (Rev 9:11). Heman lists all the great words of God's character, wonders, steadfast love (*chesed*), faithfulness, righteousness, and asks whether any of them reach the grave.
- **"The land of forgetfulness" (v12).** The grave pictured as the place where the dead are forgotten, and where they forget. The four names (grave, Abaddon, darkness, land of forgetfulness) build a picture of death as total loss.
- **Does this deny life after death?** Not as a teaching. Old Testament believers saw the afterlife dimly, but they also had flashes of hope (16:10; 49:15; Job 19:25–27; Dan 12:2). Heman is speaking from the edge of death as it looks from this side, where praise in the temple ends. The full light came later: Christ "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Tim 1:10). And the answer to his question "Do the departed rise up?" turned out, in Jesus, to be yes.
- **Commentators:** Guzik cautions against building a doctrine of the afterlife on these verses. The psalm neither affirms nor denies the resurrection, and later Scripture fills in what Heman could not see. JFB reads the questions as contrasting life and death "as seasons for praising God," and notes that "rise" means rise up as the dead do.

#### Why Do You Hide Your Face? (vv13–18)
- **"But I, O LORD, cry to you; in the morning my prayer comes before you" (v13).** "But I" is a turn, not to relief, but to more prayer. JFB: the KJV's "prevent" means "meet," so his prayer goes out to meet God first thing in the morning. He has prayed day and night (v1), every day (v9), and now every morning. Nothing in the psalm says he stopped.
- **"Why do you cast my soul away? Why do you hide your face from me?" (v14).** "Why" is the honest question of lament (22:1; 44:24). God's "face" shining on someone was the great blessing (Num 6:25). Its hiding is the worst thing he can imagine.
- **"Afflicted and close to death from my youth up" (v15).** JFB: "all my life." This was not a bad week. It was a lifetime of suffering. "I am helpless" is uncertain in Hebrew, perhaps "I am distracted" or "I am in despair."
- **"Your wrath has swept over me; your dreadful assaults destroy me" (v16).** "Wrath" here is plural, "your burnings" or "your fierce angers," one blast after another. "They surround me like a flood all day long" (v17) returns to the drowning picture of v7.
- **"My companions have become darkness" (v18).** The Hebrew is abrupt, literally "my acquaintances, darkness." It can mean his friends have vanished into darkness, or, as JFB and many others read it, "darkness is my closest friend." Either way, the last word of the psalm is "darkness." There is no "yet I will praise him" as in 42:11 or 43:5.
- **Why is this psalm in the Bible?** Because believers sometimes live in it. God did not edit out the prayer that ends without an answer. Its presence says that faithful people can feel abandoned, that honesty with God is not sin, and that the right place to bring despair is to the God who seems silent. The psalm itself is the proof that Heman kept talking to him. And the title of the next psalm, by Ethan, another Ezrahite, opens: "I will sing of the steadfast love of the LORD, forever" (89:1). The two are side by side in the Psalter.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the psalm "does not conclude, as usually the melancholy psalms do, with the least intimation of comfort or joy," but "from first to last, it is mourning and woe." His counsel: "Those who are in trouble of mind may sing this psalm feelingly; those that are not ought to sing it thankfully." Guzik, following Kidner, says a happy ending in this life is not owed, and its absence is not proof of God's displeasure. He also suggests that Heman's darkness drove him closer to God, not away. JFB compares the ending with Job 17:13–14, where Job makes the grave his house and calls corruption his father.

#### Pointing to Christ
No one has ever prayed this psalm as fully as Jesus did. He was "despised and rejected by men; a man of sorrows" (Isa 53:3). His friends fled and left him alone (Mark 14:50). He was "counted among the transgressors" (Isa 53:12) and laid in the pit of the grave. On the cross the waves of God's wrath truly went over him, not for his own sin but for ours, and he cried, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). There was darkness over the land (Matt 27:45). Heman's questions, "Do you work wonders for the dead? Do the departed rise up to praise you?", were answered on the third day. Because Jesus went into that darkness and came back out, the believer who prays Psalm 88 is never actually forsaken. Even when "darkness is my closest friend," God's word stands: "even the darkness is not dark to you" (139:12).
