*Setting:* Susa again, but about four years later. Esther becomes queen "in the seventh year" of Ahasuerus (v16), around 479 BC, while Vashti was deposed in his third year (1:3). In between, as Greek historians tell it, Xerxes led his huge invasion of Greece and came home beaten after the naval defeat at Salamis (480 BC) and the land defeat at Plataea (479 BC). The Bible says none of that. It simply picks up with a king who has cooled down and is lonely. Chapter 2 introduces the two Jews at the center of the book, Mordecai and his young cousin Esther, and quietly puts both of them in place: one on the queen's throne, the other in the king's records as the man who saved his life.

#### Esther Chosen Queen (vv1–18)
- **"When the anger of King Ahasuerus had abated, he remembered Vashti" (v1).** To "remember" here has a note of regret. The decree cannot be undone (1:19), so the king is stuck with the result of his own temper. His servants move quickly to fill the gap before regret turns into blame on the advisers who urged it.
- **"Let beautiful young virgins be sought out for the king" (vv2–4).** This was not a beauty contest anyone entered by choice. Officers in every province gathered young women into the royal harem. Most would spend one night with the king and then live the rest of their lives in the harem as secondary wives, unable to marry anyone else. The plan "pleased the king," the same phrase used of Memucan's plan (1:21). Ahasuerus says yes to whatever is put in front of him.
- **"A Jew in Susa the citadel whose name was Mordecai" (v5).** "Jew" (*yehudi*) originally meant someone from Judah, but by now it was the name for the whole people in exile. Mordecai is a Babylonian-style name, probably formed from Marduk, the chief god of Babylon, the way Daniel and his friends were given Babylonian names (Dan 1:7). Tablets found at Persepolis mention an official named Marduka at Susa in this period. Some scholars think it could be him, though the name was not rare.
- **"Son of Jair, son of Shimei, son of Kish, a Benjaminite" (v5).** Kish was the name of King Saul's father (1 Sam 9:1), and Shimei was the Benjaminite from Saul's clan who cursed David (2 Sam 16:5). These may be distant ancestors named to show Mordecai's family line rather than his father and grandfather. Either way the narrator wants the reader to hear Saul's family. That matters in the next chapter, when Mordecai meets Haman "the Agagite," a descendant, it seems, of the Amalekite king Saul spared (1 Sam 15).
- **"Carried away… with Jeconiah king of Judah" (v6).** Jeconiah (also called Jehoiachin) was taken to Babylon in 597 BC (2 Kings 24:10–16). That was more than a hundred years before this chapter, so it cannot have been Mordecai himself. Grammatically the "who" can refer to Kish, the last name in the list, and the ESV leaves it open. Most commentators take it that his family went into exile in that first wave, with Ezekiel and the leading families.
- **"Hadassah, that is Esther" (v7).** *Hadassah* is Hebrew for "myrtle," a sweet-smelling evergreen that the prophets use as a picture of God's renewal (Isa 55:13; Zech 1:8). "Esther" is her Persian name. Many take it from the Persian word for "star," and some link it to the Babylonian goddess Ishtar. Like Daniel, she lives with two names, one for her people and one for the empire.
- **"Mordecai took her as his own daughter" (v7).** Esther was Mordecai's cousin, "the daughter of his uncle." Both her parents had died, and he raised her. The book never tells us her age, but she was young enough to be called a "young woman."
- **"Esther also was taken" (v8).** The verb is passive. She did not volunteer. Christians have long debated whether Esther should have resisted, and whether marrying a pagan king was a compromise of the law (Deut 7:3). The text does not comment. It reports what happened to a young orphan under a despot's decree, and it shows God bringing her through.
- **"The young woman pleased him and won his favor" (v9).** The Hebrew is literally "she lifted up kindness (*hesed*) before him." *Hesed* is the covenant word for steadfast, loyal love, here used of the favor of a pagan official. Hegai gives her the best rooms, seven attendants and her rations at once. Compare Joseph with Potiphar and the jailer (Gen 39:4, 21) and Daniel with the chief eunuch (Dan 1:9): God gave Daniel "favor and compassion" in the sight of his overseer. Esther's story does not say "God gave," but the pattern is the same.
- **"Her portion of food" (v9).** Unlike Daniel (Dan 1:8), Esther apparently did not refuse the palace food. The book is quiet about her keeping the law, and that silence fits her hidden identity.
- **"Esther had not made known her people or kindred" (v10).** Mordecai told her to keep her Jewish identity secret. The book does not say why. Perhaps he feared prejudice, which Haman's plot shows was real. This secrecy will be the hinge of chapter 7, when she finally says, "my people."
- **"Every day Mordecai walked in front of the court of the harem" (v11).** No man could enter, but he walked past daily to hear news. It is a small picture of a father's love.
- **Twelve months of beauty treatment (v12).** Six months with oil of myrrh, six with spices and ointments. Some commentators note that a year of seclusion also proved no woman came in pregnant. The word for "beautifying" comes from a root meaning "to scrape" or "rub," the scraping and oiling of skin.
- **"She was given whatever she desired to take with her" (v13).** Each woman could choose clothes, jewels or perfume to take with her, a chance to make an impression. **"In the evening she would go in, and in the morning she would return"** (v14) to the second harem, under Shaashgaz, keeper of the concubines. Unless the king called her by name, that was it.
- **"She asked for nothing except what Hegai… advised" (v15).** Esther trusts the man who knows the king's tastes, rather than grabbing what she can. The narrator repeats that she "was winning favor in the eyes of all who saw her."
- **"The tenth month, which is the month of Tebeth" (v16).** Tebeth falls in December–January. The book is careful about dates because the feast of Purim will be fixed by them (9:21).
- **"The king loved Esther more than all the women" (v17).** He sets the crown on her head "instead of Vashti," the phrase that has been waiting since 1:19. Notice the reversal: Vashti refused to be paraded wearing the crown; Esther is given it.
- **"It was Esther's feast" (v18).** Yet another *mishteh*. The king also granted a "remission," the Hebrew *hanahah*, from the root "to rest." Most take it as a tax holiday or a release from labor. The Tyndale notes observe that the book will end with the Jews finding "rest" from their enemies (9:16–22).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads the chapter as God bringing down the mighty and lifting up the humble: "Vashti being humbled for her height, Esther is advanced for her humility," quoting Mary's song (Luke 1:52). He defends Esther against the charge that she sinned to gain the crown, since every woman the king took was legally a wife of lower rank. Guzik agrees that Esther seems to have had no real choice, and stresses that God's plan was not luck or beauty, citing Psalm 75:6–7. He is more cautious about the secrecy, warning that believers should not habitually hide their faith, though he allows there may be a time to wait. JFB says the crown was likely "a purple ribbon, streaked with white," and that the remission probably reduced the taxes that funded the queen's household.

#### Mordecai Discovers a Plot (vv19–23)
- **"When the virgins were gathered together the second time" (v19).** This is obscure. It may mean a second gathering of women even after Esther was crowned, which would fit Ahasuerus. Or it may look back to the gathering in v8. Commentators are not agreed.
- **"Mordecai was sitting at the king's gate" (v19).** The gate of an ancient palace or city was where officials did business and courts met. "Sitting at the gate" suggests Mordecai held some royal office. JFB notes that Persian officers waited in the outer courts until summoned. Whether Esther helped him get the post, the text does not say.
- **"Esther obeyed Mordecai just as when she was brought up by him" (v20).** Even as queen, she still honors the man who raised her. The repeat of v10 tells the reader that the secret is still being kept.
- **Bigthan and Teresh (v21).** Two eunuchs "who guarded the threshold," meaning the doors of the king's private rooms, plotted to kill him. Such men had the easiest access to a king. Xerxes was in fact murdered in his bedroom about fourteen years later, in 465 BC, by a court official and a eunuch. JFB guesses these two may have acted for Vashti, but that is only a guess.
- **"Esther told the king in the name of Mordecai" (v22).** She gives the credit to him. That detail is planted here and pays off in chapter 6, on a sleepless night.
- **"Hanged on the gallows" (v23).** The Hebrew is simply "hanged on a tree" or "on wood" (*'ets*). Persian inscriptions show the usual method was impalement on a stake, often after death, to display the body. Darius I boasts in his Behistun inscription of impaling rebels. English "gallows" can mislead readers into picturing a rope.
- **"Recorded in the book of the chronicles in the presence of the king" (v23).** It was written down, and then nothing happened. No reward, no thanks. The reader is meant to feel that, and to remember it.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees Mordecai living out "Fear God. Honor the king" (1 Pet 2:17) before Peter wrote it: an exile who still protects a pagan king. He explains the "tree" as impalement, not a rope. JFB says God's providence intended the unrewarded deed as "the occasion afterwards of Mordecai's preferment." Henry observes that no step had yet been taken toward Haman's plot, "but several steps are taken towards God's design of their deliverance, and this for one." He adds a dry warning to plotters: "though men presume upon secresy, a bird of the air shall carry the voice" (Eccl 10:20).

#### Pointing to Christ
An orphan from a conquered people is lifted from obscurity to the throne, not by her own scheming but through a long chain of events no one controlled. A faithful man saves the king's life and is forgotten, for now. Both threads point beyond Esther. God "raises the poor from the dust… to make them sit with princes" (1 Sam 2:8), and the gospel goes further: orphans with no claim at all are adopted into God's family and seated with Christ (Eph 2:6; Gal 4:4–7). And Christ himself is the faithful servant whose obedience looked forgotten on the cross, until God raised him and gave him "the name that is above every name" (Phil 2:9).
