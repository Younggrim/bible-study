*Setting:* "A Psalm of David," for the choirmaster. Psalm 20 was the people's prayer for their king before battle. Psalm 21 is their thanksgiving after it. The two belong together almost word for word: "May he grant you your heart's desire" (20:4) has become "You have given him his heart's desire" (21:2), and the "salvation" they hoped to shout about (20:5) is now the king's great joy (21:1). The psalm has two halves. Verses 1–7 speak to God about the king and all God has given him. Verses 8–12 speak to the king (or possibly to God) about the victories still to come. Verse 13 closes with praise. No specific battle is named. Some link the "crown of fine gold" (v3) to the day David captured Rabbah of the Ammonites and the enemy king's heavy gold crown was placed on his head (2 Sam 12:30), but that is only a suggestion. Early Jewish interpreters read the king in this psalm as the Messiah, and Christians have read it that way too.

#### The King Rejoices (vv1–2)
- **"O LORD, in your strength the king rejoices" (v1).** Notice where the joy comes from. Not the king's army, his skill or his crown, but God's strength. The psalm ends with the same word: "Be exalted, O LORD, in your strength!" (v13). The whole song is wrapped in God's power, start and finish.
- **"In your salvation how greatly he exults!" (v1).** "Salvation" (*yeshuah*) again means victory and rescue in battle, as in 20:5. "How greatly" suggests joy too big to put into words.
- **"You have given him his heart's desire" (v2).** The answer to 20:4. "Heart's desire" points to what he longed for inside, "the request of his lips" to what he said out loud. God answered both.
- **Selah (v2).** A pause, probably for reflection or music, after the answered prayer has sunk in.
- **Commentators:** Henry connects the two psalms: "Those whom we have prayed for we ought to give thanks for." He notes that David's "joy was in God's strength and in his salvation, and not in the strength or success of his armies." JFB says the second clause of v2 restates the first "by the negation of its opposite," a common pattern in Hebrew poetry. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment that real joy in God should have something inexpressible in it.

#### Rich Blessings and a Crown of Gold (vv3–6)
- **"You meet him with rich blessings" (v3).** "Meet" is literally "go before" or "come to meet." The KJV's "preventest" meant "goes ahead of" in old English. God does not wait for the king to arrive. He comes out to meet him with good things, like a father running to meet his son (Luke 15:20). The same verb was used in 17:13, "Confront him," and in 18:5, 18 of death and enemies confronting David. Here God is the one who meets him first, with blessing.
- **"You set a crown of fine gold upon his head" (v3).** David did not seize the crown. He waited years while Saul lived, refusing to take it by force (1 Sam 24:6; 26:9–11). God himself set it on his head. "Fine gold" (*paz*) is the purest, refined gold, the same word as "fine gold" in 19:10.
- **"He asked life of you; you gave it to him, length of days forever and ever" (v4).** A soldier going to war asks to come home alive. God gave far more than survival. "Forever and ever" is more than a long life. It recalls God's promise to David that his house and throne would be established "forever" (2 Sam 7:13–16). Some take the words as the usual exaggerated courtly language for a long reign ("O king, live forever," 1 Kgs 1:31; Dan 2:4). But in Scripture, only one son of David actually lives forever (Rom 6:9; Heb 7:24–25).
- **"His glory is great through your salvation; splendor and majesty you bestow on him" (v5).** "Splendor and majesty" (*hod ve-hadar*) are words normally used of God himself (96:6; 104:1; 111:3). The king's glory is real, but it is reflected glory, laid on him by God, like the splendor Moses received on his face (Exod 34:29).
- **"You make him most blessed forever" (v6).** Literally "you make him blessings," a fountain of blessing to others. This echoes God's promise to Abraham: "you will be a blessing… in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:2–3).
- **"You make him glad with the joy of your presence" (v6).** "Presence" is literally "face." The king's deepest joy is not his crown but the face of God, as in 16:11: "in your presence there is fullness of joy." Hold onto this word, because it returns in v9.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Crowns are at God's disposal; no head wears them but God sets them there." He adds, "On the head of Christ God never set a crown of gold, but of thorns first, and then of glory." On v4 he says God gave David not only a long life but "a blessed immortality" and "the continuance of his kingdom in the Messiah that should come of his loins," and notes how "God's grants often exceed our petitions." JFB links vv4–6 to 2 Samuel 7:13–16 and reads "most blessed" as "set him 'to be blessings,' as Abraham." Guzik passes on Kidner's comment that "forever" may be courtly language or an echo of the endless dynasty of 2 Samuel 7, and that the New Testament settles it on the Messiah. He also notes that the Targum, an ancient Aramaic paraphrase, reads "the king" in v1 as "King Messiah."

#### He Shall Not Be Moved (v7)
- **"For the king trusts in the LORD" (v7).** This is the center of the psalm. Everything before it is what God has given, and everything after it is what God will do. The hinge is the king's trust. He is the example of 20:7: "we trust in the name of the LORD our God."
- **"Through the steadfast love of the Most High he shall not be moved" (v7).** The king's trust is the channel, but God's steadfast love (*chesed*) is the source. "Moved" (*mot*) is the word that has run through these psalms: the wicked boasted they would not be moved (10:6), the righteous will never be moved (15:5; 16:8). "Most High" (*Elyon*) is the title of God as ruler over all nations and all kings (Gen 14:18–20; Dan 4:17).
- **Commentators:** JFB puts it neatly: "The mediate cause is the king's faith, the efficient, God's mercy." Faith is the hand that receives. God's love is what keeps the king standing.

#### Your Hand Will Find Out All Your Enemies (vv8–12)
- **Who is "you"?** In vv1–7 "you" was God and "he" was the king. From v8 the address changes. Most commentators, JFB included, think the people now turn and speak to the king, promising him future victories. Others think they are still speaking to God. Verse 9 mentions "the LORD" in the third person, which favors the king as "you." In the end the difference is small, since, as JFB says, "the king is only God's agent."
- **"Your hand will find out all your enemies" (v8).** "Find out" means to reach, to catch up with. No enemy will be able to hide. The right hand, the stronger hand, repeats the thought with more force.
- **"You will make them as a blazing oven when you appear" (v9).** "When you appear" is literally "at the time of your face." The same face that gives the king joy in v6 brings fire on his enemies in v9. God's presence is the best thing in the world for those who trust him and the most terrible thing for those who hate him. A "blazing oven" was a clay furnace heated to fierce temperatures. The picture is not that they are put into the oven but that they become like one, consumed by fire from within.
- **"The LORD will swallow them up in his wrath, and fire will consume them" (v9).** Judgment in the Old Testament is often pictured as fire (11:6; 50:3; 97:3). Jesus used fire for final judgment too (Matt 13:42). Malachi says the day of the LORD will come "burning like an oven" (Mal 4:1).
- **"You will destroy their descendants from the earth" (v10).** Literally "their fruit… and their seed." This is hard for modern readers. In the ancient world a defeated king's dynasty was usually wiped out so that it could not rise again. The psalm is speaking of the end of hostile royal lines that plotted against God's anointed, not of hating children. The promise to David was the opposite: his "offspring forever" (18:50).
- **"Though they plan evil against you… they will not succeed" (v11).** The enemies plot, scheme and devise, but their plans fail. Psalm 2:1–4 made the same point: the nations plot "in vain," and God laughs.
- **"You will put them to flight; you will aim at their faces with your bows" (v12).** "Put them to flight" is literally "make them a shoulder," meaning make them turn their backs and run (18:40). Archers aim at the faces of those who have not yet fled.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads "a fiery oven" as the inner torment of those who rejected God's king: "the reflections and terrors of their own consciences will be their hell," and those who "might have had Christ to rule and save them, but rejected him" will be "a fiery oven to themselves." JFB says "anger" is literally "face, as appearing against them," and "find out" means "reach, lay hold of." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's reminder that the time of God's anger will follow the present time of grace. He also passes on Spurgeon's point that sin planned on purpose is worse than sin done in ignorance.

#### Be Exalted, O LORD (v13)
- **"Be exalted, O LORD, in your strength!" (v13).** The psalm returns to where it began: "in your strength" (v1). The people do not finish by praising the king. They praise the God who made the king strong. "Be exalted" is not a request for God to become greater. It asks God to show his greatness for everyone to see.
- **"We will sing and praise your power" (v13).** The "we" of 20:5, who hoped to "shout for joy," now actually sing.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the people ask God to "act for him in his own strength, by the immediate operations of his power," and so "exalt himself and glorify his own name." Guzik passes on Clarke's comment that creatures cannot exalt God, so the prayer means "exalt yourself."

#### Pointing to Christ
Henry wrote that some lines of this psalm are "more applicable" to the Messiah than to David, and that is true. David asked for life and received a long reign, but he died. Jesus asked for life (Heb 5:7), and God raised him from the dead to live forever, "length of days forever and ever." The crown first placed on his head was made of thorns. Now God has crowned him "with glory and honor" (Heb 2:9). He trusted the Father perfectly, and through God's steadfast love he was not moved, even by death. He is glad "with the joy of your presence" (Acts 2:28, quoting Ps 16), and he has become blessing for all the families of the earth. The same face that brings joy to those who trust him will one day be a fire to those who refuse him (2 Thess 1:7–10). So the psalm ends the only way it can: "Be exalted, O LORD, in your strength!"
