*Setting:* Israel has just left Mount Sinai (10:11–13), after nearly a year there and ten chapters of careful preparation. Chapter 10 ended on a high note, with Moses praying every morning, "Arise, O LORD." Chapter 11 is where it starts to go wrong. Within days of setting out, the people are complaining. This is the first of a series of rebellions that runs through chapters 11–25 and ends with a whole generation dying in the desert. The chapter has three scenes, each named after what happened at the place: a fire at Taberah ("burning"), a crisis that leaves Moses wishing he were dead and God sharing his Spirit with seventy elders, and a flood of quail that ends at "the graves of craving."

#### The People Complain (vv1–15)
- **"The people complained in the hearing of the LORD about their misfortunes" (v1).** The Hebrew suggests people "acting like complainers," muttering about hardship. Sinai had been a stable camp for a year. Now there was heat, dust, thirst and walking. What they complained about is not even stated, and perhaps that is the point. The grumbling was general. The Tyndale notes observe that in Numbers, complaining is treated as rejecting God himself (v20).
- **"The fire of the LORD burned among them and consumed some outlying parts of the camp" (v1).** The judgment was real but limited, falling on the edges, a warning more than a destruction. The same "fire of the LORD" had consumed Nadab and Abihu (Lev 10:2).
- **"The people cried out to Moses, and Moses prayed" (v2).** Moses' role as the one who stands between God and the people, which will be tested again and again in this book, starts here. The place is named **Taberah**, "burning" (v3; Deut 9:22).
- **"The rabble that was among them had a strong craving" (v4).** "Rabble" (*asafsuf*) is a word found only here, meaning a gathered-up crowd. It refers to the non-Israelites who left Egypt with Israel, the "mixed multitude" of Exodus 12:38. **"Strong craving"** is literally "they craved a craving," using *ta'avah*, desire or lust. The same noun describes the forbidden fruit as "a delight to the eyes" in Genesis 3:6. The craving began on the edges of the community and spread to its center: **"the people of Israel also wept again."**
- **"Oh that we had meat to eat!" (v4).** Israel had flocks and herds (Exod 12:38; Num 32:1), but those animals were too valuable to eat regularly, for milk and wool and breeding. They had enough to live on. What they wanted was variety.
- **"The fish we ate in Egypt that cost nothing" (v5).** The Nile and its canals were full of fish, and cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions and garlic were the staples of the poor in Egypt. Herodotus records that the laborers who built the pyramids were fed on radishes, onions and garlic. But "cost nothing"? They had paid for it with their freedom, under whips. Memory had edited out the slavery. A year earlier they had "groaned because of their slavery" (Exod 2:23).
- **"Nothing at all but this manna to look at" (v6).** The Hebrew is blunt: "our soul is dried up; there is nothing at all except to this manna our eyes." The bread from heaven, given fresh every morning, had become boring. Psalm 78:24–25 calls it "the grain of heaven" and "the bread of the angels."
- **What manna was like (vv7–9).** Moses (or the narrator) pauses to describe it, and the description answers the complaint. It was **"like coriander seed,"** small and round, and looked like **"bdellium,"** a pale, translucent resin (Gen 2:12). Exodus 16:31 says it was white and tasted like wafers made with honey. It could be ground, pounded, boiled or baked, and tasted **"like cakes baked with oil."** And it came every night **"with the dew"** (v9). People have tried to explain manna as a natural substance, such as the sweet secretions some insects leave on tamarisk trees in Sinai. Whatever its nature, the Tyndale notes point out that its amount, its timing (none on the Sabbath, a double portion the day before; Exod 16:22–27) and its forty-year supply were plainly miraculous (Deut 8:3; Josh 5:12).
- **"Weeping throughout their clans, everyone at the door of his tent" (v10).** It was not a few. Whole families sat in their tent doors and wept, openly and publicly. **"The anger of the LORD blazed hotly, and Moses was displeased."** Both God and Moses are angry, but it is Moses' reaction that the next verses follow.
- **"Why have you dealt ill with your servant?" (v11).** Moses' prayer is one of the rawest in the Bible. He does not complain to the people about God. He complains to God about the people, and about the job.
- **"Did I conceive all this people? Did I give them birth?" (v12).** The image is of a mother or nurse carrying a baby at her breast. The expected answer is: No, *you* did, LORD. Moses is saying this is God's child, not his. The Bible elsewhere does picture God as Israel's mother: "the God who gave you birth" (Deut 32:18; compare Isa 49:15).
- **"I am not able to carry all this people alone" (v14).** Jethro had told him the same thing at Sinai: "You are not able to do it alone" (Exod 18:18). The judges appointed then handled legal disputes. This burden is different: the weight of a people's spiritual discontent.
- **"Kill me at once… that I may not see my wretchedness" (v15).** Moses joins Elijah (1 Kgs 19:4), Jeremiah (Jer 20:14–18) and Jonah (Jonah 4:3) in asking to die. God does not rebuke him. He answers him. The Tyndale notes draw the contrast: the people complained about God while longing for Egypt; Moses complained *to* God, and that is a different thing.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the chapter "a melancholy scene" after good progress, and says, "it is sin that makes all this mischief." Guzik notes that the people went to Moses rather than straight to God, and that the rabble shows that not everyone in a community of faith truly belongs to God. He reads the Egypt nostalgia as forgetting the bitterness of bondage. JFB identifies the rabble as Egyptians who "fell a lusting," and treats the craving as an attack on God's provision. JFB sympathizes with Moses' distress but says the tone of his words to God "cannot be justified." Guzik is similar: Moses' complaint was understandable but a lapse in trust. The Tyndale notes are gentler, and see it as an acceptable lament.

#### Elders Appointed to Aid Moses (vv16–30)
- **"Gather for me seventy men of the elders of Israel" (v16).** God answers both problems: Moses' burden (vv16–17) and the people's craving (vv18–23). Seventy elders had gone up Sinai with Moses and seen God (Exod 24:1, 9–11). These were men already recognized as leaders, **"whom you know to be the elders… and officers."** God did not invent new leaders. He equipped the ones who were already serving. Jewish tradition later traced its high council, the Sanhedrin of seventy members plus a president, back to this passage.
- **"I will take some of the Spirit that is on you and put it on them" (v17).** This is the first time the Old Testament explicitly says that the Spirit rested on Moses. The point is not that Moses had less afterward. A lamp does not lose its flame by lighting others. The same Spirit equips them, so that **"they shall bear the burden of the people with you."** The Tyndale notes draw the contrast with Jethro's judges, who handled practical cases; these men needed the Spirit for spiritual leadership.
- **"Consecrate yourselves for tomorrow, and you shall eat meat" (v18).** The language of preparing for a holy event (Exod 19:10) is used with grim irony. They are getting ready for judgment.
- **"Until it comes out at your nostrils and becomes loathsome to you" (v20).** God will give them what they want, and far more than they want. The reason is stated plainly: **"you have rejected the LORD who is among you."** Their complaint about food was really a rejection of the God living in their camp. **"Why did we come out of Egypt?"** is the question of people who would undo their own salvation.
- **"Six hundred thousand on foot" (v21).** Moses does the math and cannot see how it will work. Flocks and herds? All the fish in the sea? (v22). He believes God enough to pray but not enough to see the answer.
- **"Is the LORD's hand shortened?" (v23).** A short arm is a weak one, unable to reach. Isaiah uses the same picture: "Is my hand shortened, that it cannot redeem?" (Isa 50:2; 59:1). **"Now you shall see whether my word will come true."**
- **"The LORD came down in the cloud… and put it on the seventy elders… they prophesied" (v25).** Prophesying here probably means an outward sign of the Spirit's coming, perhaps inspired praise or speech, like Saul among the prophets (1 Sam 10:10–11). The Tyndale notes say we are not told exactly what happened. It was a visible sign that these men had been given God's Spirit for their work.
- **"But they did not continue doing it" (v25).** The prophesying was a one-time sign, not a lasting gift of prophecy. The Hebrew can be read two ways, and older versions, including the KJV ("they did not cease"), took it the opposite way. The ESV and most modern translations take it as "they did not do it again." The leadership was lasting; the sign was not.
- **"Eldad… and Medad… prophesied in the camp" (v26).** Two of the seventy, **"among those registered,"** had stayed in the camp. Why is not said. The Spirit came on them where they were. The Spirit was not confined to the tent.
- **"My lord Moses, stop them" (v28).** Joshua, Moses' **"assistant… from his youth,"** is worried that someone is challenging his master's authority. It is loyal, but narrow.
- **"Are you jealous for my sake? Would that all the LORD's people were prophets" (v29).** One of the great lines in Moses' life. He is not protecting his position. Chapter 12 will call him "very meek, more than all people" (12:3), and here is the proof. Jesus showed the same spirit when the disciples tried to stop a man casting out demons in his name: "Do not stop him… the one who is not against us is for us" (Mark 9:38–40).
- **Commentators:** Henry says the Spirit "qualifies the seventy elders for the government." JFB says the gifts given to the elders did not reduce Moses' gifts "in any degree," and calls his answer to Joshua "a noble disinterestedness." Guzik stresses that the elders were gathered to God before they were gathered to Moses, and sees Moses' wish in v29 as looking forward to the new covenant, when the Spirit would be poured out on all God's people. The Tyndale notes read Joshua's protest as a worthy but immature zeal, a quality Moses had grown out of and Joshua had not yet.

#### Quail and a Plague (vv31–35)
- **"A wind from the LORD sprang up, and it brought quail from the sea" (v31).** In Hebrew the word for "wind" is *ruach*, the same word translated "Spirit" in vv17–29. The Spirit came on the elders, and the wind brought the quail. Quail still migrate across Sinai in great flocks in spring and autumn, flying low and landing exhausted after crossing the sea. They are easy to catch, and Bedouin still net them. God used a natural migration, but its timing, its scale and its announcement in advance (vv18–20) were his doing. God had sent quail once before, a year earlier, without judgment (Exod 16:13).
- **"About two cubits above the ground" (v31).** A cubit was about 18 inches, so about three feet. Either the birds lay heaped that deep over a wide area, or, as many think, they flew at that height, low enough to knock down by hand. JFB mentions both readings. Either way, they covered the ground for **"a day's journey"** on each side of the camp.
- **"All that day and all night and all the next day… Those who gathered least gathered ten homers" (v32).** A homer was the largest dry measure, perhaps around 50 to 60 gallons, so ten homers was an enormous heap. JFB suggests the measure may be used loosely for "great heaps." Compare the manna, where each family gathered what it needed for the day (Exod 16:16–18). Here, even the least greedy grabbed far more than anyone could eat. **"They spread them out… around the camp"** to dry them in the sun, storing them up, as if God might not provide tomorrow.
- **"While the meat was yet between their teeth… the LORD struck down the people with a very great plague" (v33).** What the plague was is not said. Some have suggested food poisoning, since quail that eat certain plants can be toxic to humans, but the text presents it as a direct act of God. Psalm 78:29–31 retells the scene: "he gave them what they craved… while the food was still in their mouths, the anger of God rose against them." Psalm 106:15 sums it up: "he gave them what they asked, but sent a wasting disease among them."
- **"Kibroth-hattaavah, because there they buried the people who had the craving" (v34).** The name means "graves of craving," using the same word *ta'avah* from v4. The craving that began with the rabble ended in a cemetery. Paul draws the lesson for the church: these things "took place as examples for us, that we might not desire evil as they did" (1 Cor 10:6).
- **"From Kibroth-hattaavah the people journeyed to Hazeroth" (v35).** Neither site has been identified for certain. Hazeroth will be the setting for chapter 12.
- **Commentators:** Henry says God here "has turned to be their enemy, and fights against them," and that the episode shows "both his mercy and his justice." JFB notes that those who gathered were "distrustful of God's goodness" and dried the quails for later, and says the place name shows the deaths fell on those who indulged their craving. Guzik picks up on *ruach*: the Spirit drew the elders to the center of God's work, while the wind dropped quail on the outskirts, where the craving people went to gather. He closes on Jesus as the true bread from heaven, the only food that truly satisfies.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is about two kinds of bread and two kinds of wind. The people despised the manna and craved Egypt's food, and their craving killed them. Jesus later stood before a crowd that had just eaten bread in the wilderness and said, "Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died… I am the living bread that came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever" (John 6:49–51). And Moses' wish that "all the LORD's people were prophets" came true at Pentecost, when the risen Christ poured out his Spirit, as Joel had promised, on sons and daughters, young and old (Acts 2:16–18). The one whose hand is not shortened gives not quail that sickens but a Spirit who satisfies.
