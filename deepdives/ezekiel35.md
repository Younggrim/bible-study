*Setting:* Babylonia, early in 585 BC, soon after news came that Jerusalem had fallen (33:21). Ezekiel has just promised that God will shepherd his scattered flock home and give them security, so that "they shall no more be a prey to the nations" (34:28). But one neighbor was already moving in on the empty land. Edom lay to the south and east of the Dead Sea, in the rugged red-rock highlands around Mount Seir, in what is now southern Jordan. Its people descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother, and the bad blood between the two peoples ran all the way back to their birth (Gen 25:22–34; 27:41). When Babylon destroyed Jerusalem, Edom cheered, cut down fleeing Judeans and began taking over southern Judah (Ps 137:7; Lam 4:21–22; Obad 10–14). Ezekiel had already given a short oracle against Edom (25:12–14). This fuller one sits deliberately between two promises. Chapter 34 promised God's flock a home, and chapter 36 will speak to "the mountains of Israel." Before the mountains of Israel can be restored, the mountain that claims them must be dealt with.

#### Prophecy Against Mount Seir (vv1–15)
- **"Set your face against Mount Seir" (v2).** Seir was the mountain range at the heart of Edom, and the name stands for the whole country, much as "the mountains of Israel" stand for Israel. The chapter is built as a mirror of 36:1–15: one set of mountains is cursed and the other blessed. Edom here is both a real nation and a type of every enemy of God's people. Isaiah 34 and 63:1–6 use Edom the same way.
- **"Behold, I am against you… I will stretch out my hand against you" (v3).** These are the same words used against Israel's false shepherds (34:10) and against Egypt and Tyre. **"A desolation and a waste"** is the Hebrew pair *shemamah umeshammah*, a sound-alike pair meaning roughly "desolate and desolated." Words built on the same root for desolation run through the whole chapter (vv3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 14, 15).
- **"You shall know that I am the LORD" (vv4, 9, 12, 15).** Four times. This refrain runs through Ezekiel, over sixty times in all. Edom thought Israel's God had been defeated. The point of the judgment is that Edom will find out otherwise.
- **"Because you cherished perpetual enmity" (v5).** Literally an "enmity of old" or "everlasting hatred" (*eyvat olam*). Edom's hatred was not a passing quarrel. It went back to Esau's grudge against Jacob over the stolen blessing (Gen 27:41) and was kept alive for a thousand years: Edom refused Israel passage on the way from Egypt (Num 20:14–21), fought Saul and David (1 Sam 14:47; 2 Sam 8:13–14), and revolted under later kings (2 Kgs 8:20). Amos had condemned Edom for the same thing: "he pursued his brother with the sword and cast off all pity… and he kept his wrath forever" (Amos 1:11).
- **"Gave over the people of Israel to the power of the sword at the time of their calamity" (v5).** This is the specific crime. When Jerusalem fell in 586 BC, Edom helped the Babylonians, blocked the roads and handed over or killed those trying to escape. Obadiah describes it closely: "Do not stand at the crossroads to cut off his fugitives; do not hand over his survivors in the day of distress" (Obad 14). **"At the time of their final punishment"** means the moment when Judah's iniquity reached its end and judgment fell. Edom kicked a brother when he was down.
- **"I will prepare you for blood, and blood shall pursue you" (v6).** "Blood" (*dam*) appears four times in this one verse. It is a play on Edom's name, which means "red" (*edom*), and on Esau, who traded his birthright for red stew and was nicknamed "Red" (Gen 25:30). Red Edom loved blood, so blood will chase it. **"Because you did not hate bloodshed"** is a sharp turn: Edom hated its brother but did not hate killing.
- **"I will cut off from it all who come and go" (v7).** Edom grew rich on trade routes, including the King's Highway that ran north and south through its land. "All who come and go" means every traveler: the caravans will stop.
- **"I will fill its mountains with the slain… in all your ravines" (v8).** Mountains, hills, valleys and ravines together mean "everywhere." Edom's high cliffs and narrow gorges made it feel unconquerable. Obadiah says Edom boasted, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (Obad 3).
- **"I will make you a perpetual desolation" (v9).** "Perpetual" (*olam*) answers the "perpetual enmity" of v5. Everlasting hatred meets everlasting ruin. The written Hebrew text reads "your cities shall not be inhabited," which the ESV follows. The traditional reading in the margin is "shall not return." Either way, Edom's towns would not recover.
- **Did it happen?** Babylon itself attacked Edom around 553 BC under Nabonidus. By the 400s BC Arab peoples were pushing Edomites out of their homeland (Mal 1:2–4 describes Edom's land as already laid waste). The Nabataeans, a later Arab people, took over the region and carved the rock city of Petra. Edomites who had moved into southern Judah became known as Idumeans. Around 125 BC the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus conquered them and forced them to be circumcised. King Herod the Great was an Idumean, which gives an odd twist to the old rivalry: a descendant of Esau tried to kill the newborn King of the Jews. After the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70, the Idumeans disappear from history as a people.
- **"These two nations and these two countries shall be mine" (v10).** The two nations are Israel and Judah. The northern kingdom was emptied by Assyria in 722 BC, and now Judah was emptied by Babylon. Edom thought both were up for grabs.
- **"Although the LORD was there" (v10).** This is the heart of the chapter, a short clause dropped into the middle of Edom's boast. Edom assumed that when a nation was conquered its god was finished, and that the land was now ownerless. But the land belonged to the LORD (Lev 25:23). Even though his glory had left the temple (10:18–19; 11:22–23), he had not given up his claim. The last words of Ezekiel's book give the new Jerusalem its name: *Yahweh shammah*, "The LORD is there" (48:35). Edom's mistake is answered at the very end.
- **"According to the anger and envy that you showed because of your hatred" (v11).** The punishment matches the sin. This principle runs through the prophets: as Obadiah puts it, "as you have done, it shall be done to you; your deeds shall return on your own head" (Obad 15).
- **"I will make myself known among them, when I judge you" (v11).** "Among them" probably means among Israel. Edom's fall will show God's own people that he has not forgotten them.
- **"I have heard all the revilings that you uttered against the mountains of Israel" (v12).** Edom mocked: **"They are laid desolate; they are given us to devour."** The mountains of Israel will hear God's reply in 36:1–7, where these same taunts are quoted back. God repeats **"I heard it" (v13).** Edom thought it was mocking a broken people. It was speaking against God himself: **"You magnified yourselves against me with your mouth."** To insult God's people is to insult God. Jesus says the same to Saul on the road to Damascus: "why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4).
- **"While the whole earth rejoices, I will make you desolate" (v14).** The Hebrew of this verse is difficult. The ESV takes it to mean that Edom will be desolate at a time when the rest of the earth is glad. Another reading takes it as "as the whole earth rejoiced," so that the world, which once gloated over Israel, will now enjoy Edom's fall. Verse 15 makes the main point plain in either case.
- **"As you rejoiced over the inheritance of the house of Israel, because it was desolate, so I will deal with you" (v15).** Edom's sin was not only violence but glee. Proverbs warns, "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls" (Prov 24:17–18). The chapter ends with **"Mount Seir, and all Edom, all of it,"** a deliberately full phrase, so that no corner of Edom is left out.
- **What about forgiveness for Edom?** The prophets are not only harsh toward Edom. Amos 9:12 speaks of "the remnant of Edom" being called by the LORD's name, and James quotes the Greek form of that verse at the Jerusalem council to show that Gentiles are welcomed in Christ (Acts 15:16–17). The judgment here falls on Edom as an enemy nation. It does not shut the door on any individual Edomite who turned to the LORD.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees Edom as standing for all the enemies of God's church, as Esau's line stood against Jacob's. He stresses that Edom's hatred was inherited and deep-rooted, and that Edom attacked those already suffering. He reads "the LORD was there" as God's presence and promises still holding even over a ruined land. JFB says Edom is singled out as "badly pre-eminent in its bitterness against God's people," and says that "the LORD was there" "bursts in, like a flash of lightning, reproving the wicked presumption of Edom's thought." JFB also notes the play on Edom and *dam*, "blood," and gives the two readings of v9. Guzik also points to the fourfold *blood*. He warns that God may hold a society to account for its sins, but believers must not nurse old hatreds against whole peoples. He applies the chapter to hostility toward Israel today, while noting the tragedy that Edom and Israel were brothers.

#### Pointing to Christ
Edom's sin was the old sin of Cain: a brother's hatred that would not let go, and that rejoiced when the brother fell. The gospel is God's answer to that sin. Jesus tells his followers to love their enemies and pray for those who persecute them (Matt 5:44). On the cross, he prayed for the men killing him (Luke 23:34), and through the cross he broke down "the dividing wall of hostility," making one new people out of old enemies (Eph 2:14–16). Edom reached for a land it thought God had abandoned. In Christ, God gives a far greater inheritance freely, even to Esau's children, and the city at the end of Ezekiel's book bears the name Edom forgot: "The LORD is there."
