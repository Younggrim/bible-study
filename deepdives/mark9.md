*Setting:* Northern Galilee, near Caesarea Philippi, probably autumn of AD 29. A week earlier Peter confessed, "You are the Christ," and Jesus at once began teaching that the Christ must suffer, be rejected, die and rise (8:29–31). He then called anyone who would follow him to take up a cross (8:34). Chapter 9 answers the shock of that teaching in two ways. On a mountain, three disciples see the glory that lies beyond the cross. Back in the valley, everyone sees how little faith and understanding the disciples still have. The rest of the chapter is Jesus teaching them, privately and patiently, what greatness looks like in his kingdom.

#### Jesus Foretells His Death and Resurrection (v1)
- **The chapter break comes a verse late.** Verse 1 is the last line of the teaching that began in 8:31. Chapter numbers were added more than a thousand years after Mark wrote, and here the saying belongs with the warning about being ashamed of the Son of Man "when he comes in the glory of his Father" (8:38).
- **"Some standing here who will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God after it has come with power" (v1).** "Truly, I say to you" marks a solemn promise. What it refers to is debated: Jesus' resurrection, Pentecost, the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, or the transfiguration that follows at once. Mark places it straight before the mountain scene, and "some" fits the three who go up with him. Peter later describes the transfiguration as a preview of Christ's "power and coming" (2 Pet 1:16–18). Many readers see the whole sequence of cross, resurrection and Pentecost as the kingdom arriving "with power," with the transfiguration as its first glimpse.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads the promise as the kingdom "now near approaching," fulfilled in the gospel's spread after the resurrection, with the transfiguration as a pledge of it. The Tyndale notes favor the transfiguration as Mark's intended fulfillment because of where he places it.

#### The Transfiguration (vv2–13)
- **"After six days" (v2).** Mark rarely dates anything outside the passion week, so the link to Peter's confession is deliberate. Luke says "about eight days" (Luke 9:28), counting inclusively. Some hear an echo of Exodus 24:16, where the cloud covered Sinai six days before God called Moses up.
- **"Peter and James and John… a high mountain" (v2).** The same three saw Jairus's daughter raised (5:37) and will be near Jesus in Gethsemane (14:33). Tradition names Mount Tabor in Galilee, but Tabor is low and had a fortress on top in Jesus' day. Many scholars prefer Mount Hermon, about 9,200 feet high and close to Caesarea Philippi. The text does not say.
- **"He was transfigured before them" (v2).** The verb is *metamorphoō*, to be transformed, from which English gets "metamorphosis." It is used of Christ only here and in Matthew 17:2. Paul uses the same verb of believers being "transformed" by the renewing of the mind (Rom 12:2) and into Christ's image (2 Cor 3:18). Matthew and Luke add that his face shone. When Moses came down from Sinai his face reflected a glory that came from outside him (Exod 34:29–35). Here the glory is Jesus' own.
- **"As no one on earth could bleach them" (v3).** A "fuller" (the older word in this verse) cleaned and whitened cloth with lye and by beating it. Mark's homely comparison sounds like an eyewitness. Early tradition holds that Mark wrote down Peter's preaching, and details like this fit that.
- **"Elijah with Moses" (v4).** Moses gave the Law and Elijah stands for the Prophets, so between them they represent the Old Testament as a whole, now talking with the one it pointed to. Both had met God on a mountain (Exod 33–34; 1 Kings 19:8–18). Both also left this life in unusual ways: Moses buried by God himself (Deut 34:5–6) and Elijah taken up in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11). Luke adds that they spoke of Jesus' "departure," literally his "exodus," which he would accomplish at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31).
- **"Let us make three tents" (v5).** "Tents" are booths like those of the Feast of Booths (Lev 23:42–43), which recalled Israel's wilderness journey and looked ahead to God dwelling with his people. Peter may want to make the moment last. He calls Jesus "Rabbi," and his plan sets Jesus beside Moses and Elijah as one great man among three. Mark is gentle with him: "he did not know what to say, for they were terrified" (v6).
- **"A cloud overshadowed them" (v7).** This is the cloud of God's presence that led Israel (Exod 13:21), covered Sinai (Exod 24:15–16) and filled the tabernacle (Exod 40:34–35). Later Jewish writers called that presence the *Shekinah*, the dwelling glory, though the word itself does not appear in the Bible.
- **"This is my beloved Son; listen to him" (v7).** At Jesus' baptism the Father spoke to Jesus (1:11). Now he speaks to the disciples. "Listen to him" echoes Moses' promise that God would raise up a prophet like him: "it is to him you shall listen" (Deut 18:15). Coming six days after Peter rebuked Jesus for talking about the cross (8:32), the command carries an edge. Listen to him especially when he speaks of suffering.
- **"Jesus only" (v8).** Moses and Elijah fade. The Law and the Prophets were never meant to share the stage with the Son.
- **"Tell no one… until the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (v9).** This is the last of Mark's commands to silence, and the only one with an end date. Before the cross, a glorious Messiah story would only feed hopes of a political king. After the resurrection the glory can be understood rightly.
- **"Questioning what this rising from the dead might mean" (v10).** Most Jews believed in a resurrection of all the righteous at the end of history (Dan 12:2). One man rising alone, in the middle of history, made no sense to them yet.
- **"Why do the scribes say that first Elijah must come?" (v11).** Malachi 4:5–6 promised that God would send Elijah before the great day of the LORD. If they had just seen Elijah, why was the Messiah talking about dying?
- **"Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they pleased" (vv12–13).** Jesus affirms the scribes' teaching and then turns it. Elijah's promised work has been done by John the Baptist, who came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17; Matt 17:13). Herod and Herodias did to John what Ahab and Jezebel tried to do to Elijah (1 Kings 19:2; Mark 6:17–29). If the forerunner suffered, so will the Son of Man, "as it is written" (Isa 53; Ps 22). Jesus' question in v12 is the key: how can a Messiah who suffers fit with an Elijah who restores? Both are in Scripture.
- **Commentators:** All three see Moses and Elijah as the Law and the Prophets bowing to Christ. Guzik stresses that the glory came from within Jesus rather than shining on him, and adds the thought that the greater wonder is how Jesus normally kept that glory hidden. The lexicon supports "transform" but cannot by itself settle "from within"; that point rests on the contrast with Moses' face. Guzik reads Peter's three tents as putting Jesus on a level with the others, and the Tyndale notes agree, calling the voice a rebuke. Guzik holds that John fulfilled Elijah's role and spirit without being Elijah returned, and he leaves room for a future Elijah-like ministry before Christ's return (Rev 11:3–6). Henry finds in the scene a foretaste of heaven, yet notes Peter had to come down, because the time for heaven was not yet.

#### Jesus Heals a Boy with an Unclean Spirit (vv14–29)
- **Coming down from the mountain (v14).** Like Moses descending Sinai to find Israel's golden calf (Exod 32), Jesus comes down to confusion and failure. Nine disciples are in an argument with scribes in front of a crowd, and the boy is still suffering.
- **"Greatly amazed" (v15).** The word is strong, used elsewhere in Mark only of Gethsemane (14:33) and the empty tomb (16:5–6). Some think a trace of the mountain glory still showed on Jesus, as on Moses' face. Others think his sudden arrival was enough. Mark does not explain.
- **"A spirit that makes him mute" (vv17–18).** The symptoms (seizures, foaming, grinding teeth, rigidity) resemble epilepsy, and Matthew uses a word that means "moonstruck," the ancient term for seizures (Matt 17:15). Mark is clear that here the cause is a demon (vv25–26). The Bible distinguishes illness from demonic oppression elsewhere (1:32–34), so this text does not teach that all seizures are demonic.
- **"They were not able" (v18).** The same men had cast out many demons on their mission (6:7, 13). Success had perhaps become something they assumed they could repeat.
- **"O faithless generation, how long am I to be with you?" (v19).** The sigh echoes God's frustration with Israel in the wilderness (Num 14:11; Deut 32:5, 20). It falls on the whole scene: disciples, scribes, crowd and the father.
- **"From childhood" (vv21–22).** Jesus asks a question that draws out the father's story. Years of fire and water and near-death lie behind the man's weary words: "if you can do anything, have compassion on us." Note "us." The whole family has suffered.
- **"'If you can'! All things are possible for one who believes" (v23).** Jesus repeats the man's own words back to him. The question is not whether Jesus can, but whether the man will trust him. Some later manuscripts read "If you can believe," which smooths the grammar. The ESV follows the older, more abrupt reading.
- **"I believe; help my unbelief!" (v24).** This is one of the most honest prayers in the Bible. The father does not claim perfect faith. He brings his mixed, wavering faith to Jesus and asks him to make up what is lacking. Faith is measured less by how strong it feels than by where it turns.
- **"You mute and deaf spirit, I command you" (v25).** The "I" is emphatic in Greek: I, not your disciples. "Never enter him again" promises a permanent cure (compare Matt 12:43–45). Jesus acts as the crowd is gathering, before the scene can turn into a show.
- **"Like a corpse… He is dead… he arose" (vv26–27).** The demon's last violence leaves the boy looking dead. Jesus takes him by the hand and raises him, using the same verbs Mark uses for Jairus's daughter (5:41–42) and for resurrection itself. It is a small picture of what Jesus has just predicted for himself.
- **"This kind cannot be driven out by anything but prayer" (v29).** Many manuscripts add "and fasting," which the KJV follows. The oldest manuscripts lack it, and most scholars think it was added as fasting grew in importance in the early church. Either way, the point is the same. The disciples had treated Jesus' authority as a power they owned. Jesus points them back to dependence on God. The scene began with a prayerless argument and ends with a call to prayer.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the case was the harder because it came "of a child," long-rooted, and draws the lesson that old habits of sin likewise resist easy cures. On v23 he and JFB both see Jesus turning the father's "if" from Christ's power to the father's faith, and JFB describes the father appealing to Christ "against his felt unbelief." JFB thinks the crowd's amazement came from lingering traces of "His transfiguration-glory." Guzik adds that prayer and fasting do not earn power, they build dependence, and he suggests that some demons are stronger than others, drawing on Ephesians 6:12. That last point is his inference, not something the text states.

#### Jesus Again Foretells Death, Resurrection (vv30–32)
- **"He did not want anyone to know" (v30).** The public Galilean ministry is ending. Jesus now gives his time to teaching the Twelve.
- **"Delivered into the hands of men" (v31).** This is the second of three passion predictions (8:31; 10:33–34). "Delivered" (*paradidōmi*) is the word used for Judas's betrayal (14:10), for the leaders handing Jesus to Pilate (15:1) and for Pilate handing him to be crucified (15:15). Behind all those human hands, Isaiah's servant was "delivered" for our sins (Isa 53:6, 12 in the Greek Old Testament; Rom 4:25). The present tense, "is being delivered," suggests the process has already begun.
- **"They did not understand… and were afraid to ask him" (v32).** The last time Peter questioned this teaching he was called "Satan" (8:33). The silence is fear, and perhaps also a wish not to know.

#### Who Is the Greatest? (vv33–37)
- **"What were you discussing on the way?" (v33).** "On the way" is a quiet theme in this part of Mark. Jesus is on the way to the cross (10:32). His disciples, on the same road, are arguing about rank.
- **"They kept silent" (v34).** They know the answer would shame them. Rank mattered in that world. Synagogue seating, table places and even the order of names were status markers, and the disciples assumed the coming kingdom would have its own pecking order.
- **"He sat down" (v35).** Teachers sat to teach (Matt 5:1; Luke 4:20). This is a formal lesson.
- **"Last of all and servant of all" (v35).** Jesus does not tell them to stop wanting to be first. He redefines what first is. The word for servant, *diakonos*, means one who waits on others, as at a table. Jesus himself is the pattern: "the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve" (10:45).
- **"He took a child… in his arms" (v36).** Children in the ancient world were loved by their families but had no status, no rights and no power to repay anyone. Aramaic used one word, *talya*, for both "child" and "servant," which may lie behind the move from v35 to v36. Welcoming a child meant spending time on someone who could do nothing for the welcomer's reputation.
- **"Receives me… receives… him who sent me" (v37).** To welcome the lowliest person in Jesus' name is to welcome Jesus, and through him the Father. The greatest honor in the kingdom comes through the humblest service.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the disciples' silence shows conscience at work, and puts the lesson as measuring greatness by whom one serves rather than by who serves oneself. Henry says Christ rebukes them "for magnifying themselves," and observes that the argument broke out right after Jesus spoke of his suffering. The Tyndale notes add that children were not romanticized in Jesus' day but were seen as weak and of little account, which sharpens the point.

#### Anyone Not Against Us Is for Us (vv38–41)
- **"We tried to stop him, because he was not following us" (v38).** John, one of the three who had just seen the glory, now shows a narrow streak (compare Luke 9:54, where he wants to call down fire on a Samaritan village). Notice the word "us." John's complaint is not that the man failed to follow Jesus, but that he was not part of their group. The irony is plain: this outsider was casting out demons, which the disciples had just failed to do (v18). Numbers 11:26–29 has a close parallel, when Joshua wanted Moses to stop two men prophesying outside the camp and Moses replied, "Would that all the LORD's people were prophets."
- **"Do not stop him" (v39).** Someone who truly does mighty works by Jesus' name is unlikely to turn and slander him soon after. This is not a blanket approval of anyone who uses the name (Matt 7:22–23; Acts 19:13–16). It is a warning against rivalry among those who belong to Christ.
- **"The one who is not against us is for us" (v40).** Elsewhere Jesus says, "Whoever is not with me is against me" (Luke 11:23; Matt 12:30). The two sayings fit together. Toward Jesus himself, neutrality is impossible. Toward other followers of Jesus, his people should assume the best rather than the worst. Luke records both sayings (Luke 9:50; 11:23).
- **"A cup of water… because you belong to Christ" (v41).** Even the smallest kindness done to Christ's people for his sake will be rewarded. "Belong to Christ" is literally "you are of Christ," one of the few places in Mark where "Christ" is used almost as a name.
- **Commentators:** JFB says plainly that John was "wrong" and that such a man should be seen as "a reverer of His name and a promoter of His cause." Guzik suggests the man might have been a disciple of John the Baptist or one of a wider circle of followers, a guess the text neither confirms nor rules out. Henry presses the lesson that Christ's servants should not discourage anyone doing good merely because "he follows not with us."

#### Temptations to Sin (vv42–50)
- **"Causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin" (v42).** The verb is *skandalizō*, from *skandalon*, the trigger of a trap or a stumbling block. "Little ones" ties back to the child of v36 but widens to any humble believer. Leading such a person into sin, or driving them from faith, is so serious that drowning would be better.
- **"A great millstone" (v42).** Literally "a donkey's millstone," the large upper stone of a grain mill that was turned by a donkey, as opposed to a small hand mill. The picture is of certain, unrecoverable death in the sea. Romans sometimes executed people by drowning, which made it vivid.
- **Hand, foot and eye (vv43–47).** The language is deliberately extreme. Jesus is not calling for self-harm, which would not touch sin's real source in the heart (7:20–23). He is saying that nothing, not even something as dear as a hand or an eye, is worth keeping if it leads to ruin. Whatever feeds sin, however valued, should be cut off decisively.
- **"Hell, to the unquenchable fire" (v43).** "Hell" translates *Geenna*, from the Hebrew "Valley of Hinnom" just south of Jerusalem. Kings Ahaz and Manasseh burned children there in sacrifice to Molech (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6; Jer 7:31), and Josiah defiled it to end the practice (2 Kings 23:10). By Jesus' day it had become a name for final judgment. The common claim that it was a burning city rubbish dump in Jesus' time comes from much later sources and is uncertain. The name's dark history is enough to explain the image.
- **Missing verses 44 and 46.** The ESV jumps from v43 to v45 and from v45 to v47. Some later manuscripts repeat v48 after v43 and v45, which is where the KJV's verses 44 and 46 come from. The oldest manuscripts lack them, and most scholars think a scribe copied the refrain to round out the pattern. Nothing in the teaching is lost.
- **"Where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched" (v48).** This quotes the last verse of Isaiah (66:24), which describes the corpses of those who rebelled against God outside the new Jerusalem. Isaiah ends with new heavens and new earth, and also with this warning. Jesus takes it on his own lips without softening it.
- **"Everyone will be salted with fire" (v49).** One of the hardest verses in Mark. Under the Law every grain offering was to be salted (Lev 2:13), and Paul calls believers "living sacrifices" (Rom 12:1). The likeliest sense is that every disciple will pass through purifying trial, as gold is tested by fire (1 Pet 1:7; 4:12). Some take "everyone" to include the lost, salted with the fire of judgment just described. Others see the fire as the Holy Spirit (Matt 3:11). Some manuscripts add "and every sacrifice will be salted with salt," an early scribe's way of explaining the verse from Leviticus.
- **"If the salt has lost its saltiness" (v50).** Pure salt cannot lose its taste, but salt from the Dead Sea area was mixed with other minerals. When damp leached out the real salt, what remained looked like salt and was useless. A disciple who loses the distinctive mark of following Jesus is like that.
- **"Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another" (v50).** The chapter ends where its trouble began. The disciples argued about who was greatest (v34) and tried to shut out a fellow worker (v38). Salt in the Old Testament also sealed covenants and friendships, as in "a covenant of salt" (Num 18:19; 2 Chr 13:5), and eating salt together was a sign of fellowship. Disciples seasoned by the cross will be at peace with each other.
- **Commentators:** All three read the hand, foot and eye as figurative. Guzik observes that cutting off a limb would leave the sinful heart untouched, and the Tyndale notes say the same. JFB reads "salted with fire" as a "fiery trial" that seasons the disciple, tying it to the rule that "every acceptable sacrifice must be sprinkled with salt." Guzik gives two readings, fire as the suffering of a living sacrifice or as the purifying work of the Spirit, and does not choose firmly between them. Guzik repeats the familiar idea that Gehenna was a burning rubbish dump. That idea is widespread, including in the Tyndale notes, but the evidence for it is late, so it is best held loosely.

#### Pointing to Christ
On the mountain the disciples saw Jesus' real glory and heard the Father say, "Listen to him." Then they came down and heard him say again that he would be delivered into human hands and killed. Mark puts the two side by side on purpose. The glory of the Son is the glory of the one who serves, who takes a powerless child in his arms, who raises a boy who looks dead and who goes to the cross for people who are still arguing about rank. The father's prayer, "I believe; help my unbelief," is the right response to such a Lord: not confident strength, but weak faith that turns to him.
