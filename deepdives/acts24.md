*Setting:* Caesarea Maritima, about AD 57. This was Herod the Great's showpiece harbor city and the Roman capital of Judea. Paul is held in Herod's former palace, now the governor's headquarters. Five days after Paul's night ride from Jerusalem, his accusers arrive for a formal Roman trial before the governor Felix. Luke gives about equal space to the charges, Paul's defense and the governor's response. Through it all the gospel is on trial alongside the man.

#### Paul Before Felix at Caesarea (vv1–23)
- **"The high priest Ananias came down with some elders and a spokesman, one Tertullus" (v1).** The high priest himself makes the two-day trip, which shows how badly they wanted Paul condemned. Tertullus (a Latin name) is a professional advocate (*rhētōr*) who knows how Roman courts work. "Came down" is literal: Jerusalem sits high in the hills and Caesarea is on the coast.
- **"Through you we enjoy much peace" (vv2–4).** A speech was expected to open with praise of the judge (the *captatio benevolentiae*), but this is lavish. Felix had put down bandits and the Egyptian rebel (21:38), so "peace" had some truth to it. But he was also brutal and corrupt, and Jewish leaders later accused him before Nero. "To detain you no further" is a polite formula that promises a brevity the speech doesn't deliver.
- **"A plague… stirs up riots among all the Jews throughout the world… ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes" (v5).** Three charges are carefully chosen for a Roman ear:
  - "Plague" (*loimos*, pestilence) labels Paul a public menace.
  - Stirring up riots "throughout the world" (*oikoumenē*, the Roman world) is sedition, the charge Rome feared most. The same charge was brought against Jesus before Pilate (Luke 23:2, 5).
  - "Ringleader of the sect (*hairesis*) of the Nazarenes" marks him as the leader of a suspect movement. "Nazarenes" is the earliest Jewish name for Christians and is still the word for Christians in Hebrew and Arabic.
- **"He even tried to profane the temple, but we seized him" (v6).** In 21:28 the charge was that he *had* defiled it. Now it is only that he *tried*. The case is already weakening. Here the KJV adds verses 6b–8a: "and would have judged according to our law. But the chief captain Lysias came upon us…" These words are missing from the earliest manuscripts, so the ESV leaves them out and skips verse 7. If they are original, they turn the blame on Lysias.
- **"The Jews also joined in the charge" (v9).** The delegation backs up the lawyer, but no eyewitness is produced.
- **"I cheerfully make my defense" (v10).** Paul gives no flattery, just a plain courtesy: Felix had been governor "for many years," about five or six by now, so he knows Jewish affairs. "Make my defense" is *apologeomai* (22:1). Jesus promised words would be given in such moments (Luke 21:14–15).
- **"Not more than twelve days since I went up to worship" (v11).** Twelve days is not enough time to organize a revolt. And he came "to worship," not to make trouble.
- **"They did not find me disputing… or stirring up a crowd" (vv12–13).** He answers the charge of sedition with facts: no arguments, no crowds, not in the temple, the synagogues or the city. "Neither can they prove" is the decisive line. Roman law required evidence, and they have none.
- **"According to the Way, which they call a sect, I worship the God of our fathers" (v14).** He admits the second charge but recasts it. "The Way" is not a new religion. It is the true worship of Israel's God, believing "everything laid down by the Law and written in the Prophets." This mattered legally too. Judaism was a recognized, legal religion in the empire, and Paul is saying the Way belongs inside it, not outside.
- **"A resurrection of both the just and the unjust" (v15).** This is the hope of Daniel 12:2, which the Pharisees in the delegation shared. Jesus taught the same (John 5:28–29). It is the only explicit mention in Acts of a resurrection of the unrighteous. It sets up the "coming judgment" that will frighten Felix in v25.
- **"I always take pains to have a clear conscience toward both God and man" (v16).** "Take pains" (*askeō*, the root of "ascetic") means to exercise or train. A clear conscience is not automatic for Paul. He works at it, because he lives in light of the judgment (2 Cor 5:10).
- **"I came to bring alms to my nation and to present offerings" (v17).** This is the only mention in Acts of the collection that dominates Paul's letters (Rom 15:25–28; 1 Cor 16:1–4; 2 Cor 8–9). He came to Jerusalem to give, not to riot.
- **"Some Jews from Asia—they ought to be here" (vv18–19).** The sentence breaks off. The only real witnesses to the temple incident never showed up. Under Roman law accusers who abandoned their charge could be penalized, which may be why they stayed away.
- **"Other than this one thing… the resurrection of the dead" (vv20–21).** The only thing the council could fault him for was a theological statement that half of them agreed with (23:6–9).
- **"Felix, having a rather accurate knowledge of the Way, put them off" (v22).** Felix had governed for years in Caesarea, where the church had existed since Cornelius (ch 10), and his Jewish wife (v24) would have known the movement too. He knows Christians aren't revolutionaries. Instead of acquitting Paul, which would anger the Jewish leaders, he adjourns until Lysias comes, a classic delaying move. There's no record that Lysias ever came.
- **"Kept in custody but have some liberty" (v23).** This is light custody suited to an unconvicted Roman citizen. Friends may bring food and care. Luke and Aristarchus were likely among them (27:2).
- **Commentators:** JFB sorts Tertullus's charges sharply. It calls his opening "fulsome flattery" with "a semblance of truth: nothing more." It judges the ringleader charge "true enough" and the temple charge "entirely false," and if the longer text of v7 is read, its claim about Lysias is "a wilful falsehood." Guzik agrees the temple charge was built on rumor alone, with no evidence because none existed. Henry marvels at the prosecutors' speed, ready in five days: "what will not malice do?" He contrasts Paul's patience, waiting for the judge's nod rather than interrupting, and he makes "no reflections at all upon Tertullus, who he knew spoke for his fee." Henry guesses that Felix learned of the Way through Cornelius and the believers at Caesarea, which is plausible but not stated. JFB connects Paul's clear conscience in v16 to "that awful day" of judgment (2 Cor 5:10).

#### Paul Kept in Custody (vv24–27)
- **"Felix came with his wife Drusilla, who was Jewish" (v24).** Drusilla was the youngest daughter of Herod Agrippa I, who killed James and was struck down by God (12:1–2, 23). That made her the sister of Agrippa II and Bernice (25:13). As a teenager she was married to the king of Emesa. Felix, with the help of a magician (Josephus says), lured her away from her husband. She was still under twenty. Josephus adds that their son later died in the eruption of Vesuvius (AD 79).
- **"He heard him speak about faith in Christ Jesus" (v24).** A private audience with a captive preacher. Curiosity, perhaps, or Drusilla's interest in her own people's debates.
- **"Righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment" (v25).** Paul preaches to the couple in front of him. **Righteousness** before a corrupt judge. **Self-control** (*enkrateia*, mastery of one's desires) before a man who had taken another man's wife. **The coming judgment** before a man who judged others. It is the same sequence Jesus said the Spirit would bring: sin, righteousness, judgment (John 16:8).
- **"Felix was alarmed" (v25).** The word (*emphobos*) means terrified. The prisoner is calm, and the judge trembles. "Go away for the present. When I get an opportunity (*kairos*) I will summon you." Felix did send for him again, but there's no sign the "opportunity" to believe ever came.
- **"He hoped that money would be given him" (v26).** Felix had just heard Paul mention bringing "alms" (v17) and assumed there was money behind him. Taking bribes for releasing prisoners was illegal under Roman law but common. "He sent for him often and conversed with him." For two years Felix heard the gospel from the greatest missionary of the age, and listened for a bribe.
- **"When two years had elapsed, Felix was succeeded by Porcius Festus" (v27).** Felix was recalled to Rome about AD 59 after a violent clash between Jews and Gentiles in Caesarea that he had handled harshly. He escaped punishment only through his brother Pallas. **"Desiring to do the Jews a favor, Felix left Paul in prison."** He left an innocent man in chains as a parting gift to win favor with his accusers.
- **Two years in Caesarea.** Luke passes over them in one sentence, but they likely mattered. Many scholars think Luke used this time to research his Gospel and gather eyewitness accounts across Judea and Galilee (Luke 1:1–4). Some place Paul's prison letters here, though most date them to Rome.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the private meetings a conversation in which "the prisoner hoped to do good to the judge and the judge thought to get money by the prisoner, but both in vain." He notes the irony that after all the commotion over him, Paul is now left to be "as much neglected as there had been ado about him." JFB observes Paul's message "took an entirely practical turn, suited to the life which his two auditors were notoriously leading." Guzik focuses on Felix's "convenient time" as one of the most dangerous responses to the gospel. Putting off Christ is not neutral, he argues, but a way of refusing him. The Tyndale notes add that Josephus himself criticized Drusilla's marriage. Its point is that she and Felix came face to face with the judgment they were living in defiance of.

#### Pointing to Christ
Paul stands where Jesus stood: falsely charged with sedition before a Roman governor, the case clearly empty, the judge too weak to do right, and the prisoner kept in chains to please the crowd. Pilate wanted to release Jesus and didn't. Felix knew Paul was innocent and didn't release him. But the one who preached "righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment" was not afraid of that judgment. The Judge himself had stood in his place. Felix trembled and put it off. The same message still calls every hearer to settle it "now… now is the day of salvation" (2 Cor 6:2).
