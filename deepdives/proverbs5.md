*Setting:* Another of the father's talks in Proverbs 1–9 (see Proverbs 1 on authorship). In chapter 2 the father briefly named the "forbidden woman" whose house sinks down to death (2:16–19). Chapters 5, 6 and 7 come back to her at length, which shows how seriously the father takes this danger for a young man setting out in life. Chapter 4 ended with the call to keep the heart and walk a straight road without swerving. Chapter 5 shows the most common swerve. But it is not only a warning. Its center (vv15–19) is a frank and joyful celebration of married love. The father does not just say no. He points his son to a better yes. Egyptian instruction books also warned young men against other men's wives, but Proverbs grounds the warning in the eyes of the LORD (v21).

#### Warning Against Adultery (vv1–23)
- **"Be attentive to my wisdom… that you may keep discretion, and your lips may guard knowledge" (vv1–2).** The father's lips should guard knowledge because another set of lips is about to speak (v3). The battle is between two voices.
- **"The lips of a forbidden woman drip honey, and her speech is smoother than oil" (v3).** "Forbidden woman" is literally "the strange woman" (*zarah*), a woman outside the bounds, most often another man's wife (see 2:16–17). The danger starts with words. Honey and oil are pleasant and smooth. Song of Songs uses "your lips drip nectar" of a bride's kisses (Song 4:11). The same sweetness that belongs in marriage is here used to lure.
- **"But in the end she is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a two-edged sword" (v4).** "In the end" (*ʾacharit*) is a key idea in Proverbs: what comes afterward. JFB: "literally, 'her future,'" what follows. Wormwood (*laʿanah*) is a plant with a famously bitter taste, a byword for bitterness and poison (Deut 29:18; Lam 3:15; Rev 8:11). "Two-edged" is literally "a sword of mouths," a sword that cuts both ways. It starts sweet and ends cutting.
- **"Her feet go down to death; her steps follow the path to Sheol" (v5).** As in 2:18, her house is the doorway to the grave.
- **"She does not ponder the path of life; her ways wander, and she does not know it" (v6).** "Ponder" (*palles*) is the word the father just used: "Ponder the path of your feet" (4:26). She does not weigh her steps, and her life drifts without her noticing. JFB prefers a related sense, that her ways are changeable, shifting so that no one sees her true character. Either way she is unstable, and a man who follows her follows someone with no direction.
- **"Keep your way far from her, and do not go near the door of her house" (v8).** The father's strategy is distance. He does not say "be strong when you're there." He says don't go there. Paul says the same: "Flee from sexual immorality" (1 Cor 6:18; 2 Tim 2:22), and Joseph did exactly that (Gen 39:12). JFB: "Avoid the slightest temptation."
- **What adultery costs (vv9–10).** The father lists losses: **"your honor,"** reputation and dignity. **"Your years to the merciless,"** perhaps a vengeful husband (6:34–35), or simply the hard world that takes advantage of a ruined man. **"Strangers take their fill of your strength, and your labors go to the house of a foreigner."** His wealth and work end up in others' hands, whether through payment, blackmail, legal penalty or the cost of a broken home. Under the law adultery carried the death penalty (Lev 20:10), though in practice the shame and the payment to an injured husband were more common outcomes (6:35).
- **"At the end of your life you groan, when your flesh and body are consumed" (v11).** "Groan" is the deep growl of a lion (*naham*), a cry of pain. The wasting of "flesh and body" may point to disease that came from immoral sex, which was known in the ancient world (the Tyndale notes mention Mesopotamian texts about it), or to the general ruin of a life. JFB: "the whole person under incurable disease."
- **"How I hated discipline, and my heart despised reproof!" (v12).** The father lets his son hear his own future words of regret. "I did not listen to the voice of my teachers" (v13). Regret is the last stage. The man saw the warning sign and drove past it.
- **"I am at the brink of utter ruin in the assembled congregation" (v14).** The "assembly" (*qahal*) and "congregation" (*ʿedah*) are two words for the gathered community of Israel, which also served as a court. His sin, which began in private, ends in public disgrace before the whole people.
- **"Drink water from your own cistern, flowing water from your own well" (v15).** The turn to the positive. In a dry land, water was precious, and a family's own cistern or well was a treasure. The picture is sexual love within marriage. Song of Songs uses the same images of a bride: "a garden locked… a spring locked, a fountain sealed… a well of living water" (Song 4:12, 15). The wife is the husband's own well. He does not need to steal from another's.
- **"Should your springs be scattered abroad, streams of water in the streets?" (v16).** This verse is hard and read two ways. The ESV takes it as a question expecting "no": should a man's sexual energy be poured out in public, wasted on strangers? Others read it as a picture of what happens to a home when a husband strays: the waters flow away, his wife may be driven to stray too, or children are born outside the marriage. JFB reads the waters as the children of a faithful marriage. Either way, the answer comes in v17: **"Let them be for yourself alone, and not for strangers with you."**
- **"Let your fountain be blessed, and rejoice in the wife of your youth" (v18).** "Wife of your youth" means the woman he married when young, the same phrase Malachi uses for the covenant wife (Mal 2:14–15). The father does not just tolerate marriage. He calls it a blessing and calls the son to rejoice in it, year after year, as she grows older with him.
- **"A lovely deer, a graceful doe" (v19).** The deer and the doe were images of beauty and grace in ancient love poetry (Song 2:9; 4:5). This is affectionate, not crude.
- **"Let her breasts fill you at all times with delight; be intoxicated always in her love" (v19).** Scripture is not embarrassed by married sexual love. "Fill you… with delight" is literally "satisfy you." "Be intoxicated" is *shagah*, a verb that means to reel or stagger, as a drunk man does, and so to be lost in love. JFB: "literally, 'intoxicated,' that is, fully satisfied."
- **"Why should you be intoxicated, my son, with a forbidden woman?" (v20).** Here is the wordplay. The same verb (*shagah*) appears three times in the chapter: be intoxicated with your wife (v19), why be intoxicated with the forbidden woman (v20), and the fool who "is led astray" (v23). The same verb means both to be enraptured and to stray. Desire will stagger somewhere. The question is where.
- **"For a man's ways are before the eyes of the LORD, and he ponders all his paths" (v21).** "Ponders" is *palles* again (as in v6 and 4:26). The woman does not weigh her paths. The son is told to weigh his. And God weighs them all. There is no secret sin (Heb 4:13). This is the reason under every other reason. Joseph refused Potiphar's wife because "How then can I do this great wickedness and sin against God?" (Gen 39:9). David confessed after his adultery, "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Ps 51:4).
- **"The iniquities of the wicked ensnare him, and he is held fast in the cords of his sin" (v22).** Sin is its own trap. No outside jailer is needed. The sin itself becomes the rope. Henry says sinners "shall be holden in the cords of their own sins," with no need for prison or chains. Jesus: "everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin" (John 8:34).
- **"He dies for lack of discipline, and because of his great folly he is led astray" (v23).** The chapter ends with a man who refused discipline (v12) and so dies, "led astray" (*shagah*), reeling into death like a drunkard.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Solomon warns his son against women because that was the son's own danger, not because men are innocent and women are the problem. Guzik draws the practical point that temptation usually begins with conversation, and that distance is wiser than testing willpower. On v19 he follows Allen Ross, who notes that the verb pictures staggering, as with drink. The lexicon confirms that sense (it lists 5:19–20 under "be intoxicated") and the moral sense "go astray" for v23. Guzik also notes, sadly, that Solomon himself later had many wives and they turned his heart away (1 Kgs 11:3–4). Henry: "Mutual delight is the bond of mutual fidelity," and on v21, "See the eye of God always upon thee and let his fear rule in thy heart." JFB reads the well and cistern as the wife and the "rivers of waters" as the children, and on v9 he suggests that "the cruel" refers to those the sensual themselves become.

#### Pointing to Christ
The father sets two kinds of love side by side: a stolen sweetness that ends in bitterness, and the faithful love of a husband who rejoices in the wife of his youth. Scripture uses that faithful love as a picture of Christ's love for his church (Eph 5:25–32). He loved her and gave himself for her, and he does not stray. Where people have been caught in "the cords of their sin," Jesus came "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18). The eyes of the LORD that see every path also look with mercy on those who turn back. David, who knew this sin well, found it: "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven" (Ps 32:1).
