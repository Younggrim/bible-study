*Setting:* Some time after Nabal's death and David's marriages to Abigail and Ahinoam (25:38–43). David is still in the wilderness of southern Judah with his six hundred men. The men of Ziph, who betrayed him once before (23:19–24), go to Saul again. Saul had wept at En-gedi and promised to do David no harm (24:16–22), but the promise did not last. This chapter is the second time God puts Saul into David's hands, and it is clearly told as a pair with chapter 24. The lesson David learned from Abigail, that God will deal with his enemies and he does not need to "save with his own hand" (25:26, 39), is now put to work. It is also the last time David and Saul ever meet.

#### David Spares Saul Again (vv1–25)
- **"The hill of Hachilah, which is on the east of Jeshimon" (v1).** This is the same hiding place the Ziphites reported before (23:19). *Jeshimon* means "wasteland," the barren desert falling toward the Dead Sea. The hills there are full of caves and ravines.
- **"Three thousand chosen men of Israel" (v2).** The same force as at En-gedi (24:2). Nothing has changed in Saul's heart except, for a short while, his feelings.
- **"David sent out spies" (v4).** Last time Saul stumbled into David's cave by chance. This time David knows where Saul is and goes to him. He is a careful commander, and he takes the initiative.
- **"Abner the son of Ner, the commander of his army" (v5).** Abner was Saul's relative, probably his cousin (14:50–51), and the strongest man in his kingdom. After Saul's death he will make Saul's son Ish-bosheth king and fight David for years before coming over to him (2 Sam 2–3). The camp is arranged in a ring, with Saul in the center and the army around him, the safest place in the camp.
- **"Ahimelech the Hittite… Abishai the son of Zeruiah" (v6).** Ahimelech is mentioned only here. He is a foreigner in David's service, like Uriah the Hittite later (2 Sam 11:3). Abishai is David's nephew, the son of his sister Zeruiah (1 Chr 2:16), and the brother of Joab and Asahel. He and Joab will be fierce and loyal soldiers for David, but quick to kill, and David will later say, "these men, the sons of Zeruiah, are more severe than I" (2 Sam 3:39; compare 16:9–10). "I will go down with you" shows Abishai's courage.
- **"His spear stuck in the ground at his head" (v7).** A spear planted upright beside a sleeping man marked the commander's place. Spears often had a metal butt-spike for this purpose. Saul's spear runs through the whole story. He threw it at David twice (18:11; 19:10) and at Jonathan once (20:33), and held it while he ordered the priests killed (22:6). Now it is within David's reach.
- **"God has given your enemy into your hand this day" (v8).** Abishai uses almost the same words as David's men in the cave (24:4). "Let me pin him to the earth with one stroke… I will not strike him twice." The offer is tempting in a new way. David would not even have to do it himself. Abishai would take the blame. And the weapon would be Saul's own spear, the one that had been thrown at David.
- **"Who can put out his hand against the LORD's anointed and be guiltless?" (v9).** David's answer has not changed since En-gedi (24:6). Saul is still *mashiach*, anointed by God's command. Only God can remove him. David would not do it himself, and he will not let someone else do it for him.
- **"The LORD will strike him, or his day will come to die, or he will go down into battle and perish" (v10).** David lists three ways Saul's reign could end, and all of them are in God's hands. He has just seen the first one happen to Nabal: "the LORD struck Nabal, and he died" (25:38). The third is how Saul will actually die, in battle on Mount Gilboa (31:1–6).
- **"Take now the spear… and the jar of water" (v11).** The spear is the symbol of Saul's power. The jar of water was his lifeline in the desert. Both are taken from right beside his head, and both become proof, like the piece of robe at En-gedi.
- **"A deep sleep from the LORD had fallen upon them" (v12).** The Hebrew *tardemah* is the word for the deep sleep God sent on Adam before creating Eve (Gen 2:21) and on Abram when God made his covenant (Gen 15:12). It is not ordinary tiredness. God himself made the way for David to walk in and out of the camp unseen.
- **"Stood far off on the top of the hill, with a great space between them" (v13).** David learned at En-gedi to keep his distance from Saul. In the clear, dry air of the Judean hills, a voice can carry a long way across a ravine.
- **"Will you not answer, Abner?" (v14).** David calls first to the commander, not to the king. His taunt is sharp: "Are you not a man? Who is like you in Israel?" (v15). The greatest soldier in Israel failed to guard his king. "You deserve to die" (v16) is literally "you are sons of death." The irony is that the "enemy" Saul is hunting is the only one in Israel who has actually protected his life.
- **"Is this your voice, my son David?" (v17).** The same words Saul used at En-gedi (24:16). David replies with respect: "It is my voice, my lord, O king."
- **"For what have I done? What evil is on my hands?" (v18).** David has asked this before (20:1). He has never had an answer.
- **"If it is the LORD who has stirred you up against me, may he accept an offering" (v19).** David weighs two possibilities. If God is behind Saul's hostility, perhaps because David has sinned without knowing it, then a sacrifice can make peace. "Accept an offering" is literally "smell an offering," the language of a sacrifice that pleases God (Gen 8:21). If men are behind it, they are under God's curse. David is once again giving Saul a way out without blaming him directly.
- **"No share in the heritage of the LORD, saying, 'Go, serve other gods'" (v19).** This is David's deepest grief. The land of Israel was the LORD's inheritance, the place where his people worshiped him at his altar. To be driven out of it, into foreign lands, was to be cut off from the worship of the true God, as if being told to go and worship idols. Psalm 137 voices the same pain in exile: "How shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?" David is about to face exactly that temptation (27:1).
- **"Let not my blood fall to the earth away from the presence of the LORD" (v20).** David asks not to die far from God's land and people.
- **"A single flea like one who hunts a partridge in the mountains" (v20).** David returns to the flea image from En-gedi (24:14) and adds a new one. The partridge was hunted by chasing it until it tired and then striking it with a stick, much as Saul is wearing David down. There may also be wordplay. The Hebrew word for partridge, *qore'*, means "caller," and David is standing on a mountain "calling" (*qara'*, v14).
- **"I have sinned… I have acted foolishly, and have made a great mistake" (v21).** This sounds like confession, and it is more than Saul said at En-gedi. "Acted foolishly" (*sakal*) is the word Samuel used when Saul offered the sacrifice himself: "You have done foolishly" (13:13). The verse could stand as a summary of Saul's whole reign. But he still does not turn to God. He invites David home, and David does not go.
- **"Here is the spear, O king!" (v22).** David returns the spear. He does not want trophies of Saul's humiliation. "Let one of the young men come over and take it" keeps his distance.
- **"The LORD rewards every man for his righteousness and his faithfulness" (v23).** David trusts God, not Saul, to repay him. "Faithfulness" (*'emunah*) means steady loyalty. David has kept faith with Saul. He now asks God to keep faith with him.
- **"As your life was precious this day in my sight, so may my life be precious in the sight of the LORD" (v24).** This is the key line. David does not rely on Saul's promise. He commits himself to God. It echoes the principle Jesus would teach, "with the measure you use it will be measured to you" (Matt 7:2).
- **"Blessed be you, my son David! You will do many things and will succeed in them" (v25).** Saul's last words to David are a blessing, almost a prophecy. "So David went his way, and Saul returned to his place." They never see each other again.
- **Two accounts.** Chapters 24 and 26 share a pattern: betrayal by informers, Saul in David's power, a companion urging the kill, David's refusal because Saul is the LORD's anointed, a token taken, a speech from a distance, Saul's confession. Some scholars think they are two tellings of one event. But nearly every detail differs (cave and camp, a lone king and a sleeping army, robe and spear, unnamed men and Abishai, a different place and speech), and the second shows David growing. In the cave his conscience struck him for cutting Saul's robe. Here he acts without hesitation and with clear purpose. Most readers take them as two real events, set on either side of the Nabal story, showing that David's restraint was a settled conviction.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes how quickly Saul's tears at En-gedi faded: "How soon do unholy hearts lose the good impressions convictions have made upon them!" He says David "will by no means force his way to the promised crown by any wrong methods." On Saul's confession he writes, "In sinning against God, men play the fool, and err exceedingly," but warns, "Fair professions do not entitle those to confidence who have long sinned against the light." His summary is simple: "David and Saul parted to meet no more." JFB says "David's magnanimity soared far above the notions of his followers," and explains that "the Oriental spear had, and still has, a spike at the lower extremity" for planting it in the ground. Guzik sees Saul's "I have played the fool" as colder than his tears at En-gedi, the regret of a man outmaneuvered rather than repentance. He passes on G. Campbell Morgan's remark that the line is Saul's autobiography. He ties David's three ways the LORD might deal with Saul to Romans 12:19, "vengeance is mine."

#### Pointing to Christ
David walks into the enemy's camp, stands over the sleeping king with the spear in reach, and walks out again without striking. He commits himself to God: "may my life be precious in the sight of the LORD." Jesus went into a far greater darkness. He could have called "more than twelve legions of angels" (Matt 26:53), and he did not. He "continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). David's restraint saved an enemy's life for a day. Jesus' restraint, going to the cross for his enemies, saves forever: "while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son" (Rom 5:10).
