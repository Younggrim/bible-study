*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Israel is camped across the Jordan from Jericho, about to invade a land held by walled cities and armies with chariots. Forty years earlier their parents had refused to go in, terrified by reports of giants and fortified cities (1:26–28). Chapter 19 dealt with killing within Israel. Chapter 20 deals with killing in war. It gives Israel's army its first sermon, its rules for exemption from service, its terms for distant enemies, the hard command about the Canaanite cities, and a surprising limit that protects fruit trees. The Canaanite command is one of the hardest passages in the Bible for modern readers, and it is treated below with care.

#### Laws Concerning Warfare (vv1–20)
- **"Horses and chariots and an army larger than your own" (v1).** This was Israel's normal position. They were a people of foot soldiers, mostly farmers and shepherds, and their enemies had chariots, the most feared weapon of the age. Joshua later faced northern kings with "very many horses and chariots" (Josh 11:4), and the Canaanites of the plains kept Judah out because they had "chariots of iron" (Judg 1:19).
- **"You shall not be afraid of them, for the LORD your God is with you, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (v1).** Fear is answered by memory. The same God had thrown Pharaoh's horses and chariots into the sea (Exod 15:1, 4). If he could beat the greatest army in the world, he could beat these. This verse lies behind Psalm 20:7, "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God."
- **"The priest shall come forward and speak to the people" (v2).** Before the generals spoke, the priest did. Israel's wars were the LORD's wars (Num 21:14), so the first word on the battlefield was about God. Later Jewish writers spoke of a special "priest anointed for war." JFB repeats the tradition, though Scripture does not describe such a ceremony.
- **"Hear, O Israel" (v3).** The priest begins with the words of the Shema (6:4). War does not suspend Israel's faith. It tests it. **"Let not your heart faint. Do not fear or panic or be in dread of them."** Four phrases pile up because fear in battle comes in waves.
- **"The LORD your God is he who goes with you to fight for you… to give you the victory" (v4).** The Tyndale notes say this means God starts these wars, fights them and finishes them. The victory is a gift ("to give you"), not an achievement. At the Red Sea Moses had said, "The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (Exod 14:14).
- **"Is there any man who has built a new house and has not dedicated it?" (v5).** Then the officers (*shoterim*, the same clerks and officials as in 16:18) announce exemptions. "Dedicated" is *chanakh*, the verb used when Solomon dedicated the temple (1 Kgs 8:63) and in "Train up a child" (Prov 22:6). From the same root comes *Hanukkah*, "dedication," the festival Jesus attended in John 10:22. A man was not to die before he had begun to live in the house he had built.
- **"Planted a vineyard and has not enjoyed its fruit" (v6).** A new vineyard could not be harvested for its first years (Lev 19:23–25), so this could mean five years of waiting. **"Betrothed a wife and has not taken her"** (v7). In Israel betrothal was a binding agreement, and the wedding came later. The Tyndale notes point out that a man who died before marrying would leave no children to carry on his name, a great tragedy in Israel. Deuteronomy 24:5 goes further: a newly married man was free from war for a full year "to be happy with his wife."
- **"Lest he die in the battle and another man" (vv5–7).** Each exemption ends with the same refrain. Losing what one had worked for to a stranger was one of the curses for disobedience (28:30). Israel was not to let war bring that curse on obedient men. God cared about ordinary joys: a house, a harvest, a wedding.
- **"Is there any man who is fearful and fainthearted? Let him go back" (v8).** The last exemption is the most surprising. Cowardice was not punished, only sent home, **"lest he make the heart of his fellows melt like his own."** Fear spreads. Gideon applied this law and 22,000 of his 32,000 men went home (Judg 7:3). Then God cut the rest to 300, so that Israel could not boast, "My own hand has saved me" (Judg 7:2). Centuries later Judas Maccabeus applied these same exemptions before a battle against a far larger Greek army (1 Macc 3:56).
- **"Then commanders shall be appointed" (v9).** Only after the army was trimmed to the willing were the officers put in place. Trusting God did not rule out organization.
- **"When you draw near to a city to fight against it, offer terms of peace to it" (v10).** "Peace" is *shalom*. Before a siege, Israel must offer surrender on terms. This applies to **"cities that are very far from you, which are not cities of the nations here"** (v15), that is, wars outside the promised land. **"Forced labor"** (v11) meant the city became a subject people. This was the normal result of surrender in the ancient world. Israel later forced the Canaanite remnants to work on Solomon's building projects (1 Kgs 9:20–21).
- **"If it makes no peace… you shall put all its males to the sword" (vv12–13).** Defenders who refused terms were killed, but **"the women and the little ones"** were spared (v14). That is harsh by any standard, but it was more restrained than the common practice of the time, when Assyrian kings boasted in their inscriptions of flaying and burning whole populations.
- **"But in the cities of these peoples… you shall save alive nothing that breathes" (v16).** Inside the land of Canaan the rule was different. **"You shall devote them to complete destruction"** (v17) is the verb *charam*, to put under the *cherem*, the "ban." Something under the ban was given over wholly to God and could not be kept for anyone's use (Lev 27:28–29). The idea was not unique to Israel. On the Mesha Stele, a Moabite inscription from about 840 BC, King Mesha boasts that he "devoted" the Israelite town of Nebo to his god Chemosh and killed its seven thousand people, using the same word. Six nations are named here. The list in 7:1 also has the Girgashites, and some Greek manuscripts add them here.
- **"That they may not teach you to do according to all their abominable practices" (v18).** This is the reason given. Canaan's religion included child sacrifice and ritual sex, the "abominations" of 12:31 and 18:9–12. Israel was a young nation that had already fallen into idolatry and sexual sin with the Moabites (Num 25). If the Canaanites stayed, they would teach Israel their ways. And that is exactly what happened when Israel did not finish the task (Judg 2:1–3, 11–13; Ps 106:34–38).
- **How should Christians read this command?** Faithful readers hold several points together. First, it was *judgment* on specific nations at one time, delayed for four hundred years because "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (Gen 15:16), and God says the land itself "vomited out" its people for their sins (Lev 18:24–25). Israel was warned that the same would happen to them, and it did. Second, it was never a general command for Israel's wars, let alone for today. Verses 10–15 show that even Israel did not fight this way outside Canaan. Third, it had exceptions for those who turned to Israel's God: Rahab and her family were spared and joined Israel (Josh 6:25), and the Gibeonites were spared by treaty (Josh 9). Fourth, many scholars note that ancient war reports used sweeping language ("nothing that breathes," "utterly destroyed") that was not always meant literally. Joshua reports total victory (Josh 10:40) and then lists many Canaanites still in the land (Josh 13:1–6; 15:63; Judg 1). Some also stress that the main command was to *drive out* the nations (7:1; 9:3), so that those who fled were not hunted down. Christians differ on how much weight to give each of these points. None of them makes the passage easy, and none should be used to soften what the text says.
- **"You shall not destroy its trees by wielding an axe against them" (v19).** Even in war there were limits. Armies normally cut down every tree around a besieged city for siege works, firewood and to starve the people, and Assyrian reliefs show soldiers felling orchards. Israel could eat the fruit but not destroy the trees. **"Are the trees in the field human, that they should be besieged by you?"** The Hebrew is short and difficult, literally "for the man the tree of the field." The ESV, like most modern versions, reads it as a question: the trees are not your enemy. Others read it as a statement, "for the tree of the field is man's food." Either way, the trees serve human life and should not be made to suffer for a human war.
- **"Only the trees that you know are not trees for food you may destroy" (v20).** Non-fruit trees could be used for **"siegeworks,"** ramps, towers and battering rams. The Tyndale notes add a practical point: Israel would soon live in this land and need those trees. Jewish tradition later drew a wider principle from this verse, *bal tashchit*, "do not destroy," forbidding needless waste of anything useful.
- **Commentators:** Henry presses v1 as a lesson for spiritual warfare: "Let neither the number nor the power of the enemies of our souls dismay us." On the offer of peace: "Whoever are for war, we must be for peace." And on the fruit trees: "Every creature of God is good; as nothing is to be refused, so nothing is to be abused," and, "We may live to want what we carelessly waste." Guzik points to Gideon as the model of an army trimmed to the willing, and quotes Adam Clarke's warning that God's help could not be expected in wars he had not commanded. On vv16–18 Guzik treats the destruction as a unique war of judgment, not an ordinary conquest, and also gives Clarke's view that "utterly destroy" may include driving out and subduing, pointing to Rahab and the Gibeonites. JFB calls the Canaanite campaign "a war of utter extermination," says the exemptions in vv5–8 were reasonable, and doubts the Jewish claim that the ark always went into battle: "there is no evidence of this in the sacred history." On v19 it notes customs in India where cutting down a fruit tree was considered "a sort of sacrilege."

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter's battles were real, and the New Testament does not tell the church to fight them. Jesus told Peter to put away his sword (Matt 26:52), and Paul says "we do not wrestle against flesh and blood" (Eph 6:12). But the pattern still teaches. Before every battle the priest said, "the LORD your God is he who goes with you to fight for you," and Christ is the High Priest and the "founder of their salvation" who leads his people (Heb 2:10; 4:14–16). He fought the decisive battle alone, at the cross, where he "disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame" (Col 2:15). The terms of peace offered before the siege are offered now to everyone, Canaanite and Israelite alike, as Rahab found. God's final judgment on evil is coming, and it is no less serious than the judgment on Canaan. But for now the word is the one Paul carried: "be reconciled to God" (2 Cor 5:20).
