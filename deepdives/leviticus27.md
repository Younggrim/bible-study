*Setting:* Israel at Mount Sinai, early in the second year after the exodus. Chapter 26 closed the main body of Leviticus with its blessings and curses and a summary verse (26:46). Chapter 27 is a kind of appendix. It deals with something the law did not require: voluntary vows and gifts. An Israelite might promise God a person, an animal, a house or a field, often in thanks for an answered prayer or as part of a request (Gen 28:20–22; 1 Sam 1:11). This chapter sets the price for buying such gifts back, so that vows were kept honestly and taken seriously. It ends with the tithe, which was not a gift but God's due. It can seem a flat ending to Leviticus, but its point is simple: what is promised to God must be treated as holy.

#### Laws About Vows (vv1–34)

**Vowing a person (vv1–8)**
- **"If anyone makes a special vow to the LORD involving the valuation of persons" (v2).** Someone could vow himself, or a family member, to the LORD. Since God forbade human sacrifice (18:21) and only Levites served at the sanctuary, a dedicated person was usually "redeemed" by paying a set amount of silver to the sanctuary instead. Hannah vowed her son Samuel and actually brought him to serve at the tabernacle (1 Sam 1:11, 24–28), but most such vows were paid in money. The word for "special" comes from a root meaning "wonderful" or "extraordinary," a vow beyond the ordinary.
- **The valuations (vv3–7).** A man aged 20–60 was valued at **fifty shekels**, a woman at thirty. Ages 5–20: twenty and ten. One month to five years: five and three. Over sixty: fifteen and ten. A shekel was roughly 11 grams of silver, and fifty shekels was far more than a laborer earned in a year, so this was a costly vow.
- **Why the differences?** The amounts match what a person's labor was worth in an ancient farming economy, not their worth to God. The Bible is clear that men and women alike are made in God's image (Gen 1:27). A man in his prime could do the most heavy field work. Children and older adults could do less. An older woman's valuation falls less than an older man's (from 30 to 10, while a man's falls from 50 to 15), which may reflect the work women continued to do in the home in old age. Interestingly, the five-shekel price for a child under five matches the price for redeeming Israel's firstborn sons (Num 3:47), and thirty shekels was the compensation for a slave killed by an ox (Exod 21:32).
- **"If someone is too poor… the priest shall value him… according to what the vower can afford" (v8).** No one was shut out of making a vow because they were poor. The priest would set a price they could pay, as the poor could bring birds instead of a lamb (5:7–13).
- **Commentators:** Henry says God accepted such vows when "honestly meant," and since people "are apt to repent of such vows," the law provided for redemption. JFB says the poor were never excused entirely, to discourage "the rash formation of vows." Guzik reads the valuations as reflecting age and usefulness in a farming society, and applies v8: anyone, however poor, can give their life to the LORD.

**Vowing animals and property (vv9–25)**
- **"All of it that he gives to the LORD is holy" (v9).** A clean animal, one fit for sacrifice, could not be bought back once vowed. It belonged to God.
- **"He shall not exchange it… good for bad, or bad for good" (v10).** No swapping. If someone tried to substitute another animal, *both* became holy. This blocked the temptation to promise a fine animal in a crisis and then hand over a scrawny one when the crisis passed. Malachi condemned exactly that: "Cursed be the cheat who has a male in his flock, and vows it, and yet sacrifices to the Lord what is blemished" (Mal 1:14).
- **Unclean animals (vv11–13).** A donkey or camel could not be sacrificed. The priest set its value, and the sanctuary sold it or the owner bought it back by paying the value **"and add a fifth."** The 20 percent surcharge appears throughout the chapter (vv13, 15, 19, 27, 31). It made redemption possible but not cheap, and discouraged vows made lightly.
- **Houses (vv14–15).** A house could be dedicated and redeemed on the same terms.
- **Fields (vv16–25).** A family field was valued by the amount of seed needed to sow it, **"a homer of barley seed… fifty shekels."** A homer was roughly a donkey-load. The price was for the harvests until the next Jubilee, so it went down the closer the Jubilee came (vv17–18), just as in the land sales of 25:15–16. If the owner did not redeem the field, or sold it to someone else after dedicating it, it became the priests' permanently at the Jubilee (vv20–21). A purchased field, which was really a lease, could be dedicated only until the Jubilee, when it went back to the family who owned it (vv22–24). God would not let a vow undo the Jubilee.
- **"Twenty gerahs shall make a shekel" (v25).** The "shekel of the sanctuary" was a standard weight. Merchants' shekels might vary, but God's measure did not (19:35–36).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that an animal unfit for the altar could be sold, with the money used for sacred purposes, and that the field rules guarded family inheritances. Guzik explains the one-fifth rule with the example of a donkey. The Tyndale notes say the surcharge likely aimed to reduce careless dedications.

**What could not be vowed or bought back (vv26–33)**
- **"A firstborn of animals… no man may dedicate" (v26).** The firstborn already belonged to God (Exod 13:2, 12). You cannot give God what is his already. An unclean firstborn, like a donkey, could be bought back with the fifth added, or sold (v27; compare Exod 13:13).
- **"No devoted thing… shall be sold or redeemed" (v28).** "Devoted" translates *herem*, a stronger word than "dedicated." Something under *herem* was handed over to God completely and irreversibly. It could go to the priests' use, as a field (v21), or in war it could mean total destruction, as with Jericho, where everything was "devoted to the LORD for destruction" (Josh 6:17). Achan's sin was keeping *herem* plunder for himself (Josh 7).
- **"No one devoted… from mankind, shall be ransomed; he shall surely be put to death" (v29).** This is one of the hardest verses in Leviticus. It does not give a private person the right to "devote" a human being. Most interpreters take it as referring to people placed under God's judgment, such as the Canaanite cities in the conquest (Deut 7:2; 1 Sam 15:3) or an Israelite town that turned to idols (Deut 13:12–15). JFB reads it differently, as a person devoted to the sanctuary for life. The case of Jephthah, who vowed whatever came out of his door and then faced his daughter (Judg 11:30–40), is the tragic warning of what rash vows can lead to. Whatever Jephthah actually did, which Christians still debate, God's law gave no command to offer a human sacrifice.
- **"Every tithe of the land… is the LORD's" (v30).** A tenth of the crops belonged to God. This was older than the law: Abraham gave a tenth to Melchizedek (Gen 14:20), and Jacob vowed one (Gen 28:22). The tithe supported the Levites, who had no land (Num 18:21–24). Deuteronomy adds a tithe eaten in celebration at the sanctuary and a third-year tithe for the poor (Deut 14:22–29). Scholars debate whether these were separate tithes or one tithe used in different ways.
- **"Redeem some of his tithe… add a fifth" (v31).** Crops could be paid in money, for example by a family living far from the sanctuary, but at a 20 percent premium.
- **"Every tenth animal of all that pass under the herdsman's staff" (v32).** The herdsman counted animals as they passed through a gate one by one, and every tenth was touched with the staff and marked for God. **"One shall not differentiate between good or bad"** (v33). Whatever the tenth animal was, it was God's. No picking out the weaker ones.
- **Tithing and Christians.** The New Testament does not command a tithe for the church. Jesus mentions it as part of the law the Pharisees kept while neglecting "justice and mercy and faithfulness" (Matt 23:23). Paul teaches generous, proportional, cheerful giving, "as he has decided in his heart" (2 Cor 9:7; 1 Cor 16:2). Many Christians still treat the tithe as a good guide, and others treat it as a starting point rather than a limit.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls vv28–29 the most solemn vows, made with a self-imprecation, and explains the herdsman's count: animals passed singly through a gate and every tenth was marked with ochre. Guzik notes that "devoted" goes further than "consecrated" and is the word translated "accursed" in some older versions of Joshua 6:17. The Tyndale notes say the "devoted for destruction" in v29 refers to the war of conquest.

**The end of Leviticus (v34)**
- **"These are the commandments that the LORD commanded Moses for the people of Israel on Mount Sinai" (v34).** The book opened with "The LORD called Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting" (1:1), and it closes the same way. Every word came from God. Leviticus began with sacrifices that brought people near and ends with gifts given freely. Between them it has shown a holy God making a way to live among a sinful people.
- **Commentators:** Henry says 26:46 "seemed to close up the statute-book; yet this chapter is added as an appendix." JFB calls the ceremonial law "a burdensome yoke" that was also a "schoolmaster to Christ" (Gal 3:24).

#### Pointing to Christ
Again and again this chapter asks, "What is the price to redeem it?" A person vowed to God could be bought back with silver. Jesus is the price paid for people who belonged to God by right but had been lost. Peter writes that believers were ransomed "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). The chapter also shows a vow that could not be redeemed, the *herem*, the person handed over to judgment. Jesus took that place too. He was handed over for us, so that in him we would be not devoted to destruction but redeemed. Those he has bought back are now called to give themselves to God freely, "a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable" (Rom 12:1), as the willing, wholehearted vow this chapter describes.
