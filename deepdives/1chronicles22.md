*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC, for a community that had rebuilt a modest temple on the old site (Ezra 3–6) and wondered whether it mattered. In chapter 21 David's sinful census brought a plague, which God stopped at the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite, just north of the City of David. There David built an altar, and God answered with fire from heaven. This chapter opens with David naming that spot as the place for God's house. From here to the end of 1 Chronicles (chapters 22–29), the Chronicler tells something the books of Samuel and Kings do not: how David, barred from building the temple himself, spent his last years preparing everything for it. He gathered workers and materials, gave his son Solomon a solemn charge, and organized the Levites, priests, singers, gatekeepers and officials (chapters 23–27). For the Chronicler's readers, this showed that the temple and its worship were not an afterthought. They were the great work of Israel's greatest king.

#### David Builds an Altar (v1)
- **The chapter break comes in the wrong place.** Verse 1 is the last line of the story in chapter 21, and the ESV's heading for that section runs from 21:18 to 22:1. Chapter divisions were added more than a thousand years after the text was written.
- **"Here shall be the house of the LORD God and here the altar of burnt offering for Israel" (v1).** David sees the meaning of what has happened. God chose this place by stopping the angel there, commanding an altar there and answering with fire there. Moses had told Israel that once God gave them rest, they were to worship "at the place that the LORD your God will choose, to make his name dwell there" (Deut 12:10–11). For centuries no such place had been fixed. Now it is. Solomon built the temple on this exact spot (2 Chr 3:1).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the fire showed David that "the national place of worship should be fixed there." Henry notes that the very place of God's judgment became the place of his mercy and his house.

#### David Prepares for Temple Building (vv2–5)
- **"The resident aliens who were in the land of Israel" (v2).** "Resident aliens" (*gerim*) were non-Israelites living in Israel, many of them descendants of the Canaanites who had not been driven out. The Law protected them and they could join Israel's worship (Exod 12:48–49; Lev 19:33–34), but they were also used as labor. Solomon later counted 153,600 of them and put them to work on the temple (2 Chr 2:17–18; 1 Kings 9:20–21). It is a striking detail: foreigners cut the stones for the house of Israel's God, a small sign of the day when the nations would come to worship there (Isa 56:6–7).
- **"Stonecutters to prepare dressed stones" (v2).** Dressed stones were cut and squared at the quarry. 1 Kings 6:7 adds that the stones were finished before they arrived, so "neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron was heard in the house while it was being built."
- **Iron, bronze and cedar (vv3–4).** Iron was still costly in David's day, and his control over the Philistines, who had long kept the secret of ironworking (1 Sam 13:19–22), may have made it more available. Much of the bronze came from David's wars (18:8). The cedar came from Lebanon by way of **the Sidonians and Tyrians**, the Phoenicians of the coast, master builders and seafarers. Hiram of Tyre had already supplied David's palace (14:1) and later worked closely with Solomon (2 Chr 2).
- **"Solomon my son is young and inexperienced" (v5).** The phrase (*na'ar varak*, literally "a youth and tender") is used again in 29:1. Solomon himself says, "I am but a little child" (1 Kings 3:7). He was probably about twenty when he came to the throne. David does not despise his son's youth. He works to make up for it.
- **"Exceedingly magnificent, of fame and glory throughout all lands" (v5).** David's vision for the temple was not modest. It was meant to be a testimony to the nations about the God who lived there. When the queen of Sheba saw Solomon's work, "there was no more breath in her" (2 Chr 9:4).
- **"So David provided materials in great quantity before his death" (v5).** David could not build, but he could prepare. He gave the site, the materials, the workers, the plans (28:11–19) and the organization (chapters 23–26). His son would get the credit. The building would be called Solomon's temple, and David did not mind.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "David must not build, but he would do all he could; he prepared abundantly before his death." Guzik draws the lesson that much of God's work is preparing the way for others to finish, and that it should be done gladly without credit. He also notes, following John Trapp, the Gentile Tyrians contributing to a house for Israel's God. JFB identifies the "strangers" as partly descendants of the Canaanites and "partly war captives."

#### Solomon Charged to Build the Temple (vv6–19)
- **"He called for Solomon his son and charged him" (v6).** A "charge" was a solemn commission, often given by a leader near death to the one who would carry on his work. Chronicles shows a calm handover. 1 Kings 1–2 tells a messier story, in which David's son Adonijah tried to seize the throne and Bathsheba and Nathan had to act to secure it for Solomon. The Chronicler does not deny that story. He presents the side that bears on the temple.
- **"I had it in my heart to build a house to the name of the LORD my God" (v7).** "The name" stands for God himself as he reveals himself to his people. God cannot be contained in a building ("heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you," 2 Chr 6:18), but he could put his name there, making it the place where he would meet them and hear their prayers.
- **"You have shed much blood and have waged great wars" (v8).** Nathan's message in chapter 17 did not give this reason. Here David reveals it, and he repeats it in 28:3. What does it mean? David's wars were not sinful in themselves. God gave him victory "wherever he went" (18:6, 13). Some, like the Tyndale notes, think the bloodshed made him unfit to build a holy place, much as contact with death made a person ritually unclean (Num 19:11–16). Others see the point as symbolic: the temple was a picture of God's rest and peace, so it was fitting that a man of peace should build it. 1 Kings 5:3 gives a more practical angle: David was too busy fighting wars to build. These views can stand together. The verdict is not that David was wicked but that the house of God belonged to a different kind of reign.
- **"A son shall be born to you who shall be a man of rest" (v9).** "Rest" (*menuchah*) is a key word in Israel's story. God promised rest in the land (Deut 12:9–10), Joshua received it partially (Josh 21:44), and the psalmist pictures the temple as God's own "resting place" (Ps 132:8, 14). "For his name shall be Solomon" (*Shelomoh*), which sounds like *shalom*, "peace." "Peace and quiet" in the same verse is *shalom* and *sheqet*, "peace and calm." The temple would be built in a time of peace, by a king named for it.
- **"He shall be my son, and I will be his father" (v10).** David repeats God's promise from 17:12–13, now applied directly to Solomon by name. The Chronicler presents Solomon as the son chosen and promised from the start (28:5–6).
- **"The LORD be with you, so that you may succeed" (v11).** David's blessing has a condition attached in vv12–13. Success will come from the LORD's presence and Solomon's obedience together.
- **"May the LORD grant you discretion and understanding" (v12).** "Discretion" (*sekel*) and "understanding" (*binah*) are words of practical wisdom. David prays for the very thing Solomon will ask God for at Gibeon (2 Chr 1:10), and God will give it.
- **"Then you will prosper if you are careful to observe the statutes and the rules that the LORD commanded Moses" (v13).** Building the temple would not replace obeying God. A king who built God a house but ignored his law would have missed the point. Solomon's later life, when his heart was turned toward other gods (1 Kings 11:1–8), shows how serious this warning was.
- **"Be strong and courageous. Fear not; do not be dismayed" (v13).** These are almost exactly the words Moses spoke to Joshua and God repeated to him (Deut 31:7–8; Josh 1:6–9). The pattern is deliberate. Moses led Israel to the edge of the land but could not enter, and Joshua finished the task. David prepared everything for the temple but could not build it, and Solomon finished the task. The Chronicler sees David's handover to Solomon as a second Moses-to-Joshua moment.
- **"With great pains I have provided" (v14).** Literally "in my affliction" or "in my hardship" (KJV "in my trouble"). The word can mean poverty or suffering. David may be saying that he gathered all this through a hard and troubled life, or that what he gave, though vast, was humble in comparison with God's worth.
- **"100,000 talents of gold, a million talents of silver" (v14).** These figures are enormous. A talent was about 75 pounds (34 kg), so this would be roughly 3,750 tons of gold and 37,500 tons of silver. By comparison, Solomon's yearly income in gold was 666 talents (1 Kings 10:14), and David's personal gift in 29:4 is 3,000 talents of gold. Some interpreters accept the numbers as stated. Others think they are round, sweeping figures meaning "an immense amount," a common way of speaking in ancient writing. Others think they have grown in copying. All agree on the point: David gave lavishly.
- **"To these you must add" (v14).** David did not hand over the work so complete that Solomon had nothing to do. Each generation must give its own part.
- **"Arise and work! The LORD be with you!" (v16).** Preparation is over. The call is to begin.
- **"David also commanded all the leaders of Israel to help Solomon his son" (v17).** No one builds God's house alone. The whole leadership is called in.
- **"Has he not given you peace on every side?" (v18).** Literally "rest all around" (from *nuach*, the same root as "man of rest" in v9). The condition Moses set in Deuteronomy 12:10 has been met. "The land is subdued before the LORD and his people" echoes Joshua 18:1, when the land was subdued and the tabernacle was set up at Shiloh.
- **"Now set your mind and heart to seek the LORD your God" (v19).** "Seek" is one of the Chronicler's favorite words. It means turning to God in worship, obedience and dependence. Building comes after seeking, and grows from it: "Arise and build the sanctuary… so that the ark… and the holy vessels of God may be brought into a house built for the name of the LORD." That is exactly what happens at the temple's dedication (2 Chr 5:2–14).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Where God gives rest, he expects work," and Solomon "must not think, by building the temple, to purchase a dispensation to sin." On vv17–19 he reminds workers that "the work is in the hands of the Prince of peace," so "let us not then be weary of well doing." Guzik says David's wars were not wrong in themselves, but God wanted his house to rise out of rest and peace rather than struggle. He notes that commentators split over whether the 100,000 talents are exact or a copyist's error, and reads "to these you must add" as a gentle hint that Solomon must give his own part. Following Martin Selman, he takes "seek the LORD" here as active obedience, not quiet devotion only. JFB comments only on vv1, 2 and 6, and passes over David's speech without notes, beyond observing its "earnestness and solemnity."

#### Pointing to Christ
God would not let the man of war build his house. He gave the task to a son, a "man of rest," whose name meant peace, to whom God promised, "He shall be my son, and I will be his father." That promise reached past Solomon to Jesus, the true Son and the Prince of Peace (Isa 9:6). Solomon's peace lasted forty years and his temple four hundred. Christ made peace "by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20) and builds a house that will never fall, made of living stones (1 Pet 2:5; Eph 2:19–22). He also gives the rest David's wars could never give: "Come to me… and I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28; Heb 4:8–10). Like David, every generation prepares and hands on, and like Solomon, each must add its part. But the house is Christ's, and he says, "I will build my church" (Matt 16:18).
