*Setting:* Joshua's last public act. In chapter 23 he warned Israel's leaders. Now he gathers "all the tribes of Israel" to Shechem, in the pass between Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim in the hill country of Ephraim, about 30 miles north of Jerusalem. Shechem was full of memory. Abraham built his first altar in Canaan there when God promised him the land (Gen 12:6–7). Jacob bought land and built an altar there (Gen 33:18–20), and later buried his household's idols under a tree near the town (Gen 35:2–4). Joshua himself had read the law there with blessings and curses after Ai fell (8:30–35). Here Israel renews its covenant with the LORD, and the book closes with three burials.

#### The Covenant Renewal at Shechem (vv1–28)
- **"They presented themselves before God" (v1).** The phrase means a formal religious assembly, not just a meeting. The ark may have been brought from Shiloh for the occasion, or the phrase may simply mean they stood before God at a holy place (v26 mentions "the sanctuary of the LORD").
- **The shape of the covenant.** The Tyndale notes point out that this chapter follows the pattern of ancient treaties between a great king and a lesser people, known especially from Hittite treaties of the 14th–13th centuries BC. The king identifies himself (v2), recounts his past kindness (vv2–13), states what the people must do (vv14–15), warns of curses and blessings (vv19–20), names witnesses (vv22, 27), and has the agreement written down (v26). Deuteronomy follows the same pattern. Israel's God presents himself as a King who has already done everything for his people before asking anything of them.
- **"Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel" (v2).** Joshua speaks as a prophet. For eleven verses God speaks in the first person: "I took… I gave… I sent… I brought… I destroyed… I delivered." Israel's story is told as God's actions.
- **"Your fathers lived beyond the Euphrates… and they served other gods" (v2).** A frank beginning. Abraham's family came from Ur and Haran in Mesopotamia (Gen 11:31), where the moon god was worshiped in both cities. The Bible never pretends Israel began with a godly pedigree. God chose an idol-worshiping family by grace. Later Jewish tradition (Jubilees, and the rabbinic story of Abraham smashing his father's idols) filled in the story of how Abraham broke away. The Bible itself says only that God "took" him.
- **"I gave Esau the hill country of Seir" (v4).** Even Esau's land was God's gift (Deut 2:5). JFB says this was "in order that he might be no obstacle to Jacob." And "Jacob and his children went down to Egypt" passes over 430 years in a single clause.
- **"He put darkness between you and the Egyptians" (v7).** The cloud of Exodus 14:19–20, which was darkness to Egypt and light to Israel. "Your eyes saw what I did in Egypt." The older men in this crowd had been children or teenagers at the Red Sea.
- **Balak and Balaam (vv9–10).** The king of Moab hired the prophet Balaam to curse Israel, and God turned every curse into a blessing (Num 22–24; Deut 23:5). "I would not listen to Balaam" is a striking detail: Balaam wanted to curse, and God refused.
- **"I sent the hornet before you" (v12).** The Hebrew *tsir'ah* means hornets or wasps and appears only here, in Exodus 23:28 and in Deuteronomy 7:20. Readers take it three ways. Some take it literally, as swarms of stinging insects. JFB thinks of "a particular species of wasp which swarms in warm countries." Many take it as a vivid picture of the panic God sent ahead of Israel, since Exodus 23:27 says "I will send my terror before you." A few have suggested it stands for Egyptian military campaigns that weakened Canaan, since the bee was a royal symbol in Egypt, but that remains a guess. Whatever it was, the point is plain: "it was not by your sword or by your bow." The "two kings of the Amorites" are Sihon and Og, east of the Jordan (Num 21:21–35).
- **"A land on which you had not labored" (v13).** Almost word for word from Deuteronomy 6:10–11, where Moses warned that houses, wells and vineyards they had not made could lead them to forget the LORD. Israel lives on grace it did not earn.
- **"Fear the LORD and serve him in sincerity and in faithfulness" (v14).** The verb "serve" (*'abad*) appears more than a dozen times in vv14–24. The whole covenant comes down to one question: whom will Israel serve? "Sincerity" is *tamim*, wholeness or integrity, the word used of a sacrifice "without blemish." "Faithfulness" is *'emet*, truth or reliability.
- **"Put away the gods that your fathers served" (v14).** Idols were still among the people, from Mesopotamia and from Egypt (Ezek 20:7–8; Amos 5:25–26). Jacob gave the same command at this same place: "Put away the foreign gods that are among you" (Gen 35:2–4).
- **"Choose this day whom you will serve" (v15).** Joshua is not offering a choice between serving God or serving nobody. Everyone serves something. The real options are the old gods of Mesopotamia, the local gods of the Amorites, or the LORD. And Joshua does not wait to see what the crowd decides: "But as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD." He was perhaps 110 years old, still speaking for his whole household.
- **"Far be it from us that we should forsake the LORD" (vv16–18).** The people answer well, and they give the right reason. They repeat God's acts: he brought us out, did signs, preserved us, drove out the peoples. "Therefore we also will serve the LORD, for he is our God."
- **"You are not able to serve the LORD" (v19).** A shocking reply. Joshua is not discouraging faith. He is testing a quick, easy promise. God "is a holy God," set apart and perfectly pure, and "a jealous God," who will not share his people's devotion with other gods (Exod 20:5; 34:14). "He will not forgive your transgressions" does not deny God's mercy, which the law provides for repeatedly. It warns that deliberate rebellion against the covenant, turning to other gods, will not simply be overlooked (v20). Israel's later history proved Joshua right.
- **"No, but we will serve the LORD" (v21).** They insist, and Joshua makes it formal: "You are witnesses against yourselves" (v22). A covenant needed witnesses (Deut 19:15), and the people themselves are the first.
- **"Put away the foreign gods… and incline your heart to the LORD" (v23).** Commitment has to be acted on. Turning away from idols and turning the heart toward God are two sides of one act.
- **"Joshua made a covenant with the people that day" (v25).** Literally he "cut" a covenant, the usual Hebrew idiom, probably from the practice of cutting animals in two when a covenant was made (Gen 15:9–18; Jer 34:18).
- **"Joshua wrote these words in the Book of the Law of God" (v26).** The record of the covenant was added to the scroll of Moses' law. This is one of the few places where Scripture describes Scripture being written and added to.
- **"A large stone… under the terebinth that was by the sanctuary of the LORD" (vv26–27).** Standing stones marked agreements in the ancient world (Gen 31:44–52, where Jacob and Laban set one up). The terebinth may be the same great tree at Shechem where Abraham camped and Jacob buried the idols (Gen 12:6; 35:4). In Judges 9:6, Abimelech is made king "by the oak of the pillar at Shechem," probably the same tree and this same stone. "It has heard all the words." Joshua speaks of the stone as a silent witness, as Moses called heaven and earth to witness (Deut 30:19). Archaeologists found a large standing stone at the temple area of ancient Shechem (Tell Balata), though no one can say whether it is this one.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says Joshua wanted Israel to make religion "their deliberate choice" and then "their determinate choice," freely chosen and firmly kept. On v15 he writes, "Those that resolve to serve God must not mind being singular in it," and "It is no abridgment of our liberty to bind ourselves with a bond to God." Guzik reads "you are not able" as Joshua refusing to let the people make a shallow promise, much as Jesus told would-be followers to count the cost (Luke 14:28–33). He notes that Joshua's own vow rested on a lifetime of choosing God: fighting Amalek, standing apart from the golden calf, and trusting God when ten of the spies did not. He sees three sets of gods tied to three bodies of water: the gods of ancestry beyond the Euphrates, the gods of upbringing in Egypt across the Red Sea, and the gods of the surrounding culture across the Jordan. JFB stresses that Israel owed its existence "not to any merits of their own, but to the free grace of God," and notes it was "the usage of ancient times to erect stone pillars."

#### Joshua's Death and Burial (vv29–33)
- **"Joshua the son of Nun, the servant of the LORD, died" (v29).** This is the first time the book calls Joshua "the servant of the LORD." The title belonged to Moses throughout (1:1–2, 13, 15). Joshua began as "Moses' assistant" (1:1) and ends with Moses' title. He died at 110, the same age as his ancestor Joseph (Gen 50:26), an age Egyptians counted as the ideal life span.
- **"In his own inheritance at Timnath-serah" (v30).** The town Joshua asked for last of all, after every tribe had its share (19:49–50). Judges 2:9 spells it Timnath-heres, "portion of the sun," with two letters reversed. Either spelling may be the original.
- **"Israel served the LORD all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua" (v31).** A good report, but with a limit built in. Faithfulness lasted as long as people who "had known all the work that the LORD did" were alive. Judges 2:7–10 repeats this verse and then adds: "there arose another generation after them who did not know the LORD."
- **"The bones of Joseph… they buried them at Shechem" (v32).** Joseph, dying in Egypt, made his family swear to carry his bones to the land God promised (Gen 50:24–25). Moses took them out of Egypt (Exod 13:19), and they were carried through forty years of wilderness. Hebrews 11:22 counts this as an act of faith. They were buried in the field Jacob bought at Shechem "for a hundred pieces of money" (Gen 33:19). Jesus later sat by Jacob's well near "the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph" (John 4:5–6).
- **"Eleazar the son of Aaron died" (v33).** The high priest who served beside Joshua in dividing the land (14:1; 19:51) is buried at Gibeah, a hill in Ephraim belonging to his son Phinehas, who carried on as high priest (Judg 20:28).
- **A longer ending in the Greek.** The Greek Septuagint adds lines to this chapter. It says the flint knives used to circumcise Israel at Gilgal (5:2–3) were buried with Joshua, and after Eleazar's death it tells how the people turned to idols and were handed over to Eglon of Moab, a bridge into Judges 3. The Hebrew text, which the ESV follows, ends simply with the three graves.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "This book, which began with triumphs, here ends with funerals, by which all the glory of man is stained." He sets "the servant of the Lord" beside Jesus' "Well done, good and faithful servant," and says that while Israel's faith faded after Joshua's generation, Christ, "our Joshua," still lives and stays with his church by his Spirit. Guzik points out that Joshua is buried without great ceremony, remembered chiefly as God's servant, and that the faithfulness of the people after him was his best memorial. He also sees Joseph's bones as a sign that God finishes what he starts. JFB notes that Joseph's bones were laid "in a parcel of ground which Jacob bought" and that tradition places Eleazar's tomb at the village of Awertah.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joshua could lead Israel into the land, write the covenant and call the people to choose, but he could not make them faithful, and his name lived on only in a grave at Timnath-serah. Jesus bears the same name, "the LORD saves," and he does what Joshua could not. Joshua warned, "You are not able to serve the LORD." The gospel agrees, and then answers: Christ was the faithful servant in Israel's place, and by his blood he made a new covenant (Luke 22:20) in which God himself gives his people a new heart (Ezek 36:26–27). Joseph's bones waited for a resting place in the promised land. The tomb of the true Joshua is empty, and "because I live, you also will live" (John 14:19).
