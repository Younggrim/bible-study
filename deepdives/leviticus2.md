*Setting:* God is still speaking to Moses from the newly finished tabernacle at Mount Sinai, laying out the offerings Israel will bring (chs 1–7). Chapter 1 covered the burnt offering, an animal wholly burned. Chapter 2 turns to the one offering in the list with no animal and no blood: the grain offering, a gift of flour, oil and incense from the work of a farmer's hands. It usually went alongside a burnt offering or peace offering (Num 15:1–10; 28:3–6), so it rarely stood alone.

#### Laws for Grain Offerings (vv1–16)
- **"When anyone brings a grain offering" (v1).** "Anyone" here is *nephesh*, often translated "soul" or "person," a different word from the *'adam* of 1:2. "Grain offering" is *minhah*. BDB gives its basic meanings as "gift" and "tribute." Jacob sent a *minhah* to calm Esau (Gen 32:13, 20), and subject kings paid *minhah* to their overlord (Judg 3:15). It was a present from a servant to his king. Cain's and Abel's offerings are both called *minhah* (Gen 4:3–4). The KJV's "meat offering" is old English for "food" and does not mean flesh.
- **"Fine flour… oil… frankincense" (v1).** Fine flour was choice wheat, finer than the barley flour poorer people ate. Olive oil was a staple of cooking, lamps and medicine, and a sign of the land's richness (Deut 8:8). Frankincense was an expensive resin brought by caravan from southern Arabia, also used in the holy incense (Exod 30:34).
- **"A handful… as its memorial portion" (v2).** "Memorial portion" is *'azkarah*, from *zakar*, "to remember." BDB lists it only in priestly texts, seven times in all. The handful, with **all of its frankincense**, went up in smoke as a token of the whole. The idea may be that it calls the worshiper to God's remembrance, or that it is the part that stands for all of it. The Greek Old Testament renders it with *mnēmosynon*, the word the angel uses to Cornelius: "your prayers and your alms have ascended as a memorial before God" (Acts 10:4).
- **"The rest… shall be for Aaron and his sons; it is a most holy part" (v3).** Priests owned no farmland (Num 18:20), so most of the grain offering became their food. "Most holy" (literally "holy of holies") meant they had to eat it in the sanctuary courtyard (6:16). Giving to God fed the people who served him.
- **Oven, griddle and pan (vv4–10).** The offering could come raw or cooked three ways: baked in a clay oven as loaves or thin wafers, cooked flat on a **griddle** and broken in pieces, or cooked in a covered **pan**, which some scholars think made something like a fried cake. This was home cooking. Rich or poor, each family could bring bread from its own kitchen.
- **"No grain offering… shall be made with leaven… nor any honey" (v11).** Leaven was sourdough starter, a lump of old fermented dough worked into new dough. Israel left Egypt with unleavened bread (Exod 12:34, 39), so leaven-free bread already carried a memory of the exodus. Later, Jesus and Paul use leaven as a picture of sin spreading through the whole (Matt 16:6; 1 Cor 5:6–8), but the law itself gives no reason. "Honey" (*debash*) can mean bees' honey or a thick syrup from dates or grapes, which ferments. Some think it was excluded because it ferments too. Others note that honey featured in pagan offerings. No one knows for sure.
- **"As an offering of firstfruits you may bring them" (v12).** Leaven and honey were not evil in themselves. Leavened loaves were brought at the Feast of Weeks (23:17) and with the thank offering (7:13). They just could not be burned on the altar.
- **"The salt of the covenant with your God" (v13).** Salt preserves food, so it was a natural sign of something lasting. Sharing salt at a meal sealed friendship and loyalty in the ancient Near East. God's covenant with the priests (Num 18:19) and with David (2 Chr 13:5) is called a "covenant of salt." Every offering was to be salted, a reminder that it was brought within a covenant that God would not let spoil. Jesus says, "Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another" (Mark 9:49–50).
- **"A grain offering of firstfruits… fresh ears, roasted with fire, crushed new grain" (v14).** At the start of harvest, a farmer could bring the first green ears, roasted and crushed. Giving God the first part before knowing how the rest of the harvest would turn out was an act of trust: "Honor the LORD with your wealth and with the firstfruits of all your produce" (Prov 3:9). The Tyndale notes add that this confessed that the land itself was God's and Israel only his tenants (25:23).
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the grain offering mainly expresses thanks for God's providence, and he notes that it follows the burnt offering because no service is accepted "without an interest in the sacrifice of Christ." He reads leaven as pride, malice and hypocrisy, honey as sensual pleasure, and says, "All religious services must be seasoned with grace." JFB calls oil a symbol of the Spirit and salt "an emblem of incorruption and purity." It says leaven was "regarded as the emblem of hypocrisy or corruption" and also mentions the pagan-rites view. Guzik likewise links leaven to sin and honey to pagan worship, and he makes a neat application: come to God as you are, without souring or sweetening yourself to impress him. He passes on Spurgeon's comment that the covenant of salt is one that cannot decay. These are fair applications, though the chapter itself does not say what leaven or honey symbolize.

#### Pointing to Christ
The grain offering was a gift of daily bread to God, mostly given back to feed those who served at his altar. Jesus called himself "the bread of life" (John 6:35), and he was without the leaven Paul calls "malice and evil" (1 Cor 5:8). Some see the fine flour, oil and frankincense as pictures of his even life, his anointing with the Spirit and the fragrance of his obedience, which is a traditional reading and not one the New Testament spells out. What the New Testament does say plainly is that every gift believers bring to God, including "the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Heb 13:15), is acceptable through him, offered within a covenant that cannot spoil.
