*Setting:* Abimelech's three-year grab for a throne has ended with a millstone and a burned city (ch 9). Judges now changes pace. Two brief reports of quiet leaders, Tola and Jair, cover forty-five years in five verses. Then the cycle of sin, oppression and crying out begins again, worse than before. Israel worships more gods than ever, two enemies press in at once (the Philistines from the west and the Ammonites from the east), and for the first time God answers Israel's cry with a refusal. The chapter ends with Israel gathered and ready to fight, but with no one to lead, which sets the stage for Jephthah (ch 11).

#### Tola and Jair (vv1–5)
- **The "minor judges."** Scholars call Tola, Jair, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon (10:1–5; 12:8–15) the "minor judges," not because they mattered less but because their records are short. Each entry gives a name, a home, years of service and a burial place, sometimes with a detail about family. No enemies, no battles. The Tyndale notes suggest their work was mainly governing and settling disputes, the everyday side of "judging."
- **"After Abimelech there arose to save Israel Tola" (v1).** Tola is the only minor judge said to "save" Israel. There is no foreign enemy here, so what he saved Israel from was probably the chaos and civil war Abimelech left behind. His name means "worm" or "crimson," from the scarlet dye made from a worm (the same word in Ps 22:6, "I am a worm"). Tola and Puah were old clan names in Issachar (Gen 46:13).
- **"Son of Dodo" (v1).** *Dodo* can be a name or the Hebrew for "his uncle." The Greek translation took it as "his father's brother's son," which would make Tola a cousin of Abimelech, and JFB thinks Tola was related to Gideon's family. Most modern translations, like the ESV, take Dodo as a name. The text does not make the family link clear.
- **"He lived at Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim" (v1).** A man of Issachar, a tribe in the north, living in Ephraim's hills. JFB suggests he chose it as a central place to govern from. Its exact location is unknown.
- **"He judged Israel twenty-three years" (v2).** Two decades of quiet faithful service sum up in one verse. Matthew Henry's comment fits: "Quiet and peaceable reigns, though the best to live in, yield least variety of matter to be spoken of."
- **Jair the Gileadite (vv3–4).** Gilead is the hill country east of the Jordan. An earlier Jair from Manasseh had conquered towns there in Moses' day and called them "Havvoth-jair," the "tent villages of Jair" (Num 32:41; Deut 3:14). This later Jair was probably his descendant and held the same towns.
- **"Thirty sons who rode on thirty donkeys… thirty cities" (v4).** Riding donkeys was a mark of rank and wealth in this period (5:10; 12:14). The Hebrew has a wordplay: the word for "donkeys" (*'ayarim*) and the word for "cities" (*'ayarim*, an unusual form) sound the same. Thirty sons on thirty donkeys over thirty towns. Many sons often meant several wives, and Jair seems to have lived like a minor king, much as Gideon did, though JFB cautions that thirty sons do not prove more than one wife.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls these judges "humble, active, and useful men, rulers appointed of God." Guzik notes how little is said about Tola despite his long service, and reads Jair's thirty sons and towns as signs of wealth and prestige, a man who never took the title of king but lived like one. JFB says Tola's name means "to save." The lexicon does not support that. *Tola* means "worm" or the crimson dye made from one. JFB may have been thinking of the verb in the verse ("arose to save").

#### Further Disobedience and Oppression (vv6–18)
- **"Again did what was evil" (v6).** This is the familiar refrain of Judges (2:11; 3:7, 12; 4:1; 6:1; 13:1). This time the list of gods is the longest in the book: the Baals and Ashtaroth (male and female Canaanite gods of storm, fertility and war), plus the gods of Syria, Sidon, Moab, Ammon and the Philistines. Seven sets of gods. Israel worshiped the gods of every nation around it, including the nations about to oppress it. "And they forsook the LORD and did not serve him." It was not the LORD plus others any more. It was everything but the LORD.
- **"He sold them into the hand of the Philistines and into the hand of the Ammonites" (v7).** God hands them over to two of the very peoples whose gods they chose. The book follows both threads: the Ammonites in Jephthah's story (chs 11–12) and the Philistines in Samson's (chs 13–16). The Philistines will remain Israel's great enemy through the days of Samuel, Saul and David.
- **The Ammonites (vv8–9).** They were descendants of Lot (Gen 19:38), living east of Gilead, with their capital at Rabbah (modern Amman, Jordan). For eighteen years they "crushed and oppressed" the Israelite tribes east of the Jordan. The two verbs are strong, the first meaning to shatter. Then they crossed the river and attacked Judah, Benjamin and Ephraim too. "Israel was severely distressed."
- **"We have sinned against you, because we have forsaken our God and have served the Baals" (v10).** This is the first time in Judges that Israel's cry includes a confession of sin. Earlier they simply "cried out" (3:9; 6:6).
- **"Did I not save you…?" (vv11–12).** God lists seven enemies he had rescued them from: Egyptians, Amorites, Ammonites, Philistines, Sidonians, Amalekites and Maonites. Seven deliverances to match the seven sets of gods in v6. Some of these rescues are recorded (Egypt in Exodus, the Amorites in Num 21, Ammon with Moab in Judg 3:13, the Philistines under Shamgar in 3:31). Others are not. The Greek translation reads "Midianites" for "Maonites," which would fit Gideon's story; the Maonites may have been a desert people from Ma'on in the south (2 Chr 26:7).
- **"I will save you no more. Go and cry out to the gods whom you have chosen" (vv13–14).** This is the shock of the chapter. The cycle of Judges 2:16–18 has always moved from cry to rescue. Now God stops it. His answer is bitterly fair: if these gods are worth serving, let them save. Moses had warned of this (Deut 32:37–38: "Where are their gods…? Let them rise up and help you"), and Jeremiah later repeats it (Jer 2:28). The point is not that God has run out of mercy but that easy, repeated "sorry" without real turning will not do.
- **"We have sinned; do to us whatever seems good to you. Only please deliver us this day" (v15).** Israel accepts God's right to punish and throws itself on his mercy. And then comes action: "they put away the foreign gods from among them and served the LORD" (v16). For the first time in Judges, the people actually remove their idols, as Jacob's household (Gen 35:2–4) and Joshua's generation (Josh 24:23) were called to do.
- **"He became impatient over the misery of Israel" (v16).** Literally, "his soul became short" with Israel's misery. The same idiom is used when Samson is worn down by Delilah's nagging (16:16, "his soul was vexed to death"). God could not bear their suffering any longer. This is one of the most tender lines in Judges. God does not respond to a bargain. He is moved by their pain. Compare Isaiah 63:9: "In all their affliction he was afflicted."
- **Mizpah (v17).** The Ammonites muster in Gilead, and Israel gathers at Mizpah ("watchtower"). Several places had this name. This one is east of the Jordan in Gilead, probably where Jacob and Laban set up their witness heap (Gen 31:49).
- **"Who is the man who will begin to fight against the Ammonites?" (v18).** The leaders ask each other, not God. At the start of Judges, Israel asked the LORD, "Who shall go up first?" (1:1). Now they offer a reward instead: whoever leads will be "head over all the inhabitants of Gilead." They have repented of their idols, but they still reach for a human solution, and they will find one in an outcast (11:1–3).
- **Commentators:** Henry says of the oppression, "By their evil ways and their evil doings they procured this to themselves," and of the repentance, "True repentance is not only for sin, but from sin." He ends on hope: "From him mercy never can be sought in vain." JFB calls confession "the first step of repentance" and says real sorrow mourns the offense against God, not only its consequences. JFB thinks the apostasy of v6 "seems to have exceeded every former one." Guzik compares God's refusal to letting a person get sick of their sin so they will truly let go of it, and suggests God saw that their first words were not yet wholehearted. He adds that people who stop believing in God do not believe in nothing but in anything. The Tyndale notes highlight that the leaders of Gilead did not ask the LORD.

#### Pointing to Christ
"I will save you no more" sounds final, and Israel deserved it. Yet when they turned, God's "soul became short" over their misery and he could not hold back. The God of Judges is not a machine that dispenses rescue on demand, but neither is he cold. He feels his people's pain. That compassion took flesh in Jesus, who "had compassion" on the crowds "because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matt 9:36), and who wept over Jerusalem. And where Gilead had to go looking for "the man who will begin to fight," God himself has provided the Man, the one mediator between God and men (1 Tim 2:5), who fought the decisive battle and is head over all his people.
