*Setting:* Galilee, then the Gentile regions to the north and east, probably spring or summer of AD 29. Jesus has just fed five thousand people and walked on the sea, and crowds across the region are bringing the sick to touch his garment (6:30–56). Religious leaders from Jerusalem are still watching him closely (3:22). Chapter 7 turns on the question of what makes a person "clean" or "unclean" before God. Jesus first takes apart the Pharisees' rules about washing and food, then goes into Gentile territory, the lands Jews regarded as unclean, where he helps a Gentile mother and heals a deaf man. What he teaches in the first half, he acts out in the second.

#### Traditions and Commandments (vv1–13)
- **"The Pharisees… with some of the scribes who had come from Jerusalem" (v1).** A second delegation from the capital (3:22). They have come to inspect.
- **"Some of his disciples ate with hands that were defiled, that is, unwashed" (v2).** "Defiled" is *koinos*, "common," meaning ritually unclean, not dirty. The issue is not hygiene but religious purity.
- **Mark's explanation (vv3–4).** Mark pauses to explain Jewish customs for his Gentile readers in Rome. The law of Moses required priests to wash before serving (Exod 30:17–21), but it never required ordinary people to wash their hands before meals. The Pharisees extended priestly purity to everyday life, so that every home table would be like the temple's. "Properly" translates an obscure word, *pygmē*, literally "with a fist," which may describe a set way of washing: cupping one hand, or rubbing the fist into the palm, or washing up to the wrist. After going to the market, where they might have brushed against unclean people or things, they washed more thoroughly. The ESV notes that the word for this washing is literally "baptize," and that some manuscripts omit "and dining couches."
- **"The tradition of the elders" (v3).** A large body of oral rules handed down by generations of teachers to apply the law to everyday life. Many Pharisees believed this oral law also went back to Moses at Sinai. It was later written down around AD 200 in the Mishnah, which has a whole section on hand-washing. To the Pharisees these rules were a fence around the law, to keep people from breaking it. Jesus will say the fence had taken the place of the field.
- **"Why do your disciples not walk according to the tradition of the elders?" (v5).** "Walk" is the Jewish way of speaking about how one lives (the rabbis called their legal rulings *halakah*, "walking"). A teacher was held responsible for his disciples (2:18, 24).
- **"Well did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites" (v6).** Jesus quotes Isaiah 29:13, following mostly the Greek version: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me." "Hypocrite" (*hypokritēs*) was the Greek word for a stage actor, someone playing a part behind a mask. Their worship is "in vain" because it is "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (v7).
- **"You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men" (v8).** Jesus does not condemn all tradition; Paul tells believers to keep the traditions he handed on (2 Thess 2:15). He condemns treating human rules as if they were God's, and then letting them push God's word aside.
- **"You have a fine way of rejecting the commandment of God" (v9).** Bitter irony. "Fine" (*kalōs*) is the same word as "well" in v6.
- **"Honor your father and your mother" (v10).** The fifth commandment (Exod 20:12), with its strong backing in Exodus 21:17. Honoring parents in Jewish understanding included caring for them in old age.
- **"Corban (that is, given to God)" (v11).** *Qorban* is the Hebrew word for an offering brought near to God (Lev 1:2). By declaring property or money "Corban," a person dedicated it to God, at least on paper. It could then not be used for others, including parents. Some teachers held that such a vow could not be undone even if made in anger. So a son could say to his aging parents, "What you would have gotten from me is Corban," and he would keep the use of it himself while his parents went without. A religious vow became a way to dodge a plain command of God. Later rabbis themselves came to allow such vows to be released for the sake of parents, which shows the abuse was real.
- **"Thus making void the word of God by your tradition" (v13).** "Making void" means to cancel, to annul. **"And many such things you do."** Corban is one example among many.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls hand-washing "a cleanly custom, and no harm in it," but says the Pharisees "placed religion in it, and would not leave it indifferent," enforcing it as a duty. He reads Isaiah this way: "what he said of the hypocrites of his own day, was applicable to the scribes and Pharisees." Guzik stresses that the washing was ceremonial, not hygienic, explains that "hypocrite" meant a masked actor, and warns that religious reasoning can make a human tradition seem as binding as God's Word. JFB gives no comment on vv1–23 and refers them to its notes on Matthew 15.

#### What Defiles a Person (vv14–23)
- **"Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v14).** Jesus turns from the experts to the crowd. This teaching is for everyone.
- **"There is nothing outside a person that by going into him can defile him" (v15).** A sweeping statement. It cuts beneath the Pharisees' hand-washing to the food laws themselves. The ESV notes that some manuscripts add v16, "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear," which the oldest manuscripts lack.
- **"His disciples asked him about the parable" (v17).** Again they need it explained (4:10). **"Then are you also without understanding?" (v18).** After the loaves and the sea (6:52), they still struggle.
- **"It enters not his heart but his stomach, and is expelled" (v19).** Jesus speaks plainly about digestion. Food passes through the body. It cannot reach the heart, which in Scripture is the center of a person's thinking, choosing and loving.
- **"(Thus he declared all foods clean.)" (v19).** This is Mark's own comment, and it is one of the most important in his Gospel. The KJV reads it differently, "purging all meats," as though it described the body's process of digestion. Most modern translations follow the reading that makes it Mark's note on what Jesus meant. The food laws of Leviticus 11 had a real purpose: they set Israel apart from the nations as a holy people. Jesus, who came to fulfill the law (Matt 5:17), now announces that the time of those boundaries is ending. The early church took years to grasp this. Peter needed a vision three times, "What God has made clean, do not call common" (Acts 10:15), and Paul wrote, "I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself" (Rom 14:14). Mark's readers in Rome, many of them Gentiles, needed to hear it.
- **"What comes out of a person is what defiles him" (v20).** The real problem is not out there but in here.
- **"From within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts" (v21).** Evil thoughts come first; the rest grows from them. Then twelve more. The first six are plural in Greek, pointing to repeated acts: sexual immorality (*porneiai*, any sexual sin), thefts, murders, adulteries, covetings (greedy wanting more), wickednesses. The last six are singular, pointing to attitudes: deceit, sensuality (open, shameless indulgence), envy (literally "an evil eye," a stingy, jealous look), slander (the word is *blasphēmia*, which covers speech against God or people), pride and foolishness (moral senselessness, not lack of intelligence).
- **"All these evil things come from within" (v23).** Jeremiah said it long before: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick" (Jer 17:9). This is why outward rituals cannot make anyone clean. What is needed is a new heart (Ezek 36:26). The list is not about "those people." It describes every human heart.
- **Commentators:** Henry says sin defiles "by the breaking out of the corruption that is in our hearts," and that "As a corrupt fountain sends forth corrupt streams, so doth a corrupt heart send forth corrupt reasonings." He follows the older reading of v19, explaining that food "goes into the stomach, and passes the several digestions and secretions that nature has appointed." Guzik reads Mark's comment as Jesus declaring foods clean, ties it to Peter's vision in Acts 10:15, and quotes Spurgeon that sin is an inner filth, not mud on the surface. The Tyndale notes count thirteen defilements in vv21–22 and note that Peter and Paul brought out the meaning of Jesus' words for the church.

#### The Syrophoenician Woman's Faith (vv24–30)
- **"He arose and went away to the region of Tyre and Sidon" (v24).** Tyre was a wealthy port city on the Mediterranean coast, in modern Lebanon, about thirty-five miles northwest of Capernaum. Sidon lay another twenty-five miles north. It was Gentile land, with an old history of tension with Israel; Jezebel came from Sidon (1 Kgs 16:31), and the prophets spoke against Tyre's pride (Ezek 26–28). Yet people from this region had already come to hear Jesus (3:8). Right after teaching that no food is unclean, Jesus walks into a land that Jews considered unclean.
- **"He entered a house and did not want anyone to know, yet he could not be hidden" (v24).** He seems to have been looking for rest and privacy, perhaps to teach his disciples. His fame got there first.
- **"A woman whose little daughter had an unclean spirit" (v25).** A desperate mother. She "fell down at his feet," as Jairus had (5:22).
- **"A Gentile, a Syrophoenician by birth" (v26).** "Gentile" is literally "Greek," meaning she spoke Greek and belonged to the Greek-speaking world. "Syrophoenician" means a Phoenician from the province of Syria, as opposed to the Phoenicians of North Africa around Carthage. Matthew calls her "a Canaanite woman" (Matt 15:22), using the old name of Israel's ancient enemies. In every way she was an outsider: a woman, a Gentile, a descendant of the Canaanites, with a daughter possessed by an unclean spirit.
- **"Let the children be fed first, for it is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs" (v27).** This sounds harsh. Jews sometimes called Gentiles "dogs," and dogs in that world were mostly scavengers, not pets. But a few things soften it. The word Jesus uses is *kynaria*, a diminutive, "little dogs," the kind kept in a house. "First" leaves the door open: the gospel went to the Jews first, and then to the Gentiles (Rom 1:16). And Jesus seems to be inviting a response, testing and drawing out her faith, as he does in other places. His mission at this stage was to Israel (Matt 15:24). But "first" implies "then."
- **"Yes, Lord; yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs" (v28).** One of the great replies in the Gospels. She does not argue or take offense. She accepts the picture and turns it into a plea. Children at a meal drop crumbs, and the household dogs under the table get them. She does not ask for the children's place, only for a crumb. And a crumb from Jesus is enough. She is also the only person in Mark's Gospel to call Jesus "Lord" (*Kyrie*).
- **"For this statement you may go your way; the demon has left your daughter" (v29).** Jesus heals from a distance, without seeing the girl, as with the centurion's servant (Matt 8:5–13). Matthew adds Jesus' words, "O woman, great is your faith!" (Matt 15:28).
- **"She went home and found the child lying in bed and the demon gone" (v30).** "Lying" may suggest she was resting quietly at last after the violence of the demon.
- **Commentators:** Henry has the woman say, in effect, "I ask not for a loaf, no, nor for a morsel, only for a crumb," and sees her humility turning Jesus' image into a plea. JFB notes that "distress is quick of hearing," that Matthew calls her a woman of Canaan as "a more intelligible description to his Jewish readers," and that Jesus marveled only at faith and unbelief. Guzik notes that "dogs" was a term of contempt but that Jesus used the softer word for little house dogs, citing Barclay, and lists with Clarke the marks of her prayer. The Tyndale notes say Jesus' reply was not a final refusal but a way of drawing out her faith, and that "first" does not shut Gentiles out of God's grace.

#### Jesus Heals a Deaf Man (vv31–37)
- **"He returned from the region of Tyre and went through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee, in the region of the Decapolis" (v31).** A long, roundabout route: north to Sidon, then east and south, ending on the eastern side of the lake. It may have taken weeks, perhaps for time alone with his disciples. The Decapolis was where the man freed from the legion had been telling everyone about Jesus (5:20). Now the people bring their sick.
- **"A man who was deaf and had a speech impediment" (v32).** The rare word *mogilalos*, "speaking with difficulty," appears only here in the New Testament. It is used in the Greek version of Isaiah 35:6, which promises that when God comes to save his people, "the ears of the deaf [will be] unstopped… and the tongue of the mute sing for joy" (Isa 35:5–6). Mark seems to have chosen the word to point to that promise.
- **"Taking him aside from the crowd privately" (v33).** Jesus does not make the man a spectacle. A deaf man cannot hear what Jesus says, so Jesus uses signs the man can feel and see.
- **"He put his fingers into his ears, and after spitting touched his tongue" (v33).** Spittle was widely thought to have healing power in the ancient world, and Jesus uses it again in 8:23 and John 9:6. The actions tell the man, in a language he can understand, what Jesus is about to do for his ears and his tongue. They also show that the healing comes from Jesus himself.
- **"Looking up to heaven, he sighed" (v34).** Looking up is a gesture of prayer (6:41). The sigh is a groan of deep feeling. It may be compassion for the man, grief over the damage sin and suffering have done to God's world, or prayer too deep for words (Rom 8:22–23, 26). It is not a sign of difficulty.
- **"Ephphatha, that is, 'Be opened'" (v34).** Another of the Aramaic words Mark preserves (5:41). It is a single word, the man could perhaps read it on Jesus' lips, and Peter evidently never forgot it.
- **"His ears were opened, his tongue was released, and he spoke plainly" (v35).** "Released" is literally "the bond of his tongue was loosed." Some see in the wording a picture of release from bondage, as though the man's tongue had been bound.
- **"Jesus charged them to tell no one. But the more he charged them, the more zealously they proclaimed it" (v36).** In 5:19 Jesus told the man freed from demons to tell his story in this very region. Here he asks for silence, perhaps because the crowds were now growing into the kind of excitement that would hinder his mission (John 6:15). The command is ignored.
- **"He has done all things well" (v37).** The words may echo Genesis 1:31: "God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good." In Jesus the Creator is at work again, making things new. **"He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak."** The crowd does not quote Isaiah 35, but Mark's readers are meant to hear it: the God who comes to save is here.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jesus "sighed, not as if he found any difficulty in working this miracle," but out of pity, and that "Christ speaks as one having authority, and power went along with the word." He reads "Ephphatha" also as a picture of God opening hearts. JFB says the spittle shows that "the source of the healing virtue" is Jesus himself, cites Trench that the sigh was "over the wreck" sin had made, observes that Mark "loves to give such wonderful words just as they were spoken," and calls the result "a new creation." Guzik notes that *mogilalos* appears only here and that Mark echoes Isaiah 35. He follows Lane and Morgan in reading the sigh as compassion and prayer, and Wiersbe in linking it to the groaning of Romans 8.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter begins with an argument about clean hands and ends with Jesus' own hands in a deaf man's ears. The Pharisees thought holiness meant keeping the right things out: unwashed hands, unclean food, unclean people. Jesus shows that the real uncleanness is already inside, in the human heart, and that no ritual can wash it. Then he crosses the line himself, into Gentile country, to help a woman who asks only for crumbs and a man who cannot hear or speak. He declares foods clean because he has come to make people clean. Later he will give his disciples the bread of his body at a table, and the children's bread will be shared with all who come, Jews and Gentiles alike. He has done all things well.
