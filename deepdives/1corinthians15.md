*Setting:* Paul writes from Ephesus in about AD 53–55. This is the longest chapter in the letter and the fullest teaching on resurrection in the Bible. Paul has saved it for last, after answering the Corinthians' questions about marriage, idol food, worship and spiritual gifts. Some in the church were saying "there is no resurrection of the dead" (v12). They did not deny that Jesus rose. What they doubted was that believers' bodies would rise. Most Greeks thought of the body as something to be shed, so that the soul could go on free of it. When Paul preached the resurrection in Athens, some laughed at him (Acts 17:32). Paul answers in four movements: what the gospel says (vv1–11), what follows if the dead don't rise (vv12–34), what kind of body we will have (vv35–49), and the final victory over death (vv50–58).

#### The Resurrection of Christ (vv1–11)
- **"The gospel I preached to you, which you received, in which you stand" (v1).** Paul is not bringing anything new. He reminds them of what they already believe and points out that they are standing on it. Deny the resurrection and the ground under them goes.
- **"By which you are being saved, if you hold fast" (v2).** Salvation here is ongoing ("are being saved"). The "if" is not a threat to real believers. It is a warning against a faith that lets go of the message it began with. **"Unless you believed in vain"**: if the resurrection were false, their believing would have been empty from the start (v14).
- **"I delivered to you… what I also received" (v3).** These two verbs (*paradidōmi* and *paralambanō*) were the standard words for passing on a teaching handed down faithfully, the way a rabbi's students passed on what they had learned. Verses 3–5 have a balanced, almost memorized shape, and most scholars agree Paul is quoting an early creed. He probably learned it in Damascus or during his visit with Peter in Jerusalem (Gal 1:18), within a few years of the cross. This is some of the earliest Christian testimony we have.
- **"As of first importance" (v3).** These are the main things, not the only things. Four statements: Christ **died** for our sins, he was **buried**, he was **raised** on the third day, and he **appeared**. The burial confirms he was really dead. The appearances confirm he was really raised.
- **"In accordance with the Scriptures" (vv3–4).** Said twice. The death fulfills passages like Isaiah 53 ("he was pierced for our transgressions"). For the third day, the Tyndale notes point to Psalm 16:10, Hosea 6:2 and Jonah, the sign Jesus himself named (Matt 12:40). "He was raised" is passive: God the Father raised him (v15).
- **The witnesses (vv5–8).** **Cephas** is Peter's Aramaic name. Jesus appeared to him alone on Easter Day (Luke 24:34). **"The twelve"** is the group's title, even though Judas was gone. **More than five hundred at one time** is mentioned nowhere else. Many think it happened in Galilee (Matt 28:16–17). Paul adds that **"most of whom are still alive"**: in effect, go and ask them. **James** is the Lord's brother, who did not believe during Jesus' ministry (John 7:5) and later led the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13). An appearance of the risen Jesus explains that change better than anything else.
- **No women on the list.** The women at the tomb were the first witnesses (John 20:14–18). The creed leaves them out, probably because a woman's testimony carried little weight in court in that culture. The Gospels, by contrast, put them first, which is a detail no one inventing the story would have chosen.
- **"As to one untimely born" (v8).** The word *ektrōma* means a miscarriage or a premature, stillborn birth (Abbott-Smith: "an abortion, an untimely birth"). It may be the insult some opponents used about Paul, which he takes up himself. Or it may mean he was born into apostleship abruptly and out of season, after the others. Either way it is a picture of something that had no business living, given life by God.
- **"I am the least of the apostles" (v9).** Paul never got over the fact that he had persecuted the church (Acts 8:3; 1 Tim 1:15).
- **"By the grace of God I am what I am" (v10).** One of the great sentences in Paul. Grace made him an apostle, and grace kept him working. "Not in vain" uses *kenos*, "empty," a word that comes back in v14 and again in the final verse (v58). **"I worked harder than any of them, though it was not I"**: Paul really worked, yet the credit is God's. Effort and grace are partners, not rivals (Phil 2:12–13).
- **"So we preach and so you believed" (v11).** Paul and the other apostles preach the same message. The Corinthians' factions (1:12) could not set Paul against Peter on this.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The doctrine of Christ's death and resurrection, is the foundation of Christianity." He sees the same grace that humbled Paul making him zealous. JFB calls Paul "the one abortively born," a supernumerary apostle added suddenly rather than trained over three years. Guzik takes the same line and suggests Paul picked up a word the Corinthians had used to belittle him. Guzik also argues that so many living witnesses, including a former skeptic and a former persecutor, leave little room for theories of hallucination or a stolen body.

#### The Resurrection of the Dead (vv12–34)
- **"How can some of you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?" (v12).** What exactly they believed is debated. Many think Greek influence made the idea of a raised body seem crude. Others think some believed the resurrection was purely spiritual and had already happened, as some in Ephesus later claimed (2 Tim 2:18). Either way, Paul's answer is the same.
- **The chain of consequences (vv13–19).** Paul reasons step by step. If the dead are not raised, then Christ was not raised. Then preaching is empty, faith is empty, and the apostles are false witnesses about God (v15). Then "**you are still in your sins**" (v17), because a dead Savior has paid nothing. Then believers who have died are simply gone (v18). The resurrection is not one doctrine among many. Take it out and the whole faith falls.
- **"Fallen asleep" (vv6, 18, 20).** *Koimaō* is the usual New Testament word for a believer's death (John 11:11; 1 Thess 4:13). Sleep is something one wakes from.
- **"Of all people most to be pitied" (v19).** If the Christian life gains nothing beyond this life, then believers who give up so much for Christ have made a bad trade. Paul is not saying faith brings no joy now. He is saying its hope reaches past death or it is not the hope it claims to be.
- **"But in fact Christ has been raised" (v20).** The turn of the whole chapter. **"Firstfruits"** (*aparchē*) was the first sheaf of the harvest offered to God (Lev 23:10–11), the pledge that the rest of the crop was coming. That offering was made the day after the Sabbath following Passover, the very day Jesus rose. His resurrection is the first sheaf, and his people are the harvest.
- **"As in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" (v22).** Two men, each the head of a humanity. Adam brought death to all who belong to him, and Christ brings life to all who belong to him (Rom 5:12–21). Verse 23 makes the second "all" clear: "**those who belong to Christ**." Other passages teach that unbelievers will also be raised, but for judgment (John 5:28–29; Acts 24:15).
- **"Each in his own order" (v23).** The word was used of ranks of soldiers. Christ first, then his people "at his coming" (*parousia*, the word for a king's arrival). **"Then comes the end" (v24).** Premillennial readers see a gap here, the thousand-year reign of Revelation 20, between Christ's coming and "the end." Amillennial and postmillennial readers see Christ reigning now from heaven, with the end following directly on his return. Both agree the end is the complete defeat of every hostile power.
- **"He must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet" (v25).** This draws on Psalm 110:1, the Old Testament verse the New Testament quotes most. The Tyndale notes mention ancient pictures of kings with their feet on the necks of the conquered. Verse 27 adds Psalm 8:6, where all things are put under the feet of "man." Christ is the true man who receives the rule Adam lost.
- **"The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (v26).** Death is not a friend or a natural part of life to be accepted. It is an enemy, and it will be abolished (*katargeō*, "made powerless, brought to nothing").
- **"The Son himself will also be subjected" (v28).** This has troubled readers since the early church, and Arians used it to argue the Son is less than God. The historic Christian reading is that Paul is speaking of the Son's work as Mediator. Having won the kingdom, he hands it to the Father, so "**that God may be all in all**." It is a son's glad honoring of his Father, not a lower nature. The same Paul calls Christ "God over all" (Rom 9:5).
- **"Baptized on behalf of the dead" (v29).** One of the hardest verses in the Bible, with dozens of proposed meanings. The main ones: (1) some in Corinth were being baptized as proxies for believers who died unbaptized, and Paul mentions it without approving, noting it makes no sense without a resurrection. Notice he says "people," not "we." (2) New converts were baptized "for the sake of" the dead, hoping to be reunited with believing loved ones. (3) Baptism here is a figure for suffering and martyrdom, as in Mark 10:38. Whatever it was, nothing else in the New Testament or early church supports a practice of baptizing the living for the dead, as the Latter-day Saints do today.
- **"I die every day!" (v31).** In context this means physical danger, not dying to sin. Paul lives with risk every hour (2 Cor 11:23–28). Why would he, if there were nothing beyond death?
- **"I fought with beasts at Ephesus" (v32).** "Humanly speaking" suggests a figure of speech. A Roman citizen could not lawfully be sent to the arena, and Paul doesn't list it among his sufferings in 2 Corinthians 11. Most think he means savage human opponents (Acts 19:23–41; 2 Cor 1:8–9).
- **"Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die" (v32).** A quote of Isaiah 22:13, where Jerusalem partied instead of repenting. Without resurrection, Paul says, that is the logical way to live.
- **"Bad company ruins good morals" (v33).** A line from the Greek comic playwright Menander, a well-known saying by then. The "company" here is those who deny the resurrection. Wrong belief leads to wrong living.
- **"Wake up from your drunken stupor" (v34).** One Greek word, *eknēphō*, "to sober up after drinking." **"Some have no knowledge of God"**: in a church proud of its knowledge (8:1), that stings, and Paul means it to.
- **Commentators:** On the consequences, Henry notes that believers "of all men, enjoy solid comforts amidst all their difficulties and trials," so v19 does not deny present joy. On v29 the three part ways. Guzik takes the plain meaning, proxy baptism, mentioned without approval, and calls the modern practice unscriptural. Henry suggests "baptism is used here in a figure, for afflictions, sufferings, and martyrdom." JFB lists several views and leans toward new believers who put off baptism until near death. On v32 all three read "beasts" as violent men. Henry's application of v32: "we must not live like beasts, as we do not die like them." Guzik and JFB both connect firstfruits to the day after Passover.

#### The Resurrection Body (vv35–49)
- **"How are the dead raised? With what kind of body?" (v35).** This sounds like a scoffer's question. A body that has rotted or burned, how could it rise?
- **"You foolish person!" (v36).** Not name-calling but the wisdom literature's word for someone who leaves God out of his thinking (Ps 14:1). The answer is lying in every field.
- **The seed (vv36–38).** A seed must be buried and "die" before it grows (John 12:24). What comes up is the same life but a far different body: a bare kernel becomes a stalk of wheat. The Tyndale notes put it well in substance: the plant does not look like its seed, but the seed decides what the plant will be. So the raised body is continuous with the body buried, the same person, and yet transformed. **"God gives it a body as he has chosen" (v38).** The power is God's.
- **Different flesh, different glories (vv39–41).** People, animals, birds, fish, sun, moon and stars each have a body and glory suited to them. If God can make that much variety, he can make a body fitted for the age to come. Some read "star differs from star" as degrees of reward among believers, but Paul's point here is simply variety.
- **Four contrasts (vv42–44).** Sown **perishable**, raised **imperishable**. Sown in **dishonor**, raised in **glory**. Sown in **weakness**, raised in **power**. Sown a **natural body**, raised a **spiritual body**. "Sown" is a burial word, the language of a graveside.
- **"A spiritual body" (v44).** This does not mean a body made of spirit, a ghost. The contrast is *psychikos* versus *pneumatikos*, the same pair Paul used in 2:14–15 for the "natural person" and the "spiritual person." Both there and here the difference is not what something is made of but what drives it. The natural body is suited to ordinary earthly life. The spiritual body is wholly given life and directed by the Holy Spirit. The risen Jesus ate fish and could be touched (Luke 24:39–43), yet he was no longer bound as before.
- **"The first man Adam became a living being" (v45).** Paul quotes Genesis 2:7 and adds "first" and "Adam." **"The last Adam became a life-giving spirit."** Adam received life. Christ gives it. He is called the *last* Adam because there is no third. Every person belongs to one of these two.
- **"The man of dust… the man of heaven" (vv47–49).** Adam came from the ground (Gen 2:7; 3:19). Christ came from heaven. We have carried Adam's likeness into weakness and death. We will carry Christ's likeness into glory (Phil 3:21; 1 John 3:2).
- **"We shall also bear" (v49).** Many early manuscripts read "let us bear," a call to live now in Christ's likeness, and the ESV notes this in its footnote. The two words differ by one letter in Greek and probably sounded alike when read aloud. Most translations prefer "we shall," because the whole passage is describing what God will do, not urging effort.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "It is foolish to question the Almighty power of God to raise the dead." He also writes, frankly, "Nothing is more loathsome than a dead body," which makes the coming glory brighter by contrast. JFB defines the natural body as literally an "animal body," and the spiritual body as one "wholly moulded by the Spirit." It calls Christ "the LAST Head of humanity." Guzik insists the spiritual body is a real, physical body, not a "pure spirit," and points to Jesus' risen body as the model. He suggests God may build the new body from the blueprint of the old, which is his own speculation, not Paul's.

#### Mystery and Victory (vv50–58)
- **"Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (v50).** "Flesh and blood" is a Hebrew way of saying human nature as it now is, mortal and weak (Matt 16:17). It does not mean bodies are shut out. It means our present, perishing bodies must be changed first.
- **"Behold! I tell you a mystery" (v51).** In Paul a "mystery" is not a puzzle but something God has now revealed that could not be known otherwise. **"We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed."** Not every believer will die, because some will be alive when Christ returns. But all will be changed, the living and the dead alike (1 Thess 4:15–17). Some say Paul expected to be alive at the return and was wrong. Others answer that he simply lived ready for it and included himself among those who might be, without setting a date.
- **"In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye" (v52).** "Moment" is *atomos*, "uncuttable," the smallest possible slice of time. It is where the word "atom" comes from. The "twinkling" is the flick of an eye. The change will not be gradual.
- **"At the last trumpet" (v52).** Trumpets summoned Israel's assemblies and announced God's coming at Sinai (Exod 19:16). The trumpet appears with Christ's return in 1 Thessalonians 4:16 and Matthew 24:31. Whether it is the same as the seventh trumpet of Revelation 11:15 is debated, and Christians who differ on the timing of the rapture read it differently.
- **"This perishable body must put on the imperishable" (v53).** Like putting on new clothes. Paul uses the same picture in 2 Corinthians 5:2–4.
- **"Death is swallowed up in victory" (v54).** From Isaiah 25:8, where God hosts a feast for all peoples on his mountain and swallows up death forever. Death, which has swallowed everyone, is itself swallowed.
- **"O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?" (v55).** From Hosea 13:14. In Hosea the line may be God summoning death to judge rebellious Israel. Paul turns it into a taunt over a beaten enemy, because of what Christ has done. "Sting" is *kentron*, the stinger of a scorpion (Rev 9:10). It is also the word for the "goads" Jesus told Paul he was kicking against (Acts 26:14).
- **"The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law" (v56).** Romans in a single verse. Sin is what gives death its deadly sting, and the law, good as it is, gives sin its power by exposing it and condemning us (Rom 7:7–13). Take sin away and death has nothing left to strike with.
- **"Thanks be to God, who gives us the victory" (v57).** Present tense. The victory is a gift, already given "through our Lord Jesus Christ."
- **"Be steadfast, immovable, always abounding" (v58).** Paul does not end with speculation about the future but with a charge for now. Believers are to stand firm, refuse to be shifted (as some in Corinth were being shifted on this very doctrine), and keep giving themselves to the Lord's work. **"Your labor is not in vain"**: *kenos* again, as in vv10 and 14. If Christ is raised, nothing done for him is empty.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Death may seize a believer, but it cannot hold him in its power." He explains the sting as sin, drawn out by Christ's atonement. JFB agrees, noting that without sin there would be no death and that the law makes sin's guilt clearer, while "Christ's people are no longer under the law." Guzik takes "we" in v51 as believers of every generation rather than a mistaken prediction, and suggests the last trumpet is not necessarily the last trumpet of Revelation. He adds that for those outside Christ, death still stings. On v58 all three read the call to steadfastness as the practical answer to the doubters at Corinth.

#### Pointing to Christ
The whole chapter hangs on one fact: "Christ has been raised from the dead." Because the firstfruits has been gathered, the harvest is certain. The last Adam has already walked out of the grave in the body his people will one day share, and he took the sting out of death by bearing the sin that gave it power. That is why a believer can stand at a graveside and say, without pretending, "O death, where is your sting?"
