*Setting:* Still the night before the crucifixion, about AD 30 or 33. Jesus has finished the meal, washed the disciples' feet, sent Judas out, and promised the Holy Spirit. Chapter 14 ended with "Rise, let us go from here," so chs 15–17 may have been spoken on the walk from the upper room toward the Kidron Valley and the olive grove across it (18:1), or in the room before they left. If they were walking, they would have passed vineyards on the slopes and perhaps the temple, whose great entrance was decorated with a golden vine, a symbol of Israel. Jesus now gives the seventh and last of his "I am" pictures in John. The chapter moves from union with Christ (vv1–11), to love within the community (vv12–17), to hatred from the world (vv18–27).

#### I Am the True Vine (vv1–17)
- **"I am the true vine" (v1).** The vine was one of the Old Testament's main pictures of Israel. God brought "a vine out of Egypt" and planted it (Ps 80:8–16). But again and again the vine disappointed him. In Isaiah's song of the vineyard, God looked for good grapes and got wild ones (Isa 5:1–7). Jeremiah says the choice vine turned "degenerate" (Jer 2:21), and Ezekiel says a fruitless vine is good only for fuel (Ezek 15). Jesus calls himself the "true" (*alēthinos*, genuine, real) vine. Where Israel failed, he is the faithful Israel, and to belong to God's people now means to be joined to him.
- **"My Father is the vinedresser" (v1).** The vinedresser (*geōrgos*, farmer or gardener) owns and tends the vine. The Father is in charge of the whole process, cutting and pruning included.
- **"Every branch in me that does not bear fruit he takes away" (v2).** The verb *airō* means "take up, lift" or "take away." Most translations and commentators take it as removal, which fits v6. Some, following a suggestion popularized by James Montgomery Boice, read it as "lifts up," like a vinedresser raising a trailing branch off the ground so it can get light and bear fruit. The lexicon allows both senses, but the flow of the passage favors "takes away."
- **"Every branch that does bear fruit he prunes" (v2).** Vines in Palestine were cut back hard in winter and trimmed again in spring, removing growth that used up sap without producing grapes. A pruned vine looks wounded but bears far more. "Prunes" is *kathairō*, "cleanse," and v3 plays on it: "Already you are clean (*katharoi*) because of the word." The word of Jesus is the Father's pruning knife (Heb 12:5–11).
- **"Abide in me, and I in you" (v4).** "Abide" (*menō*, remain, stay, make one's home) appears ten times in vv4–10. It is related to the "rooms" (*monai*) of 14:2 and the "home" of 14:23. A branch does not strain to produce grapes. It stays attached, and the sap does the work. Abiding means continuing in trust, in his words (v7), in his love (v9) and in obedience (v10).
- **"Apart from me you can do nothing" (v5).** Not "a little less." Nothing. The branch is all dependence. This is a hard word for self-reliance and a freeing word for weakness (2 Cor 12:9).
- **"Thrown away like a branch and withers… thrown into the fire" (v6).** Vine wood is too soft and crooked to build with, and pruned branches were gathered and burned (Ezek 15:2–5). This is a serious warning. Christians have read it in three main ways. Some (often in the Wesleyan and Arminian traditions) take it as a warning that true believers can fall away. Many Reformed readers see branches that were attached outwardly but never truly alive, like Judas, who has just left. Others think of believers whose fruitless works are burned while they themselves are saved (1 Cor 3:15). The plainest point stands on every view: a branch without fruit is not living from the vine.
- **"Ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you" (v7).** The condition is not a technique but a life: "if you abide in me, and my words abide in you." Someone whose desires are shaped by Christ's words asks for what Christ wants.
- **"That you bear much fruit and so prove to be my disciples" (v8).** Fruit is not the way to become a disciple but the evidence of being one. In the New Testament "fruit" includes character (Gal 5:22–23), good works (Col 1:10), praise (Heb 13:15), and new believers (Rom 1:13). The verse leads into vv12–17: the first fruit Jesus names is love.
- **"As the Father has loved me, so have I loved you" (v9).** The measure of Jesus' love for his disciples is the Father's love for the Son, a love that existed "before the foundation of the world" (17:24). "Abide in my love" means stay in the place of being loved by him.
- **"If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love" (v10).** Obedience does not earn his love. It keeps a disciple living in it, as a child who obeys stays near the parent. Jesus himself is the model: "just as I have kept my Father's commandments."
- **"That my joy may be in you" (v11).** On the night of his arrest Jesus talks about joy. His joy is the joy of the Son who knows and does the Father's will (Heb 12:2). He wants it to be theirs "full," a phrase John repeats (16:24; 17:13; 1 John 1:4).
- **"This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you" (v12).** This repeats the new commandment of 13:34. The vine bears its fruit in a community.
- **"Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends" (v13).** Within hours Jesus will do exactly this. Paul goes further: Christ died for us "while we were enemies" (Rom 5:8–10). Here Jesus speaks from the disciples' side: they are his friends, and he will die for them.
- **"You are my friends if you do what I command you" (v14).** This is not a condition for becoming his friend, as if friendship were earned. It describes what his friends are like. In the Old Testament only Abraham (2 Chr 20:7; Isa 41:8; Jas 2:23) and Moses (Exod 33:11) are called God's friends. Jesus gives the title to eleven ordinary men.
- **"No longer do I call you servants" (v15).** The word is *doulos*, slave or bondservant. A slave does what he is told without being told why. A friend is let in on the plan: "all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you." Paul, Peter, James and Jude still gladly call themselves Christ's *douloi* (Rom 1:1; Jas 1:1). Friendship with Jesus does not end serving him. It changes the relationship in which the serving happens.
- **"You did not choose me, but I chose you" (v16).** Rabbis' disciples normally chose their teacher. Jesus reversed it (1:43; Mark 3:13–14). His choice comes with a purpose: "that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide." The disciples were not chosen to sit still but to go.
- **"These things I command you, so that you will love one another" (v17).** The section ends where it began (v12). Love for one another is the fruit of the vine.
- **Commentators:** Guzik suggests Jesus may have spoken near the temple's golden vine, and notes that the Greek for "prune" is the word elsewhere rendered "cleanse," with the word of Jesus as the cleansing agent. He reports Boice's "lift up" reading of v2 and lays out the three views of the branches thrown into the fire. JFB explains pruning as "stripping it, as the husbandman does, of what is rank," painful but needful, and points to Ezekiel 15 for the fate of fruitless wood. JFB also reads "abide in my love" as staying in Christ's love for us, not our love for him. Henry warns, "Unfruitful professors are unfaithful professors; professors, and no more," and on v14 says simply, "The followers of Christ are the friends of Christ."

#### The Hatred of the World (vv18–27)
- **"If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you" (v18).** "The world" (*kosmos*) in John often means humanity organized in rebellion against God (1:10; 7:7). It is the same world God loved and sent his Son to save (3:16). The disciples should not be surprised by its hostility. It is aimed first at Jesus.
- **"I chose you out of the world" (v19).** The choosing of v16 has a second edge. Being chosen by Christ makes a person different from the world, and the world senses it. Its love is reserved for "its own."
- **"A servant is not greater than his master" (v20).** Jesus repeats his saying from the foot washing (13:16), now with a new application. The disciple shares the master's humility, and also his reception: some will persecute, some will "keep your word" as they kept his. The book of Acts shows both.
- **"On account of my name, because they do not know him who sent me" (v21).** Persecution of Christians is, at bottom, ignorance of God. Paul later said this about himself (1 Tim 1:13; Acts 26:9).
- **"If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not have been guilty of sin" (v22).** Jesus does not mean they were sinless before he came. He means the particular sin of rejecting him. Light brings responsibility (9:41). His words (v22) and his works (v24) left them "no excuse."
- **"Whoever hates me hates my Father also" (v23).** One cannot reject the Son and claim to love the Father (5:23; 1 John 2:23).
- **"The works that no one else did" (v24).** John has recorded signs no prophet ever did, such as a man born blind given sight (9:32) and a man four days dead raised.
- **"They hated me without a cause" (v25).** This is quoted from Psalm 69:4 (also 35:19). "Their Law" here means the Scriptures as a whole. Psalm 69 is one of the Psalms the New Testament quotes most about Christ: "zeal for your house" (John 2:17), the sour wine given him (Ps 69:21; John 19:29), and the curse on the betrayer (Acts 1:20). The psalmist suffered unjust hatred, and Jesus fulfills that suffering perfectly.
- **"When the Helper comes… he will bear witness about me" (v26).** The Spirit, the *paraklētos* or advocate (14:16), is pictured as a witness in a trial. The world puts Jesus on trial, and the Spirit testifies in his defense. The verse names all three persons: the Son sends the Spirit "from the Father," and the Spirit "proceeds from the Father."
- **"Who proceeds from the Father" (v26).** This phrase was taken into the Nicene Creed (AD 381). Later the Western church added that the Spirit proceeds "from the Father *and the Son*" (*filioque*), and the disagreement contributed to the split between Eastern and Western churches in 1054. In context Jesus is mainly speaking of the Spirit's mission, being sent into the world. Both East and West agree that the Son sends the Spirit, as this verse says.
- **"And you also will bear witness, because you have been with me from the beginning" (v27).** The Spirit's witness and the apostles' witness go together (Acts 5:32: "we are witnesses… and so is the Holy Spirit"). The apostles had been with Jesus "from the beginning," which is why their testimony became the foundation of the church (Acts 1:21–22; 1 John 1:1–3). John's Gospel is itself part of that witness (21:24).
- **Commentators:** Guzik says the world hates believers because their lives expose its sin, and that since Jesus faced this hatred first, believers can press on with courage. He adds that the Spirit's work will always match the character of Jesus. JFB identifies v25 as "quoted from the Messianic Ps 69:4" and says the coming of the Son leaves the world no "pretext" for its sin. JFB gives no separate comment on vv17–21, v23 or v26. Henry: "Whom Christ blesseth the world curseth."

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel was God's vine and kept failing to bear fruit. Jesus is the true vine, the faithful Israel, in whom God finally gets the harvest he was looking for. Everyone who belongs to God now belongs by being joined to him, drawing life from him the way a branch draws sap. Apart from him there is nothing, and in him there is fruit, joy, answered prayer and a love that lays down its life. On the night he is betrayed, Jesus calls frightened men his friends and tells them that the world's hatred is aimed at him first. Then he goes out to lay down his life for those friends, and sends the Spirit to tell the world the truth about him.
