*Setting:* Babylonia, around 591 BC. The exiles had a saying that explained their suffering in a way that let them off the hook: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge." In other words, our parents sinned and we are paying for it. It had some basis. King Manasseh's sins, two generations earlier, were said to have sealed Judah's fate (2 Kings 23:26; 24:3–4), and the law spoke of God "visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children" (Exod 20:5). Lamentations voices the same feeling: "Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities" (Lam 5:7). Jeremiah, in Jerusalem, heard the same proverb and answered it (Jer 31:29–30). Ezekiel 18 is God's full reply. It is not a general treatise on how guilt passes from one generation to the next. It is a pastoral word to a people using the past as an excuse, and it ends with one of the Bible's most passionate appeals to repent.

#### The Soul Who Sins Shall Die (vv1–32)
- **"The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" (v2).** Unripe grapes are so sour they make the mouth pucker. The proverb says the children feel the effect of what the parents ate. It was fatalistic: nothing we do matters, because our fate was settled before we were born. It also accused God of injustice, as the complaint of v25 makes plain.
- **"This proverb shall no more be used by you in Israel" (v3).** God swears it with an oath. He does not deny that one generation's sins affect the next. That is obvious in any family or nation. He denies that anyone in Israel is being condemned for someone else's sin while innocent themselves.
- **"Behold, all souls are mine… the soul who sins shall die" (v4).** "Soul" is *nephesh*, the whole living person. Every person belongs to God directly, father and son alike, and each stands before him on their own account. The law already said this for human courts: "Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be put to death because of their fathers. Each one shall be put to death for his own sin" (Deut 24:16). King Amaziah applied that law (2 Kings 14:6). **"Shall die"** in context means dying under God's judgment, in the disaster now falling on Judah. The New Testament takes the principle further, to eternal judgment (Rom 6:23).
- **Three generations (vv5–18).** Ezekiel builds a test case, like a priest giving a ruling, using three generations: a righteous man, his wicked son, and a righteous grandson. Many scholars see a loose echo of Judah's last kings: the godly Josiah, his wicked sons, and the hope of a new generation. The cases are meant to be general, though, and Ezekiel names no one.
- **"If a man is righteous and does what is just and right" (v5).** "Just and right" (*mishpat* and *tsedaqah*) is the standard pair for living as God requires (Gen 18:19). What follows is a sample list, much like the entrance liturgies of Psalms 15 and 24, which ask who may stand before God. It covers worship, sexual purity and justice to neighbors, the whole of life.
- **"Does not eat upon the mountains or lift up his eyes to the idols" (v6).** Eating on the mountains means sharing sacrificial meals at the hilltop shrines, the "high places" of 6:3. To "lift up the eyes" is to look to them for help (Ps 121:1). The first mark of righteousness is undivided worship.
- **"Does not defile his neighbor's wife or approach a woman in her time of menstrual impurity" (v6).** Adultery was forbidden in the commandments (Exod 20:14). The law also forbade sexual relations during a woman's period (Lev 18:19; 20:18). It was part of a holiness code that marked Israel as distinct, and it shows that the righteous man honors God's commands even in private life.
- **"Restores to the debtor his pledge" (v7).** A poor man who borrowed might give his cloak as security. The law said it must be given back by sunset, since it might be his only blanket (Exod 22:26–27; Deut 24:12–13). **"Gives his bread to the hungry and covers the naked."** Righteousness is not only refraining from harm but actively doing good (Isa 58:7). Job described his own life the same way (Job 31:16–20).
- **"Does not lend at interest or take any profit" (v8).** The word for interest (*neshekh*) comes from a verb meaning "to bite." Loans in Israel were mainly to the poor in emergencies, not business loans, and the law forbade charging a fellow Israelite interest on them (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:35–37; Deut 23:19–20). To profit from a neighbor's distress was to take a bite out of him.
- **"He is righteous; he shall surely live" (v9).** This sounds like salvation by works, but in its setting it is not. The man described **"walks in my statutes… by acting faithfully."** He lives within the covenant God gave by grace, and his deeds show that his heart belongs to the LORD. The life promised echoes Leviticus 18:5: "if a person does them, he shall live by them." Ezekiel's hearers would have known that the covenant always included sacrifice and forgiveness for those who sinned and repented.
- **"If he fathers a son who is violent, a shedder of blood" (v10).** The second generation reverses the first, point by point. **"Shall he then live? He shall not live… his blood shall be upon himself" (v13).** A godly father cannot save an ungodly son, which echoes 14:14–20. Hezekiah, one of Judah's best kings, fathered Manasseh, its worst (2 Kings 21:1–9).
- **"Suppose this man fathers a son who sees all the sins that his father has done; he sees, and does not do likewise" (v14).** The third generation turns back. The word "sees" is used twice. This son looks honestly at his father's life and chooses another way. **"He shall not die for his father's iniquity; he shall surely live" (v17).** Josiah, Manasseh's grandson, did exactly this (2 Kings 22:1–2). A wicked upbringing is real, but it is not a sentence of doom.
- **"Why should not the son suffer for the iniquity of the father?" (v19).** The people's reply is surprising. They seem almost to want the old proverb to be true, because it lets them see themselves as innocent victims. God's answer repeats the principle plainly: **"The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son" (v20).**
- **What about Exodus 20:5?** The commandment says God visits "the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and the fourth generation of those who hate me." The last phrase matters. The children who share their fathers' guilt are those who continue in their fathers' hatred of God. Ezekiel does not contradict Moses. He addresses people who were themselves sinning while blaming their ancestors. The consequences of a father's sin can reach the children, but the guilt before God belongs to the one who sins.
- **"If a wicked person turns away from all his sins… he shall surely live" (v21).** Now the focus shifts from generations to a single life. Just as a person is not trapped by their parents' past, they are not trapped by their own. The verb is *shuv*, turn or return, the Old Testament word for repentance. **"None of the transgressions that he has committed shall be remembered against him" (v22).** God does not keep a ledger to hold against the repentant. Isaiah promised the same: "I… will not remember your sins" (Isa 43:25).
- **"Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked… and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?" (v23).** This is the heart of the chapter. God's justice is real, but it is not cold. He does not want anyone to die. This verse is repeated almost word for word in 33:11 and echoed in the New Testament: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Tim 2:4) and is "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Pet 3:9).
- **"But when a righteous person turns away from his righteousness… None of the righteous deeds that he has done shall be remembered" (v24).** This works in reverse too. A good past is no insurance policy. **"The treachery of which he is guilty"** uses *ma'al* again, breaking faith (14:13; 15:8). Christians read this verse differently. Arminian and Wesleyan readers see a warning that a true believer can fall away and be lost. Calvinist and Reformed readers argue that those who finally turn away were never truly regenerate (1 John 2:19), or that the verse speaks of outward covenant righteousness and God's present judgment rather than eternal salvation. Both sides take the warning seriously. Ezekiel's own point is pastoral: no one can rest on past obedience while living in present rebellion.
- **"Yet you say, 'The way of the Lord is not just'" (v25).** The Hebrew means "is not level" or "not weighed out fairly," like a dishonest merchant's scales. The people's complaint is that God's ways are arbitrary. God throws the charge back twice (vv25, 29): **"Is it not your ways that are not just?"** The truly unfair thing would be to punish the repentant for what they have turned from, or to reward the rebel for a goodness he has abandoned.
- **"Because he considered and turned away" (v28).** Repentance begins with honest reflection. "Considered" is the verb "to see," the same word used of the grandson in v14. The prodigal son "came to himself" before he went home (Luke 15:17).
- **"I will judge you… every one according to his ways" (v30).** The chapter ends by putting each listener on trial. **"Repent and turn from all your transgressions, lest iniquity be your ruin."** "Ruin" is the word for a stumbling block (*mikshol*, 14:3). Unrepented sin is what will trip them up, not their fathers' sin.
- **"Make yourselves a new heart and a new spirit!" (v31).** This is a striking command, since God himself promises to give a new heart (11:19; 36:26). Both are true. God calls people to do what only he can enable, and the call itself is a means by which he works. Paul holds the same tension: "work out your own salvation… for it is God who works in you" (Phil 2:12–13). **"Why will you die, O house of Israel?"** The question is a plea, not a threat.
- **"For I have no pleasure in the death of anyone… so turn, and live" (v32).** The chapter ends with two words in Hebrew, "turn and live." The proverb said the future was fixed. God says it is open to anyone who will turn.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "All souls are in the hand of the great Creator." He is careful about v9: the penitent will live "in their righteousness… not for their righteousness, as if that were an atonement for their sins." On v24 he refuses to turn the verse into a debate: "The question is not whether the truly righteous ever become apostates." On the people's complaint he says, "In all our disputes with God, he is in the right, and we are in the wrong." JFB reads Exodus 20:5 as applying to children who share their fathers' hatred of God, and calls the apostasy of v24 "utter," distinct from the lapses of believers who later repent. It concludes, "Those alone are true saints who by the grace of God persevere." On v31 JFB says the command shows "what they ought to do," since "God alone can make us a new heart," and it is meant to drive people to seek the Spirit. Guzik agrees that the proverb was a fatalistic dodge. He admits that v24 is hard for the doctrine of the perseverance of the saints and leaves the warning standing. He links v23 to 2 Peter 3:9.

#### Pointing to Christ
"The soul who sins shall die" is just, and it leaves everyone guilty, because no one fully matches the righteous man of vv5–9. There has been only one man who did. Jesus is the righteous one who walked in all God's statutes, and yet he died. That death broke the rule of this chapter on purpose. The righteous one suffered for the iniquity of others, "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (1 Pet 3:18). Because of that exchange, God can say to the wicked who turn, "None of the transgressions that he has committed shall be remembered against him," without being unjust (Rom 3:26). The God who has "no pleasure in the death of anyone" proved it by sending his Son, and through the Spirit he gives the new heart that Ezekiel told Israel to make.
