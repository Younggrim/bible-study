*Setting:* About AD 28–29, roughly thirty years after chapter 2 (Luke 3:1, 23 dates John's ministry to the fifteenth year of the emperor Tiberius). Matthew skips Jesus' childhood and youth in Nazareth entirely. Herod the Great is long dead. Judea is now under a Roman governor, Pontius Pilate, and Galilee under Herod Antipas. After four centuries without a prophet, a man appears in the desert by the Jordan who looks and sounds like Elijah. Crowds stream out to him. Chapter 3 introduces John the Baptist's message, his clash with the religious leaders, and the moment when Jesus himself comes to be baptized and the Father and the Spirit bear witness to him.

#### John the Baptist Prepares the Way (vv1–12)
- **"In those days John the Baptist came preaching in the wilderness of Judea" (v1).** "In those days" is a loose Old Testament phrase for "at that time." The wilderness of Judea is the dry, rugged land falling from the Judean hills down to the Dead Sea and the lower Jordan valley. The wilderness was full of meaning for Israel: the exodus generation wandered there forty years, and the prophets promised that God would meet his people there again and begin a new exodus (Hos 2:14; Isa 40:3). "The Baptist" (*ho baptistēs*) means "the one who baptizes." It became his title because his baptism was so distinctive.
- **"Repent" (v2).** The Greek *metanoeō* means a change of mind, but in the Bible it carries the Hebrew sense of *shuv*, "turn around." It is not just feeling sorry but changing direction: turning from sin to God, in thinking and in living. John will demand "fruit in keeping with repentance" (v8). Luke gives examples: share food and clothes, collect no more tax than is owed, do not extort (Luke 3:10–14).
- **"For the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (v2).** This is the theme of Matthew's whole Gospel. Jesus will preach the same words (4:17). "Kingdom" means God's reign or rule more than a territory. "At hand" means it has come near, close enough to touch. Matthew says "kingdom of heaven" over thirty times where Mark and Luke say "kingdom of God." Most think this reflects the Jewish habit of avoiding saying God's name out of reverence, using "heaven" in its place (compare the prodigal's "I have sinned against heaven," Luke 15:18). The two phrases mean the same thing. Daniel had promised that "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that shall never be destroyed" (Dan 2:44; 7:13–14). John says: it is about to arrive.
- **"The voice of one crying in the wilderness: 'Prepare the way of the Lord'" (v3).** This is Isaiah 40:3, the opening of the great "comfort" section of Isaiah. In ancient times, before a king visited a region, workers would fill potholes and straighten roads. Isaiah pictured a road built through the desert for God himself to come back to his people. John is that road-builder. Repentance is the road work. And "the Lord" in Isaiah is the LORD God, so the one John prepares for is God himself. The Gospel writers apply it to Jesus.
- **"A garment of camel's hair and a leather belt around his waist" (v4).** This is how Elijah was described: "He wore a garment of hair, with a belt of leather about his waist" (2 Kings 1:8). Malachi had promised Elijah would come before the day of the LORD (Mal 4:5). John's clothing was a deliberate sign, like a uniform. Jesus later says plainly, "he is Elijah who is to come" (11:14).
- **"His food was locusts and wild honey" (v4).** Locusts were clean food under the law (Lev 11:22) and are still eaten by poor people in the region, roasted or dried. Wild honey came from wild bees in the rocks, or perhaps sap from trees. It is the diet of a man living off the land, outside the comforts and systems of the city.
- **"Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region about the Jordan were going out to him" (v5).** A huge movement. Josephus, the Jewish historian, describes John as "a good man" who called the Jews to virtue and baptism, and says the crowds were so large that Herod Antipas feared a revolt.
- **"They were baptized by him in the river Jordan, confessing their sins" (v6).** "Baptize" (*baptizō*) means to dip, plunge or immerse. Jews practiced ritual washings for purity, and Gentiles converting to Judaism were immersed. The community at Qumran by the Dead Sea had frequent ritual washings. But John's baptism was different: it was once for all, it was for Jews, and it was tied to confession of sins and readiness for the coming kingdom. To accept it, a Jew was admitting that being born into Israel was not enough. The Jordan is the river Israel crossed into the promised land (Josh 3). John calls the nation back to the starting line.
- **"Many of the Pharisees and Sadducees coming to his baptism" (v7).** These two groups rarely agreed. The Pharisees ("separated ones") were a lay movement devoted to careful keeping of the law and its oral traditions. They believed in the resurrection and angels. The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy who ran the temple. They accepted only the five books of Moses as authoritative and denied the resurrection (22:23; Acts 23:8). Matthew links them several times as joint opponents of Jesus (16:1–12). "Coming to his baptism" may mean coming to be baptized or coming to inspect it. John's response suggests he doubted their sincerity.
- **"You brood of vipers! Who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?" (v7).** The image is of snakes fleeing a brush fire in the desert. Jesus uses the same phrase of the Pharisees later (12:34; 23:33). "The wrath to come" is God's coming judgment, the "great and awesome day of the LORD" Malachi spoke of (Mal 4:1, 5).
- **"Bear fruit in keeping with repentance" (v8).** Real repentance shows. It is like a tree, known by its fruit (7:16–20). Going through a ritual without a changed life is not repentance.
- **"Do not presume to say to yourselves, 'We have Abraham as our father'" (v9).** Many believed that being a physical descendant of Abraham guaranteed a place in God's favor. John cuts that away. Jesus says the same (John 8:33–39), and Paul works it out: "not all are children of Abraham because they are his offspring" (Rom 9:7). The true children of Abraham are those who share his faith (Gal 3:7).
- **"God is able from these stones to raise up children for Abraham" (v9).** John may have pointed to the stones of the riverbank. In Hebrew and Aramaic "stones" and "sons" sound alike (Hebrew *avanim* and *banim*), so there may be a wordplay. God once made Adam from dust and Israel from a childless old man. He can make children for Abraham from rocks, and in fact he will soon bring in Gentiles (8:11; 28:19). Isaiah called Abraham "the rock from which you were hewn" (Isa 51:1–2).
- **"Even now the axe is laid to the root of the trees" (v10).** The woodsman has already set his axe against the trunk, ready to swing. Israel was often pictured as a tree or a vineyard (Isa 5:1–7; Jer 11:16). Judgment is not far off. It is imminent. Every fruitless tree "is cut down and thrown into the fire." Jesus repeats this exact line (7:19).
- **"I baptize you with water for repentance, but he who is coming after me is mightier than I" (v11).** John points away from himself. "He who is coming" was a phrase for the Messiah (11:3).
- **"Whose sandals I am not worthy to carry" (v11).** Carrying or removing a master's sandals was a slave's job. Mark, Luke and John say "untie" (Mark 1:7). Matthew says "carry." Both were slave tasks. The point is the same: the greatest prophet is not worthy to be the coming one's lowest servant.
- **"He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (v11).** John immerses in water. The coming one will immerse people in God's Spirit and in fire. The prophets promised that God would pour out his Spirit on his people in the last days (Joel 2:28; Ezek 36:25–27). Fire in the Bible can purify, as gold is refined (Mal 3:2–3; Zech 13:9), or destroy, as in v10 and v12. Interpreters differ. Some think John means one baptism of Spirit-and-fire that purifies believers (fulfilled at Pentecost, when "tongues as of fire" appeared, Acts 2:3). Others think he means two outcomes: the Spirit for the repentant and fire for the unrepentant, which fits the fire in vv10 and 12. Both readings fit the text. John himself may have expected the judgment to come quickly, which is why he later sent asking, "Are you the one who is to come?" (11:2–3).
- **"His winnowing fork is in his hand" (v12).** At harvest, farmers threshed grain on a flat, hard threshing floor, then tossed it into the air with a wooden fork or shovel. The wind blew the light chaff away and the heavy grain fell back. The grain was gathered into the barn. The chaff was swept up and burned. The coming one will sort people the same way, and the fork is already in his hand. "Unquenchable fire" is the language of final judgment (Isa 66:24; Mark 9:48).
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that repentance is an action, a change of direction, not merely a feeling, and sees no difference in meaning between "kingdom of heaven" and "kingdom of God." He notes that proselyte baptism was for Gentiles, so Jews submitting to John's baptism were humbling themselves greatly. Henry calls repentance "a total alteration in the mind," and describes the Sadducees as "the scornful infidels of that time and country." Henry also warns that "No outward forms can make us clean." JFB calls descent from Abraham "that pillow on which the nation so fatally reposed," sees the stones as hinting at the calling of the Gentiles, and takes the fire in v11 as "the fiery character of the Spirit's operations upon the soul," not a separate baptism of judgment. On this JFB and the Tyndale notes, which allow both purification and judgment, differ somewhat.

#### The Baptism of Jesus (vv13–17)
- **"Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to John, to be baptized by him" (v13).** Jesus walks about 60–70 miles from Nazareth. He comes on purpose, "to be baptized." He is about thirty (Luke 3:23).
- **"I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?" (v14).** Only Matthew records this exchange. John's baptism was for repentance and confession of sins (v6, v11). Jesus had no sins to confess (2 Cor 5:21; Heb 4:15; 1 Pet 2:22). John, who had just said he was unworthy to carry Jesus' sandals, tries to stop him. "Would have prevented" is a continuous tense: he kept trying to prevent him.
- **"Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness" (v15).** These are Jesus' first words in Matthew. "Now" suggests that the normal order (lesser baptized by greater) is suspended for this moment. "All righteousness" in Matthew means doing everything God requires (5:20; 6:33). Why would the sinless one need this? Several answers fit together. Jesus identifies with the people he came to save, standing in line with sinners in the river. He submits to a baptism God had sent John to give (21:25), obeying all of God's will. And he begins the work of the Servant of the LORD, who would be "numbered with the transgressors" and bear "the sin of many" (Isa 53:12). His baptism looks ahead to the "baptism" of his death (Luke 12:50; Mark 10:38). "Us" includes John: both have parts to play in God's plan.
- **"He went up from the water, and behold, the heavens were opened to him" (v16).** Isaiah had prayed, "Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down" (Isa 64:1). Now heaven opens. Ezekiel saw visions when "the heavens were opened" (Ezek 1:1).
- **"He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming to rest on him" (v16).** The Spirit came "like a dove," in bodily form (Luke 3:22). At creation the Spirit hovered over the waters (Gen 1:2). After the flood a dove brought Noah the sign of a new world (Gen 8:8–12). The dove was also a symbol of gentleness (10:16). Isaiah had promised: "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" (Isa 11:2), and "I have put my Spirit upon him" (Isa 42:1). This is Jesus' anointing for his ministry. "Christ" means "anointed one," and here he is anointed, not with oil but with the Spirit himself (Acts 10:38).
- **"A voice from heaven said, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased'" (v17).** Three Old Testament passages seem to be woven into the Father's words. "You are my Son" is from Psalm 2:7, where God speaks to his anointed King. "Beloved son" echoes God's words to Abraham about Isaac, "your son, your only son… whom you love" (Gen 22:2), the son offered on the mountain. "In whom… well pleased" echoes Isaiah 42:1, "my chosen, in whom my soul delights," the Servant who will suffer for others. So the Father announces Jesus as the King, the beloved Son who will be offered, and the suffering Servant, all at once. Mark and Luke have "You are my beloved Son," spoken to Jesus. Matthew has "This is," spoken to the onlookers. Both are true: it was a word to Jesus and a witness to others.
- **"Well pleased."** Jesus has not yet preached a sermon or done a miracle. The Father's pleasure rests on the Son for who he is and for thirty quiet years of faithfulness in Nazareth.
- **The Trinity revealed.** The Son stands in the water. The Spirit descends upon him. The Father speaks from heaven. The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible, but scenes like this are why Christians confess one God in three persons. Matthew's Gospel ends with the command to baptize "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (28:19). Christian baptism begins at Jesus' own baptism.
- **The new Israel.** Chapter 2 had Jesus come "out of Egypt" as God's son (2:15). Now he passes through the water and is declared God's Son. Next he goes into the wilderness for forty days (4:1–2). It is Israel's story again: Egypt, the Red Sea, the wilderness. Paul calls Israel's crossing of the sea a baptism (1 Cor 10:1–2). Where Israel failed in the wilderness, the true Son will not.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says Jesus was baptized not to confess sin but to identify with sinners, and sees the Trinity shown together at the Jordan. Henry says Jesus chose "to fulfil all righteousness," honoring God's appointed ordinances, and that the Spirit's coming showed Christ was "endued with his sacred influences without measure." JFB paraphrases "Suffer it to be so now" as "Let it pass for the present," and says that by his baptism, like his circumcision, Jesus pledged himself to fulfil the whole law. JFB reads the dove as a sign of "chaste purity" and "meekness, gentleness," and hears in the Father's words "absolute complacency, once and for ever felt" toward the Son.

#### Pointing to Christ
John the Baptist is the last great voice of the Old Testament, calling Israel to repent because God himself is coming. Then Jesus walks into the water. The one who will baptize with the Spirit and fire stands in the river with sinners, though he has no sin of his own. He takes his place with the guilty from the start, and at the end he will take their judgment on the cross. Heaven opens, the Spirit rests on him, and the Father declares the verdict over his life: this is my beloved Son. For all who are joined to him, the Father's pleasure rests on them too.
