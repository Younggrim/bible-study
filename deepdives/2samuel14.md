*Setting:* Jerusalem, around 980 BC. Five years have passed since Amnon raped Tamar: two years while Absalom waited for revenge and three years since he killed Amnon and fled to his grandfather's court at Geshur (13:23, 38). David has stopped mourning Amnon, and his heart turns toward the son in exile (13:39), but he does nothing to bring him back or to deal with the crime. Absalom is now probably the eldest surviving son. Chileab, the second son, is never heard of after 3:3. So the question of Absalom is also the question of who will be king next. Joab, David's hard-headed army commander, decides to force the issue. The chapter is full of wise words, half-truths and a reconciliation that is only skin-deep. It sets the stage for Absalom's rebellion.

#### Absalom Returns to Jerusalem (vv1–33)
- **"Joab… knew that the king's heart went out to Absalom" (v1).** The Hebrew says the king's heart was "on" (*ʿal*) Absalom. That can mean "toward" him, as the ESV takes it, or "against" him, and some scholars read it that way, as David still holding his crime against him. The second half of the chapter, where David refuses to see him, fits either. What is clear is that David is stuck, and Joab sees it.
- **"Joab sent to Tekoa and brought from there a wise woman" (v2).** Tekoa was a village in the hills about 10 miles south of Jerusalem, later the home of the prophet Amos (Amos 1:1). "Wise woman" may mean more than a clever person. It may have been a recognized role, someone known for speaking well and settling disputes. Another wise woman saves a city in 20:16–22. Coming from a small town, she would not be recognized at court.
- **"Pretend to be a mourner… So Joab put the words in her mouth" (vv2–3).** The plan is a staged legal case, like Nathan's parable in chapter 12. But where Nathan's story brought David to repentance, this one is designed to get a ruling the king can then be held to. It is a parable used for politics.
- **"Your servant had two sons, and they quarreled… one struck the other and killed him" (v6).** The story echoes Cain and Abel: two brothers "in the field," and one kills the other (Gen 4:8). It also mirrors David's family, though not exactly: Absalom's killing was planned for two years, not a sudden fight.
- **"Give up the man who struck his brother, that we may put him to death" (v7).** The law named a near relative as the "avenger of blood" (*gōʾēl haddām*) to execute a murderer (Num 35:19–21). In her story the family insists on that right. But killing the last son would end her husband's line and lose his share of the land, the "heritage of God" (v16; compare Num 27:1–11). **"They would quench my coal that is left"** is a vivid picture: one glowing coal saved in the ashes to start tomorrow's fire, her only hope for the family's future.
- **"On me be the guilt, my lord the king… let the king and his throne be guiltless" (v9).** Bending the law to spare a killer would put guilt on someone. She offers to carry it herself so the king can rule in her favor with a clear conscience.
- **"As the LORD lives, not one hair of your son shall fall to the ground" (v11).** She pushes David step by step: first a vague promise (v8), then protection (v10), then an oath in the LORD's name. That oath is what Joab wanted. Ironically, Absalom's hair will play a part in his death (18:9).
- **"In giving this decision the king convicts himself" (v13).** Now the trap closes, just as it did with Nathan. If the king will spare a stranger's son, why not his own? She frames it as a matter for the whole nation, "the people of God," since the heir to the throne is in exile.
- **"We must all die; we are like water spilled on the ground, which cannot be gathered up again" (v14).** Life is short, and Amnon cannot be brought back. Holding a grudge will not restore him.
- **"But God will not take away life, and he devises means so that the banished one will not remain an outcast" (v14).** This is the most famous line in the chapter. She appeals to God's own character: he does not simply cast sinners away but makes a way for them to come home. God had done just that for David himself, sparing his life after his sin (12:13). It is a true and beautiful statement about God. But in her mouth it is used to skip over justice for an unrepentant murderer, and that is the difference between her argument and the gospel. God brings the banished home without ignoring justice. He satisfies it.
- **"My lord the king is like the angel of God to discern good and evil" (v17).** High flattery, repeated in v20. This phrase is used only of David in the Bible (also 19:27; 1 Sam 29:9). It means he has God-given wisdom to judge. The irony is that he has just been outwitted.
- **"Is the hand of Joab with you in all this?" (v19).** David sees through it. She admits it at once: "it was he who put all these words in the mouth of your servant." Joab's purpose, she says, was "to change the course of things" (v20), to break the deadlock.
- **"Go, bring back the young man Absalom" (v21).** David grants the request. **Joab** bows and thanks him as if he had received a personal favor (v22). He has his own reasons. He may see Absalom as the coming king and want to be on good terms with him, or he may simply want to avoid a civil war over the succession.
- **"Let him dwell apart in his own house; he is not to come into my presence" (v24).** So Absalom comes home, but not to his father. He is neither punished nor forgiven. This half-measure is perhaps the worst choice of all. It leaves the crime unaddressed and the relationship broken, and it gives Absalom two years to nurse a grievance.
- **"No one so much to be praised for his handsome appearance as Absalom… there was no blemish in him" (v25).** The narrator pauses to describe him, as Saul was described as tall and handsome (1 Sam 9:2). The reader has learned to be wary of this. God told Samuel, "Do not look on his appearance… the LORD looks on the heart" (1 Sam 16:7). "No blemish" is the language used of sacrificial animals (Lev 22:21). Outwardly perfect, inwardly ruthless.
- **"He weighed the hair of his head, two hundred shekels by the king's weight" (v26).** Roughly 5 pounds (about 2.3 kg), though the "king's weight" may have differed from the ordinary shekel. It is an exaggerated, admiring detail, and it sets up his death, when his head is caught in the branches of an oak (18:9).
- **"Three sons, and one daughter whose name was Tamar" (v27).** He named his daughter after his wronged sister, which shows real feeling for her. The sons are never named. Later Absalom sets up a monument because "I have no son to keep my name in remembrance" (18:18), so they probably died young. The Greek Old Testament adds that this Tamar became the wife of Rehoboam, Solomon's son, and the mother of Abijah. 1 Kings 15:2 does say Abijah's mother was "Maacah the daughter of Abishalom," probably a granddaughter of Absalom through Tamar.
- **"Absalom lived two full years in Jerusalem, without coming into the king's presence" (v28).** "Two full years" is the same phrase as 13:23, when Absalom waited to kill Amnon. Waiting does not soften him. It hardens him.
- **"Joab's field is next to mine, and he has barley there; go and set it on fire" (v30).** Joab ignores two summonses, perhaps now wary of being linked to Absalom. So Absalom burns his barley. Samson did something similar to the Philistines (Judg 15:4–5). It works. Absalom gets what he wants by force, which is how he will try to get the kingdom.
- **"If there is guilt in me, let him put me to death" (v32).** This is not a confession. It is a dare. Absalom thinks he did nothing wrong, and he knows his father will not execute him.
- **"He… bowed himself on his face to the ground before the king, and the king kissed Absalom" (v33).** Compare the father in Jesus' parable who "ran and embraced him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20), or Joseph weeping on his brothers' necks (Gen 45:14–15). Here there is no running, no tears and no words from either one. The son bows, the father kisses, and the narrator records nothing else. It looks like reconciliation. The next chapter shows it was not.
- **Commentators:** All three see David failing as both king and father. JFB says "the feelings of the father triumphed over the duty of the king," since the law did not allow a king to pardon murder (Num 35:30–31). It also suggests the woman was brought from a distance so her story could not easily be checked. Matthew Henry adds that "God's laws were never designed to be like cobwebs, which catch the little flies, but suffer the great ones to break through." On v14 Henry and Guzik both see the gospel shining through the woman's words. Henry: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself." Guzik adds the key qualification, that God brings us back by satisfying justice, not by setting it aside. Guzik argues that David swung from being too soft on Amnon to being too harsh on Absalom, and that refusing to see him for two years bred bitterness. JFB, by contrast, thinks the two years of exclusion showed "great prudence," meant to lead Absalom to repentance. Henry calls Absalom's beauty "a poor commendation for a man that had nothing else in him valuable." He thinks Joab acted partly out of self-interest, since he too had shed blood and needed the king to be lenient. Guzik sets Absalom beside the prodigal son: the prodigal came home humble, and Absalom came home burning fields.

#### Pointing to Christ
The wise woman spoke better than she knew: God "devises means so that the banished one will not remain an outcast" (v14). David brought his son home without dealing with the wrong, and the result was a kiss without peace. God does it differently. He does not ignore our sin. He deals with it in his Son, "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Rom 3:26). In the gospel the Father does run to the returning child (Luke 15:20), because the price of the homecoming has been paid. Absalom bowed without repenting. The banished who come to God through Christ come home confessing, and they are welcomed fully, not left to "dwell apart."
