*Setting:* The plains of Moab, at the end of the wilderness years. The festival calendar of chapters 28–29 ended with a reminder that, besides the required public offerings, Israelites would bring "vow offerings and… freewill offerings" (29:39). That raises a practical question, and this chapter answers it: how binding is a vow, and who can release one? It is a short, careful piece of case law, set in a household where a father or husband carried legal and financial responsibility for its members. (Hebrew Bibles number these verses one higher, because they count 29:40 as 30:1.) Its main point is simple: words spoken to God matter.

#### Men and Vows (vv1–2)
- **"Moses spoke to the heads of the tribes" (v1).** Most laws in Numbers are given to "the people of Israel." This one is addressed to the tribal leaders, perhaps because they would be the ones to judge disputes about vows.
- **"If a man vows a vow to the LORD, or swears an oath to bind himself by a pledge" (v2).** Two kinds of promise are in view. A **vow** (*neder*) was usually a promise to give God something, often in return for help: Jacob's vow at Bethel (Gen 28:20–22), Hannah's promise of her son (1 Sam 1:11). A **pledge** (*issar*, a word found only in this chapter) was a binding obligation to hold oneself back from something, like the Nazirite's vow (ch 6) or a fast (v13). Both were voluntary. No one had to make a vow (Deut 23:22). But once made, it bound.
- **"He shall not break his word" (v2).** Literally "he shall not profane his word," the same verb used for profaning something holy. A promise made to God is a holy thing. **"He shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth."** Ecclesiastes says the same: "When you vow a vow to God, do not delay paying it… It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay" (Eccl 5:4–5; also Deut 23:21–23; Ps 15:4, the one who "swears to his own hurt and does not change").
- **The danger of rash vows.** The Old Testament shows what happens when people vow carelessly. Jephthah vowed to sacrifice whatever came out of his house first, and it was his daughter (Judg 11:30–40). Saul's hasty oath nearly cost Jonathan his life (1 Sam 14:24–45). Leviticus 5:4–6 provides a sin offering for someone who "utters with his lips a rash oath." The law took vows seriously so that people would think before they made them.
- **What about Jesus' teaching?** In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus says, "Do not take an oath at all… Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'" (Matt 5:33–37; James 5:12). He was not overturning this law but exposing how people used oaths to dodge it, swearing by heaven or the temple so they could later claim the oath didn't count (Matt 23:16–22). He also condemned using a vow ("Corban," "given to God") to escape caring for one's parents (Mark 7:9–13). Christians differ on what this means for oaths today. Some, such as Quakers and Anabaptists, refuse all oaths. Most Christian traditions allow solemn oaths, in court or at a wedding, noting that God himself swears (Heb 6:13–18) and Paul calls God as witness (2 Cor 1:23). All agree on Jesus' point: a believer's ordinary word should be as reliable as an oath.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline gives the chapter's general rule "that all vows must be carefully performed." JFB notes that a vow "had to be actually expressed in words"; a private intention was not a vow. Guzik says breaking a vow made to God is sin, even if people treat it lightly, and reads Jesus' words in Matthew 5 not as banning every oath but as calling for honesty so complete that oaths become unnecessary. He adds that Jesus answered under oath at his trial (Matt 26:63–64).

#### Women and Vows (vv3–16)
- **"While within her father's house in her youth" (v3).** The law now covers a young unmarried woman living at home. In that world her father was responsible for her, including financially. If she vowed to give an offering or to go without something, it might cost the household.
- **"Her father hears… and says nothing" (v4).** Silence means consent. If he does not object, her vows stand.
- **"But if her father opposes her on the day that he hears of it" (v5).** He can cancel the vow, but only on the day he hears of it. He cannot wait to see how things go and then cancel it later. **"And the LORD will forgive her."** The verb (*salach*) means to pardon. She is released from the obligation, and God does not hold the broken vow against her. The law protects her: she will not be guilty before God for a vow someone else had the right to cancel.
- **"If she marries… while under her vows or any thoughtless utterance of her lips" (v6).** "Thoughtless utterance" (*mibta*) means a rash or careless word, a term found only here and in v8. A woman might come into a marriage carrying a vow she made as a girl. Her new husband has the same one-day right to accept or cancel it (vv7–8).
- **"Any vow of a widow or of a divorced woman… shall stand against her" (v9).** A woman with no husband answered for herself, and her vows were as binding as a man's. This shows that the rules for daughters and wives were about household responsibility, not about a woman's word being worth less. A woman standing on her own was treated exactly as a man was.
- **"If she vowed in her husband's house" (vv10–12).** The same rule for a vow made during marriage: silence confirms it, objection on the day cancels it, and **"the LORD will forgive her."**
- **"Any vow and any binding oath to afflict herself" (v13).** "Afflict herself" is the same phrase used of the Day of Atonement (29:7) and usually means fasting or other self-denial. Such a vow affected the life of the home, so the husband could confirm it or cancel it.
- **"If her husband says nothing to her from day to day, then he establishes all her vows" (v14).** Silence is a decision. Saying nothing is saying yes.
- **"If he makes them null and void after he has heard of them, then he shall bear her iniquity" (v15).** If the husband lets the day pass and later forces her to break her vow, the guilt is his, not hers. Authority carried accountability. A husband could not use his position to play games with what his wife had promised to God.
- **Hannah as an example.** Hannah vowed that if God gave her a son, she would give him to the LORD for life (1 Sam 1:11). Her husband Elkanah heard and confirmed it: "Do what seems best to you… only, may the LORD establish his word" (1 Sam 1:23). It is this law at work in a real family.
- **"These are the statutes… about a man and his wife and about a father and his daughter" (v16).** The closing line sums up the setting. These rules belong to a household where the head of the family was responsible for its members. Modern readers notice that the law gives men authority over women's vows and not the reverse. In its own world, it also protected women: it gave a clear, limited rule, released them from guilt when a vow was cancelled, and placed the blame for any wrongful cancellation on the man.
- **Commentators:** Henry's outline puts it plainly: daughters' vows are not binding "unless allowed by the father," and wives' vows likewise "unless allowed by the husband." JFB says husbands were "allowed only a day for deliberation," and if they later hindered a vow, "the sin of non-performance was to be imputed to him and not to her." Guzik draws a principle from v15: God never gives authority without accountability, and seeing that makes submission easier. The Tyndale notes add that the father or husband was the "financial partner" in the vow, and that no one, male or female, should make a vow to God and then neglect it.

#### Pointing to Christ
"He shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth." Human promises fail, and many vows are spoken in haste. God's never are: "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Cor 1:20). Jesus calls his people to be truthful through and through, so that a plain "yes" can be trusted. And this chapter has a quiet picture of grace: a woman's failed vow released, the guilt carried by another. "He shall bear her iniquity" (v15). Isaiah uses similar language of the Servant who "shall bear their iniquities" (Isa 53:11). Every broken promise to God by his people has been carried by Christ, the faithful husband of his bride (Eph 5:25), who kept every word he spoke.
