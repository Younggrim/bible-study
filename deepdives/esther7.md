*Setting:* The same day as chapter 6, a day that began with Haman coming to ask for Mordecai's death and ended with him leading Mordecai through the city on the king's horse. Haman's own wife and advisers have just told him, "you will surely fall before him" (6:13), and before he can collect himself he is hurried to Esther's second banquet (6:14). The king still does not know that his queen is a Jew, or that the decree he sealed has sentenced her to death. This short chapter is the climax of the book. In a single conversation over wine, the hidden is revealed, the accuser is exposed, and the stake Haman built becomes his own.

#### Esther Reveals Haman's Plot (vv1–6)
- **"The king and Haman went in to feast with Queen Esther" (v1).** The Hebrew says they came "to drink" with her. The ESV's heading for this scene begins at 6:14, since the summons to the banquet starts it.
- **"On the second day, as they were drinking wine" (v2).** For the third time the king asks, word for word, "What is your wish?… Even to the half of my kingdom" (5:3, 6). Esther has made him wait, and now he is ready to hear anything.
- **"Let my life be granted me for my wish, and my people for my request" (v3).** The two words "wish" and "request" are the king's own. Esther fits her answer exactly to them. She begins with herself: not a policy question, but "my life." The king who loves her must now realize that someone has threatened her. Only then does she add "my people." For the first time in the book Esther openly claims her people (compare 2:10, 20).
- **"We have been sold, I and my people" (v4).** "Sold" points to Haman's 10,000 talents (3:9; 4:7). Esther does not mention the king's part in the sale, though he was the one who took the ring off his hand. **"To be destroyed, to be killed, and to be annihilated."** These are the exact three verbs of the decree (3:13). She is quoting the document Mordecai sent her (4:8).
- **"If we had been sold merely as slaves… I would have been silent" (v4).** The end of this verse is one of the hardest lines in the book. Literally it says "for the distress (or the enemy) is not equal to the damage of the king." The word for "damage," *nezeq*, appears only here in the Old Testament, a loanword from Aramaic. The key word *tsar* can mean either "distress" or "adversary." The ESV takes it as "our affliction is not to be compared with the loss to the king": slavery would not have been worth troubling him about. The KJV takes it as "the enemy could not countervail the king's damage": nothing Haman pays could make up for the loss of a whole people. Both readings have support. Either way, Esther speaks humbly and frames her plea in terms of the king's interest, which is exactly what Haman did (3:8).
- **"Who is he, and where is he, who has dared to do this?" (v5).** The Hebrew is abrupt and repetitive, like a man stammering in shock. The irony is enormous: the answer to "who has dared" includes the king himself, who sealed the decree without asking who the people were. But Esther does not say that.
- **"A foe and enemy! This wicked Haman!" (v6).** Esther finally points. "Foe" is *tsar*, the same word as in v4. She has saved the name for last, like a prosecutor. The title "enemy of the Jews" given to Haman by the narrator (3:10) is now spoken aloud before the king.
- **"Haman was terrified before the king and the queen" (v6).** The verb means sudden, overwhelming fear. He had no idea the queen was a Jew. Now the woman whose banquet he boasted about (5:12) is the one accusing him.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that Esther, like Haman in chapter 3, does not at first name the people involved, and that her strategy of inviting Haman to the banquets now pays off: he is caught face to face. JFB explains v4 as Esther saying that slavery could at least have enriched the treasury and been reversed, but slaughter was "an irreparable evil," which follows the KJV reading of the hard phrase. Henry says Haman had "basely drawn the king in to be *particeps criminis*," a partner in his crime, without the king knowing, and that "it was time for him to fear when the queen was his prosecutor, the king his judge, and his own conscience a witness against him."

#### Haman Is Hanged (vv7–10)
- **"The king arose in his wrath… and went into the palace garden" (v7).** Ahasuerus is caught. He cannot condemn Haman without admitting his own part, and the law he sealed cannot be undone. He goes out, perhaps to cool down, perhaps to think. JFB says that when a Persian king rose in anger from a feast, it was as good as a sentence of death.
- **"Haman stayed to beg for his life from Queen Esther" (v7).** The man who would not tolerate one Jew's refusal to bow now begs a Jewish woman for mercy. He "saw that harm was determined against him," a phrase that means the king had made up his mind.
- **"Haman was falling on the couch where Esther was" (v8).** Guests reclined on couches to eat (compare 1:6, where the same word, *mittah*, is used for the gold and silver couches). In desperation Haman throws himself down at Esther's feet, onto her couch. Assyrian palace rules from earlier centuries forbade men at court to come near the king's women at all. The verb "falling" (*naphal*) is the same word Zeresh used: "you will surely fall before him" (6:13). Haman is literally falling.
- **"Will he even assault the queen in my presence, in my own house?" (v8).** The king surely does not believe Haman meant to attack Esther. But his fury finally has a target he can name without blaming himself. Haman's guilt is now beyond appeal. It is an irony the narrator enjoys: Haman is condemned for a crime he did not commit, in place of the one he did.
- **"They covered Haman's face" (v8).** Attendants covered the face of a condemned man, a sign he would never again look on the king. Greek and Roman writers record the same custom. Haman went home that morning with his head covered in shame (6:12); now his face is covered for death.
- **Harbona (v9).** One of the seven eunuchs who carried the king's summons to Vashti (1:10). He may also have been one of those who fetched Haman that evening and saw the stake standing in his yard. He speaks up at just the right moment, and with a pointed phrase: Mordecai, **"whose word saved the king."** The man Haman meant to kill had saved the king's life. Courtiers who had bowed to Haman yesterday are glad to see him go today.
- **"Hang him on that" (v9).** Just two words in Hebrew, the king's shortest command in the book. The stake fifty cubits high, built for Mordecai, now bears Haman. **"So they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai"** (v10). The Psalms say it again and again: "He makes a pit, digging it out, and falls into the hole that he has made" (Ps 7:15–16; compare Ps 9:15–16; Prov 26:27).
- **"Then the wrath of the king abated" (v10).** The same phrase was used when the king calmed down after deposing Vashti (2:1). Ahasuerus' anger rises and falls, and things change for others when it does. But note what has not changed: Haman is dead, but his decree is still law. The Jews are still sentenced to die on the thirteenth of Adar. The next chapters deal with that.
- **Commentators:** Henry pictures the king pacing the garden, "vexed at himself, that he should be such a fool as to doom a guiltless nation to destruction," and "ashamed to consult" his seven counselors about undoing what he had done without them. Of Haman begging, Henry says: "Those that are most haughty… when they are in power and prosperity, are commonly the most abject… when the wheel turns upon them." He adds that the king knew Haman meant no assault, but took the chance "to vent his passion." JFB describes covering the face as the first step when a Persian criminal was led to execution, and guesses Harbona saw the stake while fetching Haman. Guzik sees the stake as an early forerunner of crucifixion and draws a contrast: here a guilty man dies and the king's wrath is satisfied, while at the cross the innocent died for the guilty. He also mentions a Jewish legend that the angel Gabriel pushed Haman onto the couch, which he plainly treats as legend.

#### Pointing to Christ
Haman built a tree to destroy God's servant and died on it himself. Something deeper happened on another tree. The powers of sin and death put Jesus on a cross to be rid of him, and in that very act they were undone (Col 2:14–15; 1 Cor 2:8). And where Haman's death only calmed a king's temper, Christ's death truly turned away the wrath of God, not as a guilty man paying for his own crime but as "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God" (1 Pet 3:18). Like Esther, Christ identified himself with a people under sentence of death, and pleaded for them before the King.
