*Setting:* Jerusalem, Friday of Passover week, around AD 30, from dawn to sunset. During the night the Jewish council condemned Jesus for blasphemy after he said he would be seen "seated at the right hand of Power" (14:62–64), and Peter denied him three times in the courtyard below (14:66–72). The council could not carry out an execution on its own authority, so now they hand him to the Roman governor, Pontius Pilate. Mark tells the day in three-hour steps (vv1, 25, 33, 34, 42), and he tells it with stark restraint. He describes no wounds and dwells on no pain. He lets the mockery, the silence and the cry from the cross speak for themselves, and he shows, scene by scene, that the man dying as "King of the Jews" truly is the King and the Son of God.

#### Jesus Delivered to Pilate (vv1–5)
- **"As soon as it was morning… the whole council" (v1).** Roman officials held court at first light. The council met again at dawn, either a second session to make the night's verdict formal or the end of the same one. "Delivered him over" is the word of all three passion predictions (9:31; 10:33). Step by step he is handed on: by Judas to the council, by the council to Pilate, by Pilate to the soldiers (v15).
- **Pontius Pilate.** Governor (prefect) of Judea from AD 26 to 36. A stone found at Caesarea in 1961 bears his name and title. He normally lived at Caesarea on the coast and came to Jerusalem at the feasts with extra troops. Josephus and Philo describe him as stubborn, harsh and given to provoking the Jews, but also as a man who backed down when crowds pressed him.
- **"Are you the King of the Jews?" (v2).** The council's charge was blasphemy, but Pilate would not execute a man over a Jewish religious dispute. So the charge is put in political terms. "King of the Jews" was a Gentile way of saying "Messiah," and to Rome it meant a rival to Caesar. The title appears six times in this chapter (vv2, 9, 12, 18, 26, 32), more than anywhere else in Mark.
- **"You have said so" (v2).** Neither a plain yes nor a no. Jesus accepts the title but not Pilate's meaning of it. He is a king, but not the kind Rome feared (John 18:36).
- **"Jesus made no further answer, so that Pilate was amazed" (vv3–5).** Prisoners on trial for their lives argued, begged or bargained. Jesus says nothing, as Isaiah had foretold: "like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7).
- **Commentators:** Guzik points out that the leaders turned a religious charge into a political one because Pilate would care about a rival king. He reads "You have said so" as a qualified answer that admitted the title without conceding the charge of treason. The Tyndale notes add that Pilate's wavering fits what other ancient sources say of him.

#### Pilate Delivers Jesus to Be Crucified (vv6–15)
- **"At the feast he used to release for them one prisoner" (v6).** This Passover custom is known only from the Gospels, but all four mention it, and releasing prisoners at festivals is known elsewhere in the ancient world.
- **"Barabbas… among the rebels… who had committed murder in the insurrection" (v7).** Mark speaks of "the insurrection" as if his readers knew of it, but no record of it survives. Barabbas was the kind of man Rome crucified: a violent rebel. His name is Aramaic for "son of the father." Some early manuscripts of Matthew 27:16–17 give his full name as "Jesus Barabbas," which would make Pilate's choice even sharper: Jesus son of the father, or Jesus the Son of the Father.
- **"Out of envy" (v10).** Pilate saw through the leaders. Jesus' popularity threatened their position.
- **"The chief priests stirred up the crowd" (v11).** This crowd had "come up" to ask for the customary release (v8). It was probably a different group from the pilgrims who welcomed Jesus on Sunday, and the hour was early. Whoever they were, the leaders worked on them well.
- **"Crucify him" (vv13–14).** The crowd chooses the man of violence and demands the cross for the innocent one. **"Why? What evil has he done?"** Pilate's question is never answered. All four Gospels make clear that the Roman judge found no guilt in him (Luke 23:4, 14–15, 22; John 18:38; 19:4, 6).
- **"Wishing to satisfy the crowd" (v15).** Pilate knows the truth but chooses safety.
- **"Having scourged Jesus" (v15).** Roman flogging, done with a whip of leather straps weighted with bits of bone or metal, was a usual prelude to crucifixion. It tore the back to the bone, and some victims died under it. It helps explain why Jesus could not carry his cross far and why he died within hours.
- **The great exchange.** Barabbas, guilty, goes free. Jesus, innocent, takes the death meant for a rebel and murderer. Christians have long seen in this a picture of what the cross does for everyone (1 Pet 3:18).
- **Commentators:** Guzik draws out the meaning of "Barabbas," "son of the father," and the irony that he goes free because Jesus takes his place. He also gives a careful description of Roman scourging. The Tyndale notes add that Pilate's willingness to release Barabbas suggests the man had not killed Roman citizens or soldiers.

#### Jesus Is Mocked (vv16–20)
- **"Inside the palace (that is, the governor's headquarters)" (v16).** The *praetorium* was wherever the governor held court, probably Herod's palace on the city's west side, though some place it at the Antonia fortress beside the temple. "The whole battalion" is a cohort, nominally 600 men. It probably means all the soldiers on duty there.
- **"A purple cloak… a crown of thorns" (v17).** A mock coronation. Real purple dye was rare and costly, worn by kings. The cloak was likely a faded red soldier's cape. The crown may have imitated the radiant crowns on emperors' coins, with thorns pointing outward like rays, though it surely also wounded.
- **"Hail, King of the Jews!" (v18).** A parody of the soldiers' salute to the emperor, "Hail, Caesar!"
- **"Striking his head with a reed and spitting on him and kneeling down in homage" (v19).** The reed was the mock scepter (Matt 27:29). The verbs are in a tense of repeated action: they kept striking and kept spitting. Jesus had foretold exactly this (10:34). Isaiah's servant says, "I gave my back to those who strike… I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting" (Isa 50:6).
- **The irony.** The soldiers mean it as a joke. Mark means the reader to see that every gesture is true. He is the King, and one day every knee will bow to him in earnest (Phil 2:10–11).
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the parody of "Hail, Caesar" and that the verb for spitting means they kept spitting on him. The Tyndale notes suggest the robe may have been a soldier's old cloak.

#### The Crucifixion (vv21–32)
- **"Simon of Cyrene, who was coming in from the country" (v21).** Cyrene was a city in North Africa (modern Libya) with a large Jewish community (Acts 2:10; 6:9). Simon was probably a Jewish pilgrim, or a Cyrenian Jew who had settled near Jerusalem. "Compelled" is a technical word for Roman soldiers forcing civilians into service (Matt 5:41). Condemned men usually carried the crossbeam, the *patibulum*, to the place of execution, where the upright post stood waiting. After the scourging, Jesus apparently could not.
- **"The father of Alexander and Rufus" (v21).** Matthew and Luke drop these names. Mark includes them because his readers knew them. A Rufus greeted by Paul in Rome, "chosen in the Lord, also his mother, who has been a mother to me as well" (Rom 16:13), may be the same man, which would fit Mark writing for Rome. If so, the man pressed into carrying the cross became the father of a believing family.
- **"Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull)" (v22).** Aramaic *gulgoltha*, "skull." The Latin is *calvaria*, which gives "Calvary." It may have been a rounded rise or an old quarry, named for its shape or for executions. It was outside the city wall (John 19:20; Heb 13:12), near a road where passersby could see (v29). The Church of the Holy Sepulchre marks the traditional site, which was outside the wall in AD 30 and has strong early support.
- **"Wine mixed with myrrh, but he did not take it" (v23).** Proverbs 31:6 says, "Give strong drink to the one who is perishing," and a Jewish tradition says the women of Jerusalem offered a drugged drink to the condemned. Myrrh may have dulled pain. Jesus refuses. He will drink the cup the Father gives him with a clear mind (14:25, 36).
- **"They crucified him" (v24).** Mark says only three words in Greek. Every reader knew what crucifixion was: the Romans' most shameful punishment, reserved for slaves, rebels and the lowest criminals. Victims were stripped, fastened with nails or ropes and left to die slowly, often of exhaustion and suffocation, sometimes over days. Cicero called it "the most cruel and disgusting penalty." The bones of a crucified man named Yehohanan, found in Jerusalem in 1968, still had an iron nail through the heel.
- **"Divided his garments among them, casting lots" (v24).** The executioners' perk. Mark does not say "it was fulfilled," but the words echo Psalm 22:18 exactly: "they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." The chapter is full of Psalm 22.
- **"It was the third hour" (v25).** About 9 a.m. John says Pilate was still judging Jesus "about the sixth hour" (John 19:14). The usual explanations: people told time loosely by three-hour blocks, so mid-morning could be called either the third or sixth hour; or John uses a different way of counting hours. Either way, Mark's timing structures his account, with darkness at the sixth hour and death at the ninth.
- **"The King of the Jews" (v26).** The *titulus*, a board giving the crime, was carried before the condemned or hung around his neck and then fixed to the cross. Pilate means it as an insult to the Jews and a warning to rebels. It is the truth.
- **"Two robbers, one on his right and one on his left" (v27).** "Robbers" (*lēstai*) can mean bandits or rebels, men like Barabbas. James and John had asked to sit at his right and left in his glory (10:37). Mark wants readers to see who actually took those places. Some later manuscripts add v28: "And the Scripture was fulfilled that says, 'He was numbered with the transgressors'" (Isa 53:12). It is missing from the earliest manuscripts and was probably added from Luke 22:37, so the ESV leaves it out.
- **"Those who passed by derided him, wagging their heads" (v29).** Again Psalm 22: "All who see me mock me… they wag their heads" (Ps 22:7). They throw back the false charge from the trial: "You who would destroy the temple" (14:58).
- **"He saved others; he cannot save himself" (v31).** The truest thing anyone says that day, though meant as a sneer. He could have saved himself. He would not, because he was saving others. "Come down… that we may see and believe" (v32): they had seen many signs and not believed. The greatest sign, the empty tomb, is three days away.
- **"Those who were crucified with him also reviled him" (v32).** Everyone turns on him: the crowd, the leaders, even the dying men beside him. Luke adds that one of the two later repented (Luke 23:39–43).
- **Commentators:** Guzik suggests the Rufus of Romans 16:13 was Simon's son, which the Tyndale notes also allow. He holds that Jesus refused the drugged wine to keep his mind clear to bear the cross. He compares the Holy Sepulchre and Gordon's Calvary sites and admits uncertainty. On v25 he lists ways of reconciling Mark and John. On v31 he says the leaders' jeer was true: Jesus stayed on the cross precisely because he was saving others. JFB's notes on this chapter of Mark mostly refer readers back to its comments on the other Gospels.

#### The Death of Jesus (vv33–41)
- **"When the sixth hour had come, there was darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour" (v33).** From noon to 3 p.m. Passover fell at full moon, when a solar eclipse is impossible, so this was no eclipse. Amos had warned: "On that day… I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight… I will make it like the mourning for an only son" (Amos 8:9–10). Darkness was also the ninth plague on Egypt before the Passover (Exod 10:21–23). It is a sign of judgment, falling on the one bearing it.
- **"Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?" (v34).** Jesus' only words from the cross in Mark, given in Aramaic and then translated. They are the opening line of Psalm 22: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" It is the only time in the Gospels Jesus addresses God as "God" rather than "Father."
- **What does the cry mean?** Some stress that quoting the first line of a psalm called the whole psalm to mind, and Psalm 22 ends in triumph: "he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him" (22:24). Others stress that the cry is real abandonment. Jesus was bearing sin and its curse (2 Cor 5:21; Gal 3:13), and he experienced the Father's turning away that sin deserves. Both are true. The cry is a real cry of desolation, and it is also a cry of faith: even forsaken, he says "*my* God." The Trinity was not broken apart. But the Son, in his human soul, entered the darkness of judgment for others.
- **"Behold, he is calling Elijah" (v35).** "Eloi" sounds like "Elijah." Many Jews believed Elijah came to help the righteous in trouble. The bystanders mishear the cry or mock it.
- **"Filled a sponge with sour wine" (v36).** Cheap wine vinegar, the soldiers' drink. Psalm 69:21: "for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink." Whether the offer is kindness or mockery, the words "wait, let us see whether Elijah will come" are mocking.
- **"Jesus uttered a loud cry and breathed his last" (v37).** Crucified men usually died slowly, weak and exhausted. A loud cry at the end is unusual. John records the word as "It is finished" (John 19:30), and Luke "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46). Jesus does not fade out. He gives up his life (John 10:18). Mark says simply, "breathed his last" (*exepneusen*).
- **"The curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (v38).** The temple had two great curtains: an outer one at the sanctuary entrance, visible from the courts, and an inner one closing off the Most Holy Place, which only the high priest entered, once a year (Lev 16; Heb 9:7). Either could be meant. "Torn" (*schizō*) is the same rare verb Mark used when the heavens were "torn open" at Jesus' baptism (1:10). Both times, God opens the way. "From top to bottom" means it was God's act. The sign points two ways. The temple's day is over (13:2), and through Jesus' death the way into God's presence is open (Heb 10:19–20).
- **"When the centurion… saw that in this way he breathed his last" (v39).** A Roman officer in charge of about a hundred men, here commanding the execution squad. He had seen many men die. "In this way," the darkness, the cry and the death itself, moves him.
- **"Truly this man was the Son of God!" (v39).** Mark's Gospel opens with "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1). God called him "my beloved Son" at the baptism and on the mountain (1:11; 9:7). Demons knew it (3:11; 5:7). Now, for the first time, a human being says it, and he is a Gentile soldier at the cross. Grammatically the Greek could mean "a son of God," and a Roman might have meant "a divine man." But Mark plainly intends his readers to hear the full confession.
- **"Women looking on from a distance" (vv40–41).** The men have fled. These women stay. Mary Magdalene was from Magdala on the Sea of Galilee, and Jesus had freed her from seven demons (Luke 8:2). "Mary the mother of James the younger and of Joses" is otherwise unknown, unless she is "Mary the wife of Clopas" (John 19:25). Salome may be the mother of James and John (compare Matt 27:56). They had followed and served him in Galilee, supporting him from their own means (Luke 8:3). They see him die (v40), see where he is buried (v47) and will find the tomb empty (16:1–8). They are the eyewitnesses who tie the death, burial and resurrection together.
- **Commentators:** Henry links the darkness to Amos 8:9 and says the cry shows the anguish of a soul "made sin for us." He sees the torn curtain as a warning to unbelief, "Ichabod, The glory is departed," and to believers "a new and living way into the holiest." Guzik notes that a natural eclipse could not happen at full moon. He holds that Jesus truly experienced separation from the Father as a substitute but that the separation was not total, citing 2 Corinthians 5:19. He reads "from top to bottom" as God's own act. The Tyndale notes list each voice that has named Jesus Son of God through the Gospel, ending with this Gentile.

#### Jesus Is Buried (vv42–47)
- **"The day of Preparation, that is, the day before the Sabbath" (v42).** Friday. The Sabbath began at sunset, and the Law said the body of an executed man must not be left hanging overnight (Deut 21:22–23). Romans often left the crucified on crosses to rot as a warning, but in Judea they usually allowed burial. There was little time.
- **"Joseph of Arimathea, a respected member of the council" (v43).** Arimathea was probably Ramathaim, a town northwest of Jerusalem. Joseph was a wealthy council member (Matt 27:57) who had not agreed with the verdict (Luke 23:51) and was a secret disciple (John 19:38). "Looking for the kingdom of God" marks him as a devout Jew who hoped for God's promises, like Simeon and Anna (Luke 2:25, 38).
- **"Took courage and went to Pilate" (v43).** Asking for the body of a man executed for treason meant openly linking himself to him, before Pilate and before his own colleagues. The man who stayed quiet while Jesus lived speaks up when it can only cost him. Nicodemus joins him (John 19:39).
- **"Pilate was surprised to hear that he should have already died" (v44).** Crucified men often lingered for a day or more. Pilate checks with the centurion, whose job was to know. Mark includes this to make the point plainly: Jesus was really dead. This answers in advance the later theory that he only fainted and revived in the tomb.
- **"He granted the corpse to Joseph" (v45).** The word is blunt, *ptōma*, a dead body.
- **"A linen shroud… a tomb that had been cut out of the rock… a stone" (v46).** Wealthy families in Jerusalem had tombs carved into rock, with a ledge or niches inside, and the entrance closed by a large stone, sometimes round and rolled in a groove. Matthew says it was Joseph's own new tomb (Matt 27:60). Isaiah 53:9 says the servant was "with a rich man in his death." The burial was hurried, so the full anointing with spices had to wait until after the Sabbath (16:1).
- **"Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses saw where he was laid" (v47).** They know the right tomb.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees in Joseph a quiet, costly act of service from a man who had been silent too long. He notes that the centurion, a professional soldier, could be trusted to know when a man was dead. The Tyndale notes add that granting the body to a council member suited Rome: it honored Jewish law and kept the body away from Jesus' disciples.

#### Pointing to Christ
Mark shows the King crowned with thorns, saluted in mockery, enthroned on a cross between two rebels, with his title nailed above him. Every insult is a truth turned inside out. "He saved others; he cannot save himself" is exactly how salvation works: he stayed so that others could go free, as Barabbas did. In the darkness he cried the cry of the forsaken, so that those who trust him would never have to. As he died the curtain tore from top to bottom, and a Roman soldier said what the whole Gospel has been building to: "Truly this man was the Son of God." Then he was laid, really dead, in a borrowed tomb. The women watched where. They would be back.
