*Setting:* This is the second half of one heavenly scene. In chapter 4, John was called up through an open door and saw God the Creator enthroned, with the elders and living creatures worshipping him. Now attention turns to a scroll in God's right hand and to the question at the heart of the book: who is worthy to open it? The answer is a Lamb. Chapter 4 praises God for creation. Chapter 5 praises the Lamb for redemption, and then both together. Every judgment from chapter 6 on begins when this Lamb breaks the seals.

#### The Scroll and the Lamb (vv1–7)
- **"In the right hand of him who was seated on the throne a scroll written within and on the back, sealed with seven seals" (v1).** A scroll was a roll of papyrus or parchment. Writing on both sides was unusual and means the contents are complete, with nothing left to add (Ezek 2:9–10). Seven seals mean it is completely closed. Interpreters differ on what it is:
  - **God's plan for history and its end**, his purposes of judgment and salvation (Dan 12:4, 9). This is the most common view, and the Tyndale notes take it.
  - **A title deed** to the creation humanity lost, which only a qualified kinsman-redeemer can reclaim (Jer 32:6–15; Ruth 4). This is popular among futurists.
  - **A sealed will.** Roman wills were sealed by seven witnesses and opened only after the testator's death. The picture fits strikingly with a Lamb who has died.

  These views overlap. Whatever the scroll is, it holds God's purposes for the world, and opening it puts them into action. That is why the seals in chapter 6 bring events, not just information.
- **"Who is worthy to open the scroll and break its seals?" (v2).** "Worthy" is the key word of the chapter (vv2, 4, 9, 12). It means qualified by character and right, not just strength. In 4:11 God was worthy as Creator. Now the question is who is worthy to bring history to its goal.
- **"No one in heaven or on earth or under the earth was able" (v3).** These are the three tiers of the ancient picture of the universe: angels, the living, and the dead. Abraham, Moses, David, Michael and Gabriel are all included, and no created being qualifies.
- **"I began to weep loudly" (v4).** John isn't being dramatic. If the scroll stays sealed, God's plan to judge evil and renew the world stays shut. The suffering churches of chapters 2–3 have no ending to their story.
- **"Weep no more; behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered" (v5).** Two great messianic titles:
  - **Lion of Judah:** Jacob blessed Judah as "a lion's cub" from whom the sceptre would not depart (Gen 49:9–10).
  - **Root of David:** the "shoot from the stump of Jesse" and "root of Jesse" (Isa 11:1, 10). Jesus is both David's descendant and David's source (22:16; Mark 12:35–37).

  "Has conquered" (*enikēsen*) is the word promised seven times to "the one who conquers." He conquered first.
- **"I saw a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (v6).** This is one of the great turns in Scripture. John is told "Lion" and turns to see a Lamb. Jesus defines what "Lion" means: he conquers by being sacrificed. "Lamb" is *arnion*, a diminutive, "little lamb," as the lexicon confirms. The diminutive force had faded in everyday Greek, but the contrast with the lion is real. Revelation calls Jesus "Lamb" twenty-eight times. "Slain" is *sphazō*, to slaughter, especially a sacrificial victim, by cutting the throat. This is the Passover lamb (Exod 12; 1 Cor 5:7) and the lamb led to the slaughter (Isa 53:7). Yet it is "standing": it was killed, and it is alive. The wounds remain, as with the risen Jesus in John 20:27.
- **"Between the throne and the four living creatures and among the elders" (v6).** The Lamb stands at the centre of the throne room, where the attention of all heaven converges. In 7:17 he is "in the midst of the throne."
- **"Seven horns and with seven eyes, which are the seven spirits of God sent out into all the earth" (v6).** Horns stand for power in the Old Testament (Deut 33:17; Dan 7:24), so seven horns means complete power. The eyes come from Zechariah 4:10, "the eyes of the LORD, which range through the whole earth," so the Lamb has complete knowledge. The Spirit is "sent out" from him (John 15:26). The slain Lamb is not weak. He holds all power and sees everything.
- **"He went and took the scroll from the right hand of him who was seated on the throne" (v7).** This is the decisive act of the scene, and the text puts it simply. It echoes Daniel 7:13–14, where one like a son of man comes to the Ancient of Days and receives dominion. The Lamb takes the scroll not by force but because he has the right.
- **Commentators:** On the scroll, Henry sees "the counsels and decrees of God… as in a book, which God held in his right hand". JFB calls it the "title-deed" of the inheritance Christ redeemed for humanity. Guzik weighs several views. He finds the title deed appealing but unproven and leans toward a sealed will, God's final settlement of the universe. Henry reads the Lion of Judah and Root of David as Christ's human and divine natures. JFB puts it as "David's Son and David's Lord, the Lamb slain and therefore the Lion of Juda". Henry and JFB both stress the wounds: "He appears with the marks of his sufferings upon him" (Henry), "bearing marks of His past death wounds" (JFB). Guzik draws out the contrast: John is told of a lion and turns to see a small lamb. JFB says *arnion* is a word of endearment, set apart from the beast (*thērion*). The lexicon supports "little lamb" as the basic meaning.

#### The New Song and the Worship of Heaven (vv8–14)
- **"Fell down before the Lamb" (v8).** The same beings who fell before God in 4:10 now fall before the Lamb. Revelation forbids worshipping angels (19:10; 22:8–9), so this is a claim that Jesus is God.
- **"Each holding a harp, and golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (v8).** The harp (*kithara*), a lyre, was the instrument of the psalms (Ps 33:2). Incense rising stood for prayer (Ps 141:2). "Saints" means all God's people, not a special class. The prayers return in 6:10 and 8:3–5, where they rise before God and fire is cast on the earth. The prayers of suffering believers are part of how God brings history to its end. The elders hold the prayers before God. The text doesn't say they hear or answer them, and nothing here suggests praying to them.
- **"They sang a new song" (v9).** A "new song" in the Psalms celebrates a new act of God's salvation (Ps 33:3; 96:1; 98:1; Isa 42:10). The act here is the cross.
- **"For you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God" (v9).** "Ransomed" is *agorazō*, to buy in the marketplace. The lexicon notes it was common in deeds of sale. It is used for buying slaves and for redemption (1 Cor 6:20; 7:23). The price was blood. The worthiness of the Lamb rests on one thing: "you were slain."
- **"From every tribe and language and people and nation" (v9).** This fourfold phrase appears seven times in Revelation in various orders (7:9; 10:11; 11:9; 13:7; 14:6; 17:15). It reverses Babel (Gen 11) and fulfils the promise to Abraham that all nations would be blessed (Gen 12:3).
- **A textual note on "people… them" (vv9–10).** The KJV reads "redeemed *us*… made *us*… *we* shall reign," which makes the elders sing about themselves. The oldest manuscripts read "ransomed [people]… made *them*… *they* shall reign," and modern translations, the ESV included, follow them. The change matters to the question of who the elders are. With "us," they are redeemed humans. With "them," they could be angels singing about the redeemed. The evidence is mixed in v9: Codex Alexandrinus, one of the oldest manuscripts, has no object at all, while many others read "us." It is clearer in v10, where the main early witnesses read "them."
- **"You have made them a kingdom and priests to our God, and they shall reign on the earth" (v10).** This is Israel's calling at Sinai (Exod 19:6), now given to the redeemed of all nations (1:6; 1 Pet 2:9). The reign is "on the earth," not only in heaven. God's goal is a renewed earth (21:1–3). Manuscripts differ between "shall reign" (future) and "reign" (present). Premillennial readers connect this promise with the thousand-year reign of 20:4–6. Amillennial readers see believers' present share in Christ's rule, completed in the new creation.
- **"Myriads of myriads and thousands of thousands" (v11).** A myriad was ten thousand, the largest single number word in Greek. This is beyond counting, echoing Daniel 7:10. The circle of worship widens: the creatures and elders first, then the angels around them.
- **"Worthy is the Lamb who was slain, to receive power and wealth and wisdom and might and honor and glory and blessing!" (v12).** Seven gifts are ascribed to him, the number of completeness. He takes up what the world denied him at the cross. He who became poor receives wealth (2 Cor 8:9). He who was mocked receives honour. Handel set this verse as the closing chorus of *Messiah*.
- **"Every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea" (v13).** The circle widens to all of creation. The three tiers that could not open the scroll (v3) now praise the one who could. Paul has the same picture: every knee bowing and every tongue confessing (Phil 2:10–11). Some think the verse looks ahead to the end, when every creature acknowledges Christ, willingly or not. Others see a picture of praise ringing through the whole creation now.
- **"To him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb" (v13).** This is the climax. The praise of chapter 4 (to God) and of chapter 5 (to the Lamb) merge into one doxology to both together. Jewish monotheism, which gives worship to God alone, here includes the Lamb within that worship without any sense of strain.
- **"The four living creatures said, 'Amen!' and the elders fell down and worshiped" (v14).** The scene closes where it began, with the inner circle. "Amen" means "so be it."
- **Commentators:** Henry finds a lesson in the bowls and harps: "prayer and praise should always go together." He notes of the angels: "though they did not need a Saviour themselves, yet they rejoice in the redemption and salvation of sinners". On the incense, JFB says it "gives not the least sanction to Rome's dogma of our praying to saints." Guzik says the same: the elders are not mediators, since there is one mediator (1 Tim 2:5). JFB alone discusses the variant, noting that the older manuscripts read "them" in v10. Henry and Guzik comment on the KJV-style "us." JFB also argues that the reign is "over" the earth. Henry says the shared honour in v13 shows the Lamb shares the Father's nature. Guzik reaches the same conclusion from the joint worship. He adds that "worthy" echoes the acclamation crowds shouted to emperors, a point the Tyndale notes also make in chapter 4.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter is about Jesus from beginning to end. History has a goal, and only one person in the universe is qualified to bring it there. He is announced as a Lion but appears as a Lamb, slaughtered and standing. His power is the cross. His worthiness is "you were slain." His victory is a people bought from every nation and made a kingdom of priests. The rest of Revelation can be frightening, but it is unrolled by these hands, still marked by the wounds. All heaven ends up singing to "him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb."
