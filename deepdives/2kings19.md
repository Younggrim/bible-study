*Setting:* Jerusalem, 701 BC. Sennacherib of Assyria has taken Judah's fortified cities and is besieging Lachish, 30 miles to the southwest. Hezekiah has already paid a huge tribute, stripping even the temple doors (18:14–16), but it bought nothing. Three of Assyria's highest officials stood outside the walls and shouted to the people in Hebrew that no god had ever saved a nation from Assyria and the LORD would be no different (18:19–35). The people kept silent, and Hezekiah's officials came back with their clothes torn. Chapter 19 is the turning point: what Hezekiah does with that blasphemy, and what God does. The same account appears almost word for word in Isaiah 37, and in shorter form in 2 Chronicles 32:20–22.

#### Isaiah Reassures Hezekiah (vv1–7)
- **"He tore his clothes and covered himself with sackcloth and went into the house of the LORD" (v1).** Sackcloth was rough goat-hair cloth worn in mourning and repentance (Joel 1:13). Hezekiah's first move is not to the war room but to the temple. The Tyndale notes say he went where he could pour out his heart to God. Guzik observes that seeing the danger accurately and grieving over it was the right first reaction.
- **"He sent Eliakim… and Shebna… and the senior priests… to the prophet Isaiah the son of Amoz" (v2).** This is the first time Isaiah appears in Kings, though he had been prophesying for nearly forty years (Isa 1:1; 6:1). Kings in trouble often sent to a prophet (3:11–12; 22:13). Isaiah's whole ministry had urged trust in the LORD rather than in Egypt or Assyria (Isa 7:9; 30:15: "in quietness and in trust shall be your strength").
- **"This day is a day of distress, of rebuke, and of disgrace" (v3).** "Rebuke" may mean God's discipline on Judah, and "disgrace" the contempt the enemy heaps on them. **"Children have come to the point of birth, and there is no strength to bring them forth."** A painful proverb: a mother in labor too exhausted to deliver, so both mother and child may die. Judah has reached the crisis and has no strength to get through it. Isaiah later uses the same picture in reverse: "Shall I bring to the point of birth and not cause to bring forth?" (Isa 66:9).
- **"It may be that the LORD your God heard all the words of the Rabshakeh… to mock the living God" (v4).** "Your God" is not a sign of distance. The Tyndale notes say Hezekiah was honoring Isaiah's special relationship with God. "The living God" is the contrast that drives the whole chapter: Assyria has lumped the LORD with the dead idols of Hamath and Arpad. David used the same phrase against Goliath: "who is this uncircumcised Philistine, that he should defy the armies of the living God?" (1 Sam 17:26). Hezekiah's hope rests not on Judah's worth but on God's honor.
- **"Lift up your prayer for the remnant that is left" (v4).** With 46 cities lost, Jerusalem is nearly all that remains. "Remnant" is a key idea in Isaiah, whose son was named Shear-jashub, "a remnant shall return" (Isa 7:3).
- **"Do not be afraid because of the words that you have heard, with which the servants of the king of Assyria have reviled me" (v6).** God takes the insults personally: they "reviled *me*." "Servants" (*naʿarim*) is literally "lads" or "boys," a belittling word for the Assyrian grandees. Guzik, following Motyer, makes this point. Sennacherib's mighty officers are just errand boys to God.
- **"I will put a spirit in him, so that he shall hear a rumor and return to his own land, and I will make him fall by the sword in his own land" (v7).** Three predictions: a "spirit" (an inner impulse from God, as in 1 Kings 22:22), a report that sends him home, and a violent death at home. JFB says all three were fulfilled. Guzik notes the prophecy was specific enough to prove Isaiah true or false.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Jerusalem was saved "not by sword or bow, but by prayer and prophecy, and by the hand of an angel." JFB says the message to Isaiah describes "by a strong figure, the desperate condition" of the kingdom. Guzik takes "your God" as Hezekiah appealing to God's personal stake in the insult.

#### Sennacherib Defies the Lord (vv8–13)
- **"Found the king of Assyria fighting against Libnah, for he heard that the king had left Lachish" (v8).** Lachish had fallen. Libnah was a nearby town about eight miles to the north, as the Tyndale notes say, on Sennacherib's way back toward the coast road.
- **"Tirhakah king of Cush… has set out to fight against you" (v9).** Cush was the land south of Egypt (modern Sudan), whose Nubian kings ruled Egypt as its 25th Dynasty. Tirhakah (Taharqa) became pharaoh only around 690 BC, so in 701 he was a young prince leading the army for his brother. The writer calls him "king" by the title he later held, as the Tyndale notes and Guzik explain. Sennacherib's annals record defeating an Egyptian-Cushite army at Eltekeh that year. This is likely the "rumor" of v7, at least in part. JFB calls him the "expected succor" the Rabshakeh had mocked.
- **"Do not let your God in whom you trust deceive you" (v10).** Earlier the Rabshakeh said, "Do not let Hezekiah deceive you" (18:29). Now Sennacherib goes further: your *God* is the deceiver. He writes it in a letter this time, aimed at the king himself.
- **"What the kings of Assyria have done to all lands, devoting them to destruction" (v11).** "Devoting to destruction" is *charam*, the word used of Israel's conquest of Jericho (Josh 6:21), total destruction as a sacred act. Guzik notes the term. The Tyndale notes add that Assyria's torture and cruelty were well known. Sennacherib is claiming for himself the role of the holy war that belonged to God.
- **"Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, and the people of Eden who were in Telassar" (v12).** Cities and peoples in upper Mesopotamia and Syria crushed by earlier Assyrian kings. Haran was where Abraham's family stopped on their way to Canaan (Gen 11:31). Gozan was where Israel's exiles had been sent (17:6). **"Where is the king of Hamath…?" (v13).** The same list as 18:34, but now asking about kings instead of gods. Kings and gods alike have vanished.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes Sennacherib's letter as an attempt to frighten Hezekiah into surrender. Guzik notes the use of *charam* and that Tirhakah is called "king" in anticipation. JFB identifies Tirhakah as reigning in Upper Egypt, which reflects 19th-century knowledge; today Tirhakah is known to have ruled all of Egypt from about 690 BC.

#### Hezekiah's Prayer (vv14–19)
- **"Hezekiah received the letter… and spread it before the LORD" (v14).** One of the most loved pictures of prayer in the Bible. He unrolls the scroll in the temple and lays it out for God to read. JFB: "in the childlike confidence of faith." Guzik ties it to "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Pet 5:7). Hezekiah does not argue with the letter or answer it. He hands it to the one it insulted.
- **"O LORD, the God of Israel, enthroned above the cherubim" (v15).** The prayer starts with who God is, before asking for anything. The cherubim were the golden winged figures on the ark of the covenant in the Most Holy Place, a few yards from where Hezekiah stood. God was pictured as invisibly enthroned above them (1 Sam 4:4; Ps 80:1). Isaiah's version adds "LORD of hosts," the commander of heaven's armies (Isa 37:16), fitting for a military crisis, as Guzik observes.
- **"You are the God, you alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth; you have made the heavens and the earth" (v15).** This answers the Assyrian taunt directly. Assyria claimed to be above all the gods of the nations; Hezekiah confesses that the LORD alone rules all nations, including Assyria, because he made everything. This is the same confession as Deuteronomy 6:4 and Isaiah 44:6.
- **"Incline your ear, O LORD, and hear; open your eyes, O LORD, and see" (v16).** Bold, human language. Daniel prays almost the same words (Dan 9:18). Idols have ears and eyes that do nothing (Ps 115:5–6); the living God hears and sees.
- **"Truly, O LORD, the kings of Assyria have laid waste the nations… and have cast their gods into the fire, for they were not gods" (vv17–18).** Hezekiah admits the Assyrian's facts are true. Assyria really had destroyed nations and their gods. But he draws the opposite conclusion. Those gods fell because they were "the work of men's hands, wood and stone" (Isa 44:9–20). The LORD is not that kind of god. Honest prayer does not deny the danger.
- **"So now, O LORD our God, save us, please, from his hand, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you, O LORD, are God alone" (v19).** The request is short, and the purpose is God's glory. Like Moses (Num 14:13–16) and Elijah on Carmel (1 Kings 18:36–37), Hezekiah asks for rescue so that the world will know who God is. The Tyndale notes say Sennacherib's victories did not matter, because the LORD, unlike the idols, is the living God.
- **Commentators:** Henry says Hezekiah "recommended his case to God, the righteous Judge." Guzik lays out the prayer's shape: address God for who he is, ask plainly, and aim at God's glory. JFB sees the spread letter as an appeal to God against taunts on his own honor.

#### Isaiah Prophesies Sennacherib's Fall (vv20–37)
- **"Your prayer to me about Sennacherib king of Assyria I have heard" (v20).** The answer is linked directly to the prayer. Guzik stresses that it came *because* Hezekiah prayed (compare James 4:2: "You do not have, because you do not ask").
- **"She despises you, she scorns you — the virgin daughter of Zion" (v21).** The poem that follows is a taunt song, a known ancient form for celebrating an enemy's fall, as the Tyndale notes explain (compare Isa 14:4–20). Jerusalem is pictured as a young unmarried woman whom the Assyrian brute has tried to seize, and she laughs at him as he slinks away. "Virgin" may also say that, as Guzik suggests, she has never been conquered by this enemy. **"She wags her head behind you."** A gesture of contempt, the same one people later used at the cross (Matt 27:39).
- **"Whom have you mocked and reviled?… Against the Holy One of Israel!" (v22).** "The Holy One of Israel" is Isaiah's signature name for God, used about 25 times in his book and rarely elsewhere. Sennacherib thought he was insulting a small nation's god. He was insulting the God whose holiness made the seraphim cover their faces (Isa 6:3).
- **"With my many chariots I have gone up the heights of the mountains… I felled its tallest cedars" (v23).** God quotes the Assyrian's boasting back to him, and it matches Assyrian royal inscriptions closely. Kings boasted of crossing impossible mountain passes and cutting Lebanon's cedars for their palaces, as the Tyndale notes observe from Sennacherib's own records.
- **"I dug wells and drank foreign waters, and I dried up with the sole of my foot all the streams of Egypt" (v24).** Boasts of crossing deserts and of conquering Egypt so easily that its Nile canals dried under his marching feet. Sennacherib never conquered Egypt, though his son did. The boast is empty.
- **"Have you not heard that I determined it long ago?… that you should turn fortified cities into heaps of ruins" (v25).** The heart of God's answer. Assyria's victories happened only because God planned them. Isaiah 10:5–15 makes the same point: Assyria is the axe in God's hand, and "shall the axe boast over him who hews with it?" The Tyndale notes put it plainly: God had brought about all of Sennacherib's great achievements.
- **"Like grass on the housetops, blighted before it is grown" (v26).** Flat roofs were covered with packed earth, and grass sprang up there after rain, but with no depth of soil it withered fast (Ps 129:6). That is how the conquered nations fell.
- **"I know your sitting down and your going out and coming in, and your raging against me" (v27).** God knows every move of the emperor (Ps 139:1–3). The rage is not against Judah but "against me."
- **"I will put my hook in your nose and my bit in your mouth" (v28).** A ring through the nose led a bull; a bit controlled a horse. Assyrian kings boasted of leading captive kings by hooks or ropes through the lips or nose, and a stela of Esarhaddon shows exactly this. The Tyndale notes and Guzik both make the point. God will do to Sennacherib what Sennacherib did to others: lead him home like a tamed animal. Ezekiel uses the same image of Gog (Ezek 38:4).
- **"This shall be the sign for you: this year eat what grows of itself, and in the second year what springs of the same" (v29).** The invasion had stopped the farmers from sowing. For this year and next, people would live on what sprang up on its own from fallen seed. In the third year normal farming would return. The sign is not a wonder but a promise: the land would be free long enough to plant and harvest again. JFB says the enemy would interrupt farming for two years; Guzik, following Motyer, works out the years. The sign could only be confirmed after the event, as with Moses (Exod 3:12).
- **"The surviving remnant… shall again take root downward and bear fruit upward" (v30).** A tree cut nearly to the ground puts down roots and grows again (Isa 6:13; 11:1). **"The zeal of the LORD will do this" (v31).** The same words close the great promise of the coming child in Isaiah 9:7.
- **"He shall not come into this city or shoot an arrow there… or cast up a siege mound against it" (v32).** Remarkably specific. At Lachish the Assyrians built a huge siege ramp, still visible today. At Jerusalem they would not build one or fire a single arrow. Sennacherib's own annals agree: he boasts of shutting Hezekiah up "like a bird in a cage" but never claims to have taken or stormed the city, as the Tyndale notes observe.
- **"For I will defend this city to save it, for my own sake and for the sake of my servant David" (v34).** Not because Jerusalem deserved it, but for God's honor and his covenant promise to David (2 Sam 7:12–16; 1 Kings 11:13). A century later people turned this into a superstition, chanting "the temple of the LORD" as if Jerusalem could never fall, and Jeremiah had to warn them (Jer 7:4–15). Guzik notes the warning too.
- **"That night the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians" (v35).** The Tyndale notes recall the destroying angel of the Passover (Exod 12:23). The text gives no natural cause. Some have suggested a sudden plague; JFB suggests a hot desert wind as the means. The Greek historian Herodotus, writing 250 years later, preserves an Egyptian story that in one night field mice gnawed through the bowstrings and shield straps of Sennacherib's army, forcing it to retreat; since mice carry plague, some see a garbled memory of the same event. Guzik mentions both and treats the natural explanations as guesses. The number 185,000 has been debated (the Hebrew word for "thousand" can sometimes mean a military unit), but the text plainly describes a catastrophe that ended the campaign. Isaiah had foretold it: "Assyria shall fall by a sword, not of man" (Isa 31:8). Lord Byron's poem "The Destruction of Sennacherib" retells this night.
- **"Then Sennacherib king of Assyria departed and went home and lived at Nineveh" (v36).** He reigned another twenty years and led other campaigns, but never came back to Judah, as the Tyndale notes say.
- **"As he was worshiping in the house of Nisroch his god, Adrammelech and Sharezer, his sons, struck him down with the sword" (v37).** The Babylonian Chronicle confirms that Sennacherib was murdered by his son in 681 BC, about twenty years after this campaign. The writer telescopes the time to finish the story. The irony is the point, as the Tyndale notes observe: the king who mocked the LORD's power to save was killed in his own god's temple, and his god could not save him. Nisroch is unknown from Assyrian sources; it may be a garbled form of a god's name such as Nusku or Ninurta. "Ararat" is Urartu, a kingdom in the mountains of modern Armenia and eastern Turkey. **"Esarhaddon his son reigned in his place."** He reigned until 669 BC.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls God's reply "a very comfortable message, assuring him of deliverance," and sums up the chapter: "And so God glorified himself and saved his people." Guzik explains the hook in the nose from the Esarhaddon stela, ties the sign to the years 701–700 BC, and links the night of v35 to Hosea 1:7, deliverance "not by bow or by sword." JFB calls the slaughter "the miraculous interposition of the Almighty" and suggests a desert wind as the secondary cause. JFB has no notes on the taunt song's imagery (vv21–28).

#### Pointing to Christ
Hezekiah, the son of David, faces an enemy far too strong for him, spreads the enemy's words before God, and asks for rescue so that all the earth will know the LORD alone is God. God answers for his own sake and "for the sake of my servant David." That same promise to David is why God sent the greater Son of David, who faced the last enemy, death, and was not rescued from it but through it. The taunt "He trusts in God; let God deliver him" was hurled at him too (Matt 27:43), and the answer came on the third day. The power that turned back Assyria without an arrow fired is the power that raised Jesus, and the remnant that "shall take root downward and bear fruit upward" grows from him, the shoot from the stump of Jesse (Isa 11:1).
