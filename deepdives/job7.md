*Setting:* Job is still answering Eliphaz. His reply began in chapter 6, where he defended his outburst, wished again that God would end his life, and accused his friends of drying up like a desert stream just when he needed them. In chapter 7 his eyes move away from the friends. He starts with the hard lot of every human being (vv1–6), then from v7 on he speaks straight to God, the first time in the book that he speaks directly to God. The prayer is raw. He complains about sleepless nights, terrifying dreams, a God who will not leave him alone, and a death that is coming fast. The reader knows what Job does not: God has called him "blameless and upright" (1:8), and his suffering is a test Satan asked for, not a punishment. That gap between what Job can see and what is really happening is what makes this chapter so painful and so honest.

#### Job Continues: My Life Has No Hope (vv1–21)
- **"Has not man a hard service on earth?" (v1).** "Hard service" translates *tsaba*, the ordinary Hebrew word for an army, war, or a term of military duty. The lexicon confirms that range, and older translations caught it: the KJV has "an appointed time," and the Latin Vulgate reads "warfare." Life feels like being drafted into a long, harsh campaign that no one volunteered for. Job uses the same word later for his whole time of suffering, "all the days of my service" (14:14), and Isaiah uses it when he announces comfort to Jerusalem: "her warfare is ended" (Isa 40:2). Job is not yet talking only about himself. He is saying that the human lot as a whole is hard. The Tyndale notes trace that hardness back to the curse on the ground after the first sin (Gen 3:17) and forward to Paul's picture of a creation groaning and "subjected to futility" (Rom 8:20–22).
- **"Like a slave who longs for the shadow, and like a hired hand who looks for his wages" (v2).** Two pictures from the working day. A slave in the fields longs for evening, when the shadows lengthen and the work stops. A day laborer watches for sundown, when he is paid. God's law required employers to pay workers before nightfall (Lev 19:13; Deut 24:15), though prophets and apostles show that many didn't (Jer 22:13; Jas 5:4). Both workers at least have something to look forward to. That is the point of the comparison, and it sets up the painful contrast in the next verse.
- **"So I am allotted months of emptiness" (v3).** "Allotted" is inheritance language. Job's "inheritance" is not land or flocks but empty months and nights of misery. The word for "misery" is *'amal*, trouble or toil, a word that runs all through the book (Eliphaz used it in 5:6–7: "man is born to trouble"). The slave gets his evening and the laborer his wages, but Job's long months bring nothing. The word "months" also gives a hint about the timeline: his suffering has already gone on for some time, not just a few days.
- **"When I lie down I say, 'When shall I arise?'" (v4).** Anyone who has been seriously ill or grieving knows this. Night is the worst time. The hours drag, he tosses and turns, and he waits for dawn just to have the night over with.
- **"My flesh is clothed with worms and dirt" (v5).** Job's disease is described in plain, ugly detail. His sores breed maggots, and the dust and ashes he sits in (2:8) crust over them. His skin hardens into scabs, then cracks and runs again. The Tyndale notes point out that in Job "worms" are also linked with the grave (17:14; 21:26; 24:20). Job's body is already starting to look like what happens in a tomb. No one knows exactly what the disease was. JFB thought it was elephantiasis, a guess some older writers shared, but the text does not name it.
- **"My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle and come to their end without hope" (v6).** The shuttle carries the thread back and forth across a loom, so fast the eye can barely follow it. Here there is a likely wordplay. The Hebrew for "hope" is *tiqvah*, and the same spelling also means "cord" or "thread" (it is the word for Rahab's scarlet cord in Josh 2:18). The lexicon lists the two as separate words and puts Job 7:6 under "hope," but a reader hearing "shuttle" and then *tiqvah* would hardly miss the double meaning: the days run out like a shuttle when the thread runs out, and Job's hope has run out with it. Notice the strange combination. At night time drags (v4), but life as a whole is flying by. Both feelings are true in long suffering.
- **"Remember that my life is a breath" (v7).** Now Job turns and speaks to God directly. In the Psalms, asking God to "remember" usually means asking him to act in mercy (Ps 106:4). "Breath" here is *ruach*, wind or breath, something that passes and is gone. Psalm 78:39 says God "remembered that they were but flesh, a wind that passes and comes not again," and James says the same of all human life: "you are a mist" (Jas 4:14). "My eye will never again see good" is the sharpest line yet. Eliphaz had promised that if Job repented he would go to his grave "in ripe old age" surrounded by blessing (5:26). Job does not believe it.
- **"While your eyes are on me, I shall be gone" (v8).** The friends who see him now will soon not see him at all. Even God, looking for him, will find him gone. The Tyndale notes say the ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) leaves this verse out, probably because the translators did not want to suggest that anyone could escape God's sight. Job is not making a statement about the limits of God's knowledge. He is speaking as a dying man: soon there will be nothing left here to look at.
- **"As the cloud fades and vanishes, so he who goes down to Sheol does not come up" (v9).** This is the first plain mention of *Sheol* in Job. In the Old Testament Sheol is the place of the dead, pictured as deep, dark and dusty, a place from which no one returns to ordinary life. It is not the same as the later, fuller teaching on heaven and hell. Job is not denying a future resurrection; he says something very different in 19:25–26. He is saying that once he dies he will not come back to this life and this house. A cloud in that dry land promised rain and then simply evaporated.
- **"He returns no more to his house, nor does his place know him anymore" (v10).** Home mattered deeply in the ancient world. JFB comments that "the Oriental keenly loves his dwelling." Psalm 103:16 uses almost the same words of human life: "its place knows it no more." Everything that once marked Job's life, the house, the place at the city gate (29:7), will carry on as if he had never been.
- **"Therefore I will not restrain my mouth" (v11).** This is a turning point. Since life is short and death is final, Job decides he has nothing left to lose by speaking honestly. He will "speak in the anguish" of his spirit and "complain in the bitterness" of his soul. The Tyndale notes connect this to Eliphaz's opening line, where Eliphaz said he couldn't keep from speaking (4:2). If Eliphaz could not hold back his lecture, Job will not hold back his cry. What follows is a lament addressed to God, the same kind of prayer found in many psalms. It is complaint, but it is complaint taken *to* God, not away from him.
- **"Am I the sea, or a sea monster, that you set a guard over me?" (v12).** The sea (*yam*) and the sea monster (*tannin*) were pictures of chaos in the ancient world. In Canaanite stories Yam was the sea god whom another god had to defeat and fence in. The lexicon gives *tannin* as serpent, dragon or sea monster, the same word used in Psalm 74:13 and Isaiah 27:1 and 51:9 for the forces God crushed. The Bible borrows the imagery without borrowing the myth: in Scripture the sea is not a rival god but a creature God set limits on (Job 38:8–11; Ps 104:9). Job's question is bitter irony. Is he such a dangerous threat to the universe that God has to post a guard around him? He feels as if God has penned him in with the vigilance he uses to hold back the ocean.
- **"When I say, 'My bed will comfort me'... then you scare me with dreams" (vv13–14).** Even sleep is no escape. Job lies down hoping for relief and wakes up terrified. He traces the nightmares directly to God. The Tyndale notes observe the irony: Eliphaz claimed authority from a frightening night vision (4:12–16), and now Job says his own night visions bring only terror.
- **"So that I would choose strangling and death rather than my bones" (v15).** "Strangling" probably means suffocation, the feeling of choking for breath that can come with severe illness, though some take it as any violent death. Job would rather die than go on living in this wasted body ("my bones"). As in 6:9, he wishes God would end his life. He does not say he will end it himself. JFB takes v16 to mean Job loathes the very thought of strangling himself, but most translations, including the ESV, read v16 as loathing his life.
- **"I loathe my life; I would not live forever. Leave me alone, for my days are a breath" (v16).** The word for "breath" now is *hebel*, vapor or mist. It is the same word that opens Ecclesiastes ("Vanity of vanities"), and the lexicon lists Job 7:16 among its uses for the fleeting shortness of life. Job asks God to "leave me alone." That is a startling prayer from a man who loved God, but it is not unbelief. He wants a break from what he feels is God's crushing attention, and Psalm 39:13 contains the same kind of plea: "Look away from me, that I may smile again."
- **"What is man, that you make so much of him?" (v17).** This is almost a word-for-word echo of Psalm 8:4: "what is man that you are mindful of him?" In Psalm 8 the question is full of wonder that the great Creator cares for small human beings. Job turns it upside down. Why does God pay so much attention to someone as small as him, if all that attention brings is pain? Scholars disagree about which text came first, so it is not certain Job is consciously parodying the psalm, but the contrast is unmistakable. Psalm 144:3 asks the question a third way, and Hebrews 2:6 takes Psalm 8 and applies it to Jesus.
- **"Visit him every morning and test him every moment" (v18).** "Visit" is *paqad*, a word with a wide range. The lexicon lists both "visit graciously" (as when the LORD "visited" Sarah with a son, Gen 21:1) and "visit to search, test," and it places this verse in that second category. The Tyndale notes say Job is using the word ironically. The same verb that elsewhere means God's tender care feels to Job like a daily inspection. JFB makes the point that it is God's mercies, not our trials, that are "new every morning" (Lam 3:23), but Job cannot see that now.
- **"How long will you not look away from me, nor leave me alone till I swallow my spit?" (v19).** Most believers pray for God to turn his face toward them (Num 6:25; Ps 27:9). Job begs him to look away. "Till I swallow my spit" was a proverb for the shortest possible moment, like "a split second." JFB, and Guzik citing E. W. Bullinger, both call it an Arabic proverb. Job wants one moment to breathe.
- **"If I sin, what do I do to you, you watcher of mankind?" (v20).** The Hebrew has no "if." It reads simply "I have sinned," and translations differ on how to take it. Many, like the ESV, read it as a supposition: even if I have sinned, how could that hurt you? Others, like the KJV and Matthew Henry, take it as a real confession. Either way, Job is not claiming to be sinless. He is saying that nothing he has done could account for this. "Watcher" is from *natsar*, a word usually used of God's protective keeping. The same verb describes God guarding Israel "as the apple of his eye" (Deut 32:10) and "keeping steadfast love for thousands" (Exod 34:7). Job feels that watching as hostile surveillance. The Tyndale notes point out the irony that this kind of prying, accusing scrutiny actually describes Satan, who had been "going to and fro on the earth" (1:7) looking for faults.
- **"Why have you made me your mark? Why have I become a burden to you?" (v20).** A "mark" is a target. Job has already said God's arrows are in him (6:4), and Lamentations uses the same picture (Lam 3:12). The last line has an interesting history. The standard Hebrew text reads "a burden to myself" (as in the KJV), but ancient Jewish scribal notes record that the original read "a burden to you," and that scribes changed it out of reverence, uncomfortable with the idea that a human could burden God. The Septuagint also has "to you." The ESV follows that older reading. It is a small change, but it fits Job's argument: why should one man's life weigh so heavily on God?
- **"Why do you not pardon my transgression and take away my iniquity?" (v21).** Job ends with a real question about grace. If he has sinned, why doesn't God simply forgive it and let him go? He does not understand why a merciful God would keep him in this pain instead of pardoning him. This is a significant line. Job is not proud or self-righteous here. He assumes he needs forgiveness and that forgiveness is God's to give.
- **"For now I shall lie in the earth; you will seek me, but I shall not be" (v21).** "Seek" is *shachar*, a verb formed from the word for dawn. The lexicon gives its meaning as to look for early or earnestly. Job pictures God coming to look for him one morning, as if God will finally relent, only to find that Job has already died. The same verb comes back in the next speech, when Bildad tells Job, "if you will seek God" (8:5). Job imagines God seeking him; his friend tells him he is the one who needs to do the seeking. The chapter ends with Job lying in the dust, unanswered. But God has not stopped watching, and the story is not over.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads v1 as soldiering, "Man's life is a warfare," with set hardships and a set end, which matches the lexicon's sense of *tsaba*; Guzik makes the same point, drawing on Adam Clarke and the Vulgate. On the shuttle, Henry notes that "every day, like the shuttle, leaves a thread behind it," and Guzik passes on the hope-and-thread wordplay from the rabbi Ibn Ezra. On v12 the commentators differ. Henry has Job feeling treated like "a raging sea, that must be kept within bounds," and JFB calls the sea in Old Testament poetry "a violent rebel against God." Guzik, following Spurgeon, adds a gentle counterpoint: a weak, sinful human may actually need watching more than the sea does. On vv17–18 Henry is generous to Job, finding "a pious admiration" of God's grace even inside the complaint, while JFB says Job should have seen God's close attention as a sign of a wise purpose in trial. On v20, Henry reads "I have sinned" as a true confession: "The best must acknowledge, before God, that they have sinned." On v21 he puts the right order of things into Job's mouth: "Lord, pardon my sins, and give me the comfort of that pardon, and then I can easily bear my afflictions." JFB reads God's "seeking" in v21 as a morning visit to a sick man who is found already dead, "not the resurrection." Guzik ends on a note the reader of the whole book can see: Job feels abandoned, but God is watching with approval, and in the end (42:7–8) he will say Job spoke rightly.

#### Pointing to Christ
Job asks, "What is man, that you make so much of him?" and turns Psalm 8's wonder into a complaint. Hebrews 2:6–9 takes the same psalm and answers it with Jesus. The true Man was "made lower than the angels" so that "by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone." Jesus also knew what it is to have days that run out, nights of anguish (Luke 22:44), and a cry of "Why?" thrown up to God: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). Job asks why God does not "pardon my transgression and take away my iniquity." The answer comes at the cross, where the one who had no sin became "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). Job feared God would look for him and find him gone into the dust. In Christ the story turns around: the Son of Man "came to seek and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10), and those who lie down in the dust will one day "awake and sing for joy" (Isa 26:19).
