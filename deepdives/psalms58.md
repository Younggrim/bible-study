*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David." This is the middle psalm of three that share the same title (57–59), all from the years when Saul hunted David. No occasion is named, but the target is clear: people with power to judge who use it to crush the innocent. Henry passes on a suggestion from the French scholar Amyraut that before Saul sent soldiers after David, he had him condemned unheard by a council of elders and declared an outlaw. That is a guess, but it fits the mood of Saul's court, where he reminded his officials that the son of Jesse could not give them fields and vineyards (1 Sam 22:7–8). Psalm 58 is one of the "imprecatory" psalms, psalms that call down God's judgment on the wicked, and its images are among the most violent in the Psalter. It needs to be read carefully: it is a cry for justice from a man who refused to take vengeance with his own hand (1 Sam 24:12; 26:10–11), and it hands the whole matter to God. The psalm moves from an accusation (vv1–5) to a prayer (vv6–9) to a confident ending (vv10–11).

#### Do You Indeed Decree What Is Right? (vv1–5)
- **"Do you indeed decree what is right, you gods?" (v1).** The opening is a sarcastic question to the rulers. The Hebrew word here is difficult. As written, it is *'elem*, "silence," which the King James took as "O congregation" and JFB as "O dumb ones": "Are ye dumb? ye should speak righteousness." BDB calls *'elem* a word for "silence" and, like most modern scholars, suggests reading *'elim*, "gods" or "mighty ones," with a change of vowels only. The ESV takes that reading. "Gods" is a biting title for human judges, as in Psalm 82: "God has taken his place in the divine council; in the midst of the gods he holds judgment… 'I said, "You are gods"… nevertheless, like men you shall die'" (82:1, 6–7). The Tyndale notes explain that the word could mean high officials or judges, angelic beings, or the gods of the nations. Either reading lands the same blow. Judges who should speak for justice are silent, or they think they are gods.
- **"Do you judge the children of man uprightly?" (v1).** The question sets "gods" against "the children of man." They act as if they were above everyone, but the people they judge are human beings made in God's image. Henry reminds them that they too are "sons of men; mortal and dying."
- **"No, in your hearts you devise wrongs" (v2).** The answer comes at once: no. The corruption starts inside. Henry: "all the wickedness of the life is wrought in the heart." Caryl, cited by Guzik, calls the heart an underground workshop where evil is forged before it is used.
- **"Your hands deal out violence on earth" (v2).** The verb is *palas*, to weigh out or level, as on a balance. The King James has "ye weigh the violence of your hands." The picture is cutting. The scales are the symbol of justice, and these judges measure out violence with them. JFB: "Weigh is a figure to express the acts of judges." Henry: violence is practised "the more effectually for being practised under the pretext of law and right."
- **"The wicked are estranged from the womb; they go astray from birth, speaking lies" (v3).** The psalm moves from these judges to the wicked in general. Their evil is not a one-off slip. It goes back to the beginning. David said the same of himself: "I was brought forth in iniquity" (51:5). The Tyndale notes put it carefully: everyone is born a sinner, but the wicked indulge that nature while the godly fight it. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes how close this is to Paul's description of humanity in Romans 3, and says the reader is looking into a mirror as well as at a portrait. Boice, cited by Guzik, recalls Chesterton's remark that original sin is the one Christian doctrine that can be proved by observation.
- **"Speaking lies" (v3).** The first sin named is lying, the native tongue of Satan (John 8:44). Henry: "How soon will little children tell a lie to excuse a fault!"
- **"Venom like the venom of a serpent" (v4).** Their words poison. The Tyndale notes take "venom" as poisonous speech, as in 140:3, a verse Paul quotes in Romans 3:13: "The venom of asps is under their lips." The serpent recalls the first liar in Eden (Gen 3:1).
- **"Like the deaf adder that stops its ear, so that it does not hear the voice of charmers" (vv4–5).** Snake charming was an ancient trade in Egypt and the Near East, and the Bible mentions it elsewhere (Eccl 10:11; Jer 8:17: "adders that cannot be charmed"). Snakes cannot hear sound through the air the way people do. They pick up vibrations through the ground and react to the charmer's movements. The psalm is not making a point about snake biology. It uses a common belief that some adders "stopped their ears" and could not be charmed. Henry passes on the folk story that the adder pressed one ear to the ground and plugged the other with its tail, and he adds that the comparison "neither verifies the story" nor approves charming. The point is that these people cannot be reached. No warning, no reason, no kindness gets through. Henry: "None so deaf as those that will not hear."
- **"The cunning enchanter" (v5).** Literally "one who binds spells skillfully." The word for charming comes from a root meaning to bind or tie.
- **Commentators:** Henry divides the charge in two: the corruption of their government (vv1–2) and the corruption of their nature (vv3–5), which he calls "the root of bitterness." He lists three marks of that nature: falsehood, malice and stubbornness. JFB says vv3–5 describe the wicked "who sin naturally, easily, malignantly, and stubbornly," and notes that "stoppeth her ear" is literally "his," the wicked man himself. Guzik notes the NIV's "rulers" follows a different reading from the King James "congregation" and the ESV's "gods," and passes on VanGemeren's observation that a cobra may bite even the charmer who handles it.

#### O God, Break the Teeth (vv6–9)
- **"O God, break the teeth in their mouths; tear out the fangs of the young lions, O LORD!" (v6).** The serpent becomes a lion. David does not ask for their deaths here. He asks God to take away their power to tear and devour (3:7). Henry: "He does not say, 'Break their necks'… but, 'Break their teeth.'" Poole, cited by Guzik, notes that the "fangs" are the great grinding teeth, the strongest in the mouth.
- **The prayer is addressed to God.** This matters for every imprecatory psalm. David is not plotting revenge. Twice he had Saul in his hands and refused to strike: "May the LORD judge between me and you, may the LORD avenge me against you, but my hand shall not be against you" (1 Sam 24:12). Paul teaches the same principle: "Never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Rom 12:19). Bringing anger to God in prayer is what keeps it from turning into violence.
- **"Let them vanish like water that runs away" (v7).** In the dry land of Israel, a stream that rushes down a wadi after a rainstorm sinks into the sand and is gone within hours. JFB: "as rapid mountain torrents." Henry: like water spilled on the ground that "cannot be gathered up again" (2 Sam 14:14).
- **"When he aims his arrows, let them be blunted" (v7).** The Hebrew is compressed, and translations differ. The sense is that the enemy's weapons should fail and fall short of the target. JFB reads it as arrows that become "blunted and of no avail."
- **"Let them be like the snail that dissolves into slime" (v8).** The word for snail, *shabbelul*, occurs only here. As a snail or slug moves, it leaves a trail of moisture behind it, and in the dry heat it can shrivel to nothing. The Tyndale notes make the same point: a snail crossing dry ground dries out. Boice, cited by Guzik, admits snails do not really melt, but they seem to. Henry turns it into a picture of the self-absorbed man who "does but consume himself" as he goes.
- **"Like the stillborn child who never sees the sun" (v8).** The image is painful, and it is meant to be. Job and Ecclesiastes use it of a life that comes to nothing (Job 3:16; Eccl 6:3–5). David prays that the wicked men's plans would die before they come to anything. Henry links it to Hosea 9:14, a prayer against the fruitfulness of a corrupt people. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, names Herod and Judas as men whose schemes were like that.
- **"Sooner than your pots can feel the heat of thorns, whether green or ablaze, may he sweep them away!" (v9).** This is one of the hardest verses in the Psalms to translate, and JFB admits it is "obscure in its clauses." The picture is a cooking fire made of dry thorn brush, which flares up fast. Before the pot even warms, God's judgment will sweep the wicked away like a whirlwind. Ecclesiastes 7:6 uses the same image: "as the crackling of thorns under a pot, so is the laughter of the fools." "Green or ablaze" probably means whatever state the thorns are in, half-kindled or burning, they are carried off together. Judgment will be sudden.
- **Commentators:** Henry insists David prays against them as enemies of God's people, "actuated by a public spirit… and not by any private revenge." Guzik, following VanGemeren, argues that evil this radical calls for God's justice, so the prayer should not shock us. JFB reads the images of vv8–9 as "utter ruin" and its rapidity, and notes, with some uncertainty, that the snail figure may allude to its losing moisture as it moves.

#### There Is a God Who Judges on Earth (vv10–11)
- **"The righteous will rejoice when he sees the vengeance" (v10).** This is not joy at someone's pain. It is joy that justice has finally been done, that the oppressed have been rescued and God has kept his word. The same joy is commanded in Revelation when Babylon falls: "Rejoice over her, O heaven, and you saints and apostles and prophets, for God has given judgment for you against her!" (Rev 18:20). The martyrs under the altar cry, "How long before you will judge and avenge our blood?" (Rev 6:10). Maclaren, cited by Guzik, says such joy is right as long as it is free of personal pride and spite, and suggests holding it beside Jesus weeping over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41).
- **"He will bathe his feet in the blood of the wicked" (v10).** A battlefield image. After a great victory the winner walks across the field, and the blood of the fallen is ankle deep. JFB: "denoting great slaughter." Psalm 68:23 has the same picture, and Revelation 14:20 and 19:13–15 use similar language for Christ's final judgment. It is war poetry, not a picture of a believer enjoying cruelty. Henry reads it as the refreshment of a weary traveler whose feet are washed: the righteous are refreshed to see God glorified, and also made to tremble (119:120).
- **"Surely there is a reward for the righteous" (v11).** "Reward" is *peri*, literally "fruit." The righteous life does bear fruit in the end, even if for a long time it looks like the wicked win (Ps 1:3; 73:17). The Tyndale notes: vindication is the reward of those who live for God.
- **"Surely there is a God who judges on earth" (v11).** The psalm ends where it began, with judges. The human "gods" of v1 failed to judge justly. The true God does. "God" here is *'Elohim*, and the verb "judges" is plural in Hebrew, something that happens with *'Elohim* now and then. "Mankind will say" means this is not only a comfort for the faithful. Ordinary people watching will see it and admit it. The psalm's whole cry is that the world not be left believing that power wins and nobody answers for it.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives two good effects of the judgment: saints are "encouraged and comforted," and sinners "convinced and converted," since God's justice is made plain. JFB says the joy of triumph is right because the wicked "are God's enemies, and their overthrow shows that He reigneth." Guzik passes on Morgan's warning against a soft sentimentality that sympathizes more with oppressors than with God's anger at oppression, and Kidner's reminder that the New Testament still speaks of judgment in these terms while rejecting earthly weapons (Rev 12:11).

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 58 shows what happens when those who hold the scales of justice "weigh out violence." Jesus was condemned by exactly such a court: rulers who sought false testimony (Matt 26:59), a governor who knew he was innocent and handed him over anyway (John 19:4–16). He did not answer with vengeance. "When he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23), the God of v11. In his death the poison of the serpent was itself broken (Gen 3:15), and the judge whom the world condemned is now the one to whom the Father "has given all judgment" (John 5:22). The psalm's longing is answered in him: there is a God who judges on earth, and he has fixed a day when he will judge the world in righteousness "by a man whom he has appointed" (Acts 17:31). Until then, the cry of Psalm 58 can be prayed honestly by the oppressed, and left in his hands.
