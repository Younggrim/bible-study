*Setting:* Psalm 97 has no title in the Hebrew text, and its author and date are unknown. The Greek translation heads it "for David, when his land is being restored," which probably reflects its use after the exile. It is another of the "The LORD reigns" psalms (93–100), and it opens with the same cry as Psalm 93 and the message Psalm 96:10 told Israel to announce among the nations. Psalm 96 ended with the LORD coming to judge the earth. Psalm 97 describes his arrival. Much of its language is borrowed from older Scripture, especially the scene at Mount Sinai (Exod 19:16–18) and the storm theophanies of Psalm 18, Psalm 50 and Habakkuk 3. A "theophany" is an appearance of God, often described with storm, fire and earthquake. The psalm moves from terror to joy: creation shakes, idolaters are shamed, Zion is glad, and the righteous are told to rejoice. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the ESV's stanza breaks.

#### The LORD Reigns, Let the Earth Rejoice (vv1–5)
- **"The LORD reigns, let the earth rejoice" (v1).** *Yahweh malak*, the cry of Psalm 93, now comes with an invitation. A tyrant's coronation brings fear. This King's reign brings joy, because of what v2 says about his throne.
- **"Let the many coastlands be glad!" (v1).** "Coastlands" (*iyyim*) means islands and coasts, the far shores of the Mediterranean world. For Israel, a land people with little seafaring, they stood for the most distant nations (Gen 10:5; Isa 42:4, 10; 49:1). Isaiah says "the coastlands wait for his law."
- **"Clouds and thick darkness are all around him" (v2).** This is Sinai language. When God came down on the mountain, "there were thunders and lightnings and a thick cloud," and Moses "drew near to the thick darkness where God was" (Exod 19:16; 20:21; Deut 4:11). The darkness speaks of God's hiddenness and holiness. No one can look on him or fully understand his ways.
- **"Righteousness and justice are the foundation of his throne" (v2).** The line is repeated from Psalm 89:14. The throne is surrounded by mystery, but it is not built on mystery. It rests on two pillars: "righteousness" (*tsedeq*, doing what is right) and "justice" (*mishpat*, right rulings that set wrongs right). God may hide what he is doing, but he never hides who he is. That is why the earth can rejoice.
- **"Fire goes before him and burns up his adversaries all around" (v3).** At Sinai "the LORD had descended on it in fire" (Exod 19:18). Fire in Scripture often shows God's holiness, which purifies what it does not consume (Deut 4:24; Heb 12:29). Here it marches ahead of him like an advance guard.
- **"His lightnings light up the world; the earth sees and trembles" (v4).** The words come close to Psalm 77:18 ("your lightnings lighted up the world; the earth trembled and shook"). In a thunderstorm the whole sky flashes white for an instant. The world sees him, and shakes.
- **"The mountains melt like wax before the LORD" (v5).** Mountains are the most solid things people know. Before God they soften like candle wax near a flame (Mic 1:4; Judg 5:5; Nah 1:5). **"Before the Lord of all the earth."** This title (*adon kol ha'arets*) first appears when Israel crossed the Jordan behind the ark: "the ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth is passing over before you" (Josh 3:11, 13). He is not a local god of Israel's land. He owns the whole earth.
- **Commentators:** Henry says that "sometimes indeed clouds and darkness are round about him; his dispensations are altogether unaccountable," yet "a golden thread of justice runs through the whole web of his administration." He reads the hills of v5 as proud enemies, "for their height, and strength, and immovable obstinacy," and says "when our God appears as a consuming fire even rocks will be wax before him." JFB says that though "our minds are oppressed with terror before the throne of the King of kings," we know "it is based on righteous principles." Guzik ties the clouds and fire to Sinai and makes the point that God's throne rests not on bribery, conquest or inheritance but on righteousness and justice, which is why his reign is reason for joy.

#### The Heavens Proclaim His Righteousness (vv6–7)
- **"The heavens proclaim his righteousness" (v6).** Psalm 19:1 says the heavens declare God's glory. Psalm 50:6 says, as here, that they declare his righteousness. The storm of vv2–5 is a public display, written across the sky for everyone to see. JFB suggests "heavens" may mean those who live there, the angels.
- **"And all the peoples see his glory" (v6).** Isaiah promised the same: "the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together" (Isa 40:5). God does not reign in secret forever.
- **"All worshipers of images are put to shame" (v7).** "Images" (*pesel*) are carved idols, the thing forbidden in the second commandment (Exod 20:4). "Put to shame" is more than embarrassment. It means being exposed as wrong, with every hope collapsing. When the true God appears, those who trusted a statue are left with nothing.
- **"Who make their boast in worthless idols" (v7).** The same pun from Psalm 96:5 returns: *elilim*, "nothings." They are boasting in empty things.
- **"Worship him, all you gods!" (v7).** Whatever powers stood behind the idols must bow to the LORD. The Hebrew word *elohim* can mean God, gods, or heavenly beings, and the Greek translation here reads "worship him, all his angels." Hebrews 1:6 quotes "Let all God's angels worship him" about the Son. The words of Hebrews match the longer Greek text of Deuteronomy 32:43 most closely, but this psalm says almost the same thing, and many think the writer had both passages in mind. Either way, he takes a call for heaven to worship the LORD and applies it to Jesus.
- **Commentators:** Henry says we might not have found Christ in v7 "if we had not been directed to it by the inspired apostle," and that the verse "helps us to a key to this whole psalm." He also observes, "This speaks the honour of Christ, that he has such worshippers." JFB reasons that "if angels must worship Him, how much more those who worshipped them," and reads "gods" as "angels" (Ps 8:5; Heb 1:6). Guzik quotes an earlier commentator who compares the scene to Dagon falling on his face before the ark (1 Sam 5:3–4).

#### Zion Hears and Is Glad (vv8–9)
- **"Zion hears and is glad" (v8).** Zion is Jerusalem, the city of God's temple. What makes the nations tremble makes God's own people rejoice, because his coming means their rescue.
- **"And the daughters of Judah rejoice" (v8).** This may mean the towns and villages of Judah around Jerusalem, as a city's "daughters" were its surrounding settlements (Num 21:25; Josh 15:45). It may also picture the women of Judah who came out singing and dancing after a victory, as Miriam did at the Red Sea and the women did after David killed Goliath (Exod 15:20; 1 Sam 18:6). The line repeats Psalm 48:11 almost exactly.
- **"Because of your judgments, O LORD" (v8).** For the oppressed, God's judgments are good news. Psalm 96 said the same: creation sings because "he comes to judge the earth."
- **"For you, O LORD, are most high over all the earth; you are exalted far above all gods" (v9).** The psalm turns to speak directly to God. "Most high" (*elyon*) is the title from Psalms 91 and 92. "Far above" (*me'od na'aleta*) is literally "greatly lifted up." Paul uses the same picture of the risen Christ, seated "far above all rule and authority and power and dominion" (Eph 1:21).
- **Commentators:** JFB says "the exaltation of Zion's king is joy to the righteous and sorrow to the wicked." Henry says that Christ's reign, though "matter of joy to all," will be "matter of terror to some, and it is their own fault that it is so." Guzik mentions the tradition of choirs of young women celebrating a victory, and notes how the psalmist moves from speaking about God to the world to praising God directly.

#### O You Who Love the LORD, Hate Evil! (vv10–12)
- **"O you who love the LORD, hate evil!" (v10).** The God whose throne rests on righteousness (v2) wants a people who share his loves and his hatreds. Loving God and loving what he hates do not go together. The same balance appears in Proverbs 8:13 ("the fear of the LORD is hatred of evil") and Romans 12:9 ("abhor what is evil; hold fast to what is good"). Hating evil does not mean hating people. It means refusing to make peace with sin, beginning with one's own.
- **"He preserves the lives of his saints" (v10).** "Saints" (*chasidim*) are those bound to God by *hesed*, his loyal covenant love: faithful, devoted people. "Preserves" means guards, keeps watch over. **"He delivers them from the hand of the wicked."** The command to hate evil comes with a promise. Those who stand against evil will face opposition, but they will not face it alone.
- **"Light is sown for the righteous" (v11).** A striking picture. Light is pictured as seed scattered in a field, which will spring up later. The righteous may be walking in darkness now, but the light has already been planted, and it will come up. Psalm 112:4 says, "Light dawns in the darkness for the upright." A textual note: one Hebrew manuscript and the Greek, Syriac and Latin versions read "light dawns" (*zarach*) instead of "light is sown" (*zarua*). The two words differ by one letter. The ESV follows the main Hebrew text and gives the other reading in a footnote. Both say the same thing in different pictures: light is coming.
- **"And joy for the upright in heart" (v11).** "Joy" is sown too. The harvest of the righteous is gladness.
- **"Rejoice in the LORD, O you righteous" (v12).** The psalm ends as it began, with rejoicing, but now it is focused. The earth was invited to rejoice (v1). The righteous are told to. This line repeats Psalm 32:11 and 33:1.
- **"And give thanks to his holy name!" (v12).** Literally "to the remembrance of his holiness" (as in Ps 30:4). God's holiness, which made the mountains melt, is for his people a reason for thanks.
- **Commentators:** JFB says gratitude for "the blessings of providence and grace" should "incite saints… to holy living," and that light is "sown—to spring forth abundantly." Henry says sown light, "like a winter seedness, it may lie long under the clods, and seem to be lost and buried, yet it will return in a rich and plentiful increase," and that "a true love to God will show itself in a real hatred of all sin." Guzik suggests that the command to hate evil may be one of the most often broken among God's people, who can easily claim to love God while tolerating what he hates. He adds that anger at sin must not turn into sin of its own.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 97 describes the coming of the King in fire and storm, and Hebrews applies its call for heaven's worship to the Son: when God "brings the firstborn into the world, he says, 'Let all God's angels worship him'" (Heb 1:6). The same Jesus who came quietly at Bethlehem will come again "in flaming fire" (2 Thess 1:7–8), and "every eye will see him" (Rev 1:7). His throne rests on righteousness and justice (Heb 1:8–9). For those who refuse him that day is terror, and for those who love him it is joy, as the psalm says. The "light sown for the righteous" has already broken through in him who is "the light of the world" (John 8:12), and it will be fully harvested when "the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (Matt 13:43).
