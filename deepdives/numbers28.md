*Setting:* The plains of Moab, at the end of the wilderness years. A new generation has been counted (ch 26), and Joshua has been named to lead them into Canaan (27:12–23). Before the conquest begins, God gives Moses a calendar of public offerings, which runs through chapters 28 and 29. Most of the festivals were already set out at Sinai (Exod 29:38–42; Lev 23), but here, for the first time, the required public sacrifices for each occasion are listed together, in order: daily, weekly, monthly, and then the yearly feasts. These were the offerings made by the priests on behalf of the whole nation, besides the gifts individual Israelites brought. It reads like a schedule, and it is one. But it tells Israel that, once in the land, their life is to keep time with God.

#### Daily Offerings (vv1–8)
- **Why now?** The people about to fight for Canaan are told first how to worship. The order matters: before they receive the land, they are reminded that the land is for the LORD, and life in it is to be built around his presence. A new generation also needed to hear these laws, and when they were scattered across the land, the shared calendar would hold the nation together around one altar.
- **"My offering, my food for my food offerings, my pleasing aroma" (v2).** "Food" (literally "bread") is a bold figure of speech. God does not need to eat (Ps 50:12–13: "If I were hungry, I would not tell you… Do I eat the flesh of bulls?"). The language pictures the offerings as a meal shared at God's table, a sign of fellowship between the King and his people. "Pleasing aroma" (Gen 8:21; Lev 1:9) means the offering is accepted and God is pleased with it. Paul uses it of Christ: "a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2).
- **"At its appointed time" (v2).** The word for "appointed time" (*mo'ed*) is the same word used for the festivals themselves (Lev 23:2). God sets the times. Worship in Israel was not left to mood or convenience.
- **"Two male lambs a year old without blemish, day by day, as a regular offering" (v3).** "Regular" is *tamid*, "continual." This is the daily burnt offering, "ordained at Mount Sinai" (v6; Exod 29:38–42). It was the base layer of all Israel's worship: whatever else was offered on a feast day was always "besides the regular burnt offering" (vv10, 15, 23, 24, 31). A burnt offering was wholly burned on the altar, expressing complete dedication to God and atonement for sin (Lev 1:3–4).
- **"In the morning… at twilight" (v4).** One lamb at the start of the day and one at its close. "Twilight" is literally "between the two evenings," usually understood as late afternoon to sunset. In Jesus' day the evening offering was made around 3 p.m., "the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1), which is the hour Jesus died (Mark 15:34–37). Daniel was praying "at the time of the evening sacrifice" when Gabriel came to him (Dan 9:21).
- **Flour, oil and wine (vv5, 7).** Each lamb came with **"a tenth of an ephah of fine flour"**, perhaps two to four pounds, depending on how the ephah is estimated, mixed with **"a quarter of a hin of beaten oil"**, about a quart, and a drink offering of the same amount. These matched the rule given in 15:1–10: every burnt offering of an animal was to come with grain, oil and wine, the main produce of the land. The meal was complete: meat, bread, oil and wine.
- **"A drink offering of strong drink" (v7).** The word (*shekar*) usually means beer or another fermented drink, though here it probably means wine, as in v14. It was poured out at the altar, not drunk. Paul describes his own life being "poured out as a drink offering" (Phil 2:17; 2 Tim 4:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry says that as the people prepared for war, they were reminded to "take their religion along with them, and not forget this, in the prosecution of their wars." JFB calls the repetition "seasonable and necessary," because of the laws' importance, their frequent neglect and the new generation, and says "my bread" is "used generally for the appointed offerings." Guzik takes the morning and evening lambs as a pattern for beginning and ending each day with God, trusting the atonement he has provided, and ties it to the psalms of morning and evening prayer (Pss 5:3; 141:2).

#### Sabbath Offerings (vv9–10)
- **"On the Sabbath day, two male lambs" (v9).** Each Sabbath doubled the daily offering, two more lambs with their grain and drink. The Sabbath was the weekly sign of the covenant (Exod 31:13), a day of rest that remembered creation (Exod 20:11) and the rescue from Egypt (Deut 5:15). Doubling the offering marked the day as holy without changing its character, which was rest.
- **"Besides the regular burnt offering" (v10).** The Sabbath offering did not replace the daily one. It was added on top.
- **Commentators:** JFB points out that "there is no previous mention of a Sabbath burnt offering," which makes this a new detail in the law.

#### Monthly Offerings (vv11–15)
- **"At the beginnings of your months" (v11).** Israel's months were lunar, each beginning at the new moon, when the first sliver of the moon appeared. The new moon was marked by trumpets (10:10; Ps 81:3), and it became a day of family meals and worship (1 Sam 20:5, 24–27; 2 Kgs 4:23; Amos 8:5). Moon worship was common among Israel's neighbors. Israel marked the moon's cycle too, but as a gift from the God who made "the moon to mark the seasons" (Ps 104:19).
- **"Two bulls from the herd, one ram, seven male lambs" (v11).** A much larger offering than the Sabbath's, with matching grain and wine for each animal, measured by size (vv12–14): more flour and wine for a bull, less for a ram, less again for a lamb. This set of two bulls, one ram and seven lambs becomes the standard for most of the feasts that follow.
- **"One male goat for a sin offering" (v15).** Every new moon included a sin offering. The burnt offerings expressed dedication. The sin offering dealt with sin, cleansing the people and the sanctuary. No month began without atonement.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the new moons "sacred festivals," marked by trumpets, rest and family feasts, and suggests that because "new-moon festivals" were "common among the heathen," God gave Israel a holy way to mark the month. Guzik notes that New Moon feasts were later corrupted into empty ritual, so that God said through Isaiah that he hated them (Isa 1:13–14).

#### Passover Offerings (vv16–25)
- **"On the fourteenth day of the first month is the LORD's Passover" (v16).** The first month is Abib, later called Nisan, in March or April. Passover remembered the night God passed over the houses marked with lamb's blood in Egypt (Exod 12). Its lamb was a family sacrifice, eaten at home, so no public offering is listed for the Passover day itself.
- **"On the fifteenth day of this month is a feast. Seven days shall unleavened bread be eaten" (v17).** The Feast of Unleavened Bread followed immediately, recalling the haste of the exodus, when there was no time for dough to rise (Exod 12:39). Over time leaven became a picture of sin to be cleared away (1 Cor 5:6–8).
- **"A holy convocation. You shall not do any ordinary work" (v18).** A sacred assembly, a day of gathering for worship, with the first and seventh days set apart from normal work (v25).
- **Every day for seven days (vv19–24).** The same offering as at the new moon, two bulls, one ram and seven lambs, plus a goat for a sin offering, **"to make atonement for you" (v22)**, every day of the feast, on top of the daily lambs.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that the festival was already established in Leviticus 23, but "some details are here introduced," namely the offerings for each of the seven days. Guzik points out that these public offerings for the whole nation stood alongside the household Passover lambs.

#### Offerings for the Feast of Weeks (vv26–31)
- **"On the day of the firstfruits… at your Feast of Weeks" (v26).** It was held seven weeks after Passover week (Lev 23:15–16; Deut 16:9–10), at the end of the grain harvest in late spring. Because it came on the fiftieth day, Greek-speaking Jews called it **Pentecost**, "fiftieth." A **"grain offering of new grain"** gave God the first of the wheat harvest, an act of thanks that confessed the whole harvest was his gift.
- **The same offering again (vv27–30).** Two bulls, one ram, seven lambs and a goat **"to make atonement for you" (v30).** Even the joy of harvest came with atonement for sin. Leviticus 23:18–19 lists a different set of animals for this day, and the Jewish tradition took the two lists as separate offerings, both to be made.
- **"See that they are without blemish" (v31).** The command is repeated (vv3, 9, 11, 19). God was not to be given leftovers or damaged animals (Mal 1:8).
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "a new sacrifice is here ordered for the celebration of this festival." Guzik observes that the feast's main note was thanksgiving for the harvest, but every feast carried atonement with it, and he applies this to believers, who should never lose sight of the atonement made for them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Morning and evening, every day, every Sabbath, every month and every feast, the lambs kept dying and the fire kept burning. The system's very repetition showed that it was not yet finished: "every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins" (Heb 10:11). Jesus, "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), died at Passover and at the hour of the evening offering, "a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2), offering "a single sacrifice for sins" for all time (Heb 10:12). Fifty days later, at the Feast of Weeks, the Spirit came, and three thousand were gathered in as the firstfruits of a new harvest (Acts 2:1, 41). Now the "regular offering" God asks of his people is their own lives, "a living sacrifice" (Rom 12:1), and "a sacrifice of praise… continually" (Heb 13:15).
