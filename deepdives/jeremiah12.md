*Setting:* This chapter continues straight on from the end of chapter 11. Jeremiah has just learned that the men of his hometown, Anathoth, are plotting to kill him. His response is one of the most honest prayers in the Bible, often counted among his "confessions" (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18): why do the wicked prosper? God answers, but not with an explanation. He tells Jeremiah that harder days are coming and that his own family is against him. Then God speaks of his grief at abandoning his beloved people, and the chapter ends with a surprising promise that even Judah's hostile neighbors can be "built up in the midst of my people" if they learn to swear by the LORD's name. The setting is probably the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), though some place it late in Josiah's reign.

#### Jeremiah's Complaint (vv1–4)
- **"Righteous are you, O LORD, when I complain to you" (v1).** Jeremiah sets his foundation before he asks his question. Whatever comes next, God is in the right. Then he uses courtroom language: "I would plead my case before you" is literally "I would speak judgments with you," the way a plaintiff brings a case to a judge. He takes his complaint to God, not away from him.
- **"Why does the way of the wicked prosper?" (v1).** This is one of the oldest questions of faith. Job asks it (Job 21:7), so do the psalmists (Ps 37; 73), Habakkuk (Hab 1:13) and Malachi's hearers (Mal 3:15). It is not a question unbelievers ask, since they expect nothing better. It is a believer's question, born from trusting that God is just. **"Why do all who are treacherous thrive?"** "Treacherous" (*bagad*) is the word for faithless Judah in chapter 3. The men who betrayed Jeremiah are doing just fine.
- **"You plant them, and they take root" (v2).** Jeremiah admits God planted them. Their prosperity is not outside his control, which is exactly what makes it hard to understand. The plotters used the picture of a tree to plan Jeremiah's death (11:19). Jeremiah answers that God seems to be watering *their* tree.
- **"You are near in their mouth and far from their heart" (v2).** Literally "far from their kidneys," the seat of deep feeling. They talk about God all the time. Anathoth was a town of priests. But it is talk only. Isaiah said the same, and Jesus quoted it: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (Isa 29:13; Matt 15:8).
- **"But you, O LORD, know me" (v3).** Jeremiah lays his own heart open. He is not claiming to be sinless, but that his heart is truly "toward you," the opposite of the plotters' lip-service.
- **"Pull them out like sheep for the slaughter" (v3).** A moment ago Jeremiah was the "gentle lamb led to the slaughter" (11:19). Now he asks God to turn the tables. **"Set them apart"** is literally "sanctify them," the word for consecrating an animal for sacrifice. The Tyndale notes say plainly that Jeremiah here shows no wish for their repentance, only their punishment. Scripture records his anger honestly without holding it up as the final word.
- **"How long will the land mourn?" (v4).** Jeremiah widens his complaint. Drought has struck the land, and he sees a link between the people's sin and the withering fields, as Hosea did (Hos 4:1–3). Creation suffers under human sin (Gen 3:17; Rom 8:20–22).
- **"He will not see our latter end" (v4).** "He" may be God, as if he will not notice what becomes of them (Ps 10:11; Ezek 8:12). Or it may be the prophet: "Jeremiah won't live to see us fall," a mocking dismissal of his warnings. The ancient Greek translation reads "God." Either way, the wicked are sure there will be no reckoning.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Jeremiah "arms himself against the temptation… to envy the prosperity of the wicked, before he entered into a parley with it," by first declaring God righteous, and he draws a rule from it: when providence is dark, hold to the general truths about God. JFB says much the same, that it is right "to fortify our minds by justifying God beforehand." Guzik compares the question to Asaph's in Psalm 73 and notes Cundall's observation that Jeremiah, smarting from the plot against his life, wanted God to hurry his judgment. On "He will not see our latter end," JFB prefers the reading that "he" is the prophet, while the Septuagint and others take it as God.

#### The LORD Answers Jeremiah (vv5–17)
- **"If you have raced with men on foot, and they have wearied you, how will you compete with horses?" (v5).** God does not answer the "why." He answers with a question of his own, a proverb of the kind "if this is hard, what about that?" The plot in Anathoth is a footrace. What is coming is a race against horses. Jeremiah would later face beatings and the stocks (20:2), a trial for his life (ch 26), imprisonment (37:15), a muddy cistern (38:6) and the fall of the city. God is not mocking him. He is telling him the truth and calling him to grow stronger.
- **"If in a safe land you are so trusting, what will you do in the thicket of the Jordan?" (v5).** The "thicket of the Jordan" is literally "the pride of the Jordan," the dense jungle of tamarisk, willow and reeds along the river's lower banks. In biblical times lions lived there (49:19; 50:44; Zech 11:3). If Jeremiah stumbles in open country, how will he manage in lion country? The KJV's "the swelling of Jordan" takes the word as the spring flood that overflows the banks (Josh 3:15). Either way, the point is the same: worse is ahead.
- **"Even your brothers and the house of your father… have dealt treacherously with you" (v6).** The plot was closer than Jeremiah knew. His own family was in on it. **"They are in full cry after you"** is a hunting picture, the shout of men driving game. **"Do not believe them, though they speak friendly words to you."** Literally "though they speak good things." They smiled to his face. This is the betrayal of 9:4 ("put no trust in any brother") turned on the prophet himself. Jesus knew the same: "even his brothers did not believe in him" (John 7:5), and his family once tried to take him home, saying "He is out of his mind" (Mark 3:21).
- **"I have forsaken my house; I have abandoned my heritage" (v7).** Now God himself speaks, and the voice is grief. "My house" here is probably the people rather than the temple, matching "my heritage," the people God had chosen as his own (Deut 32:9). **"I have given the beloved of my soul into the hands of her enemies."** The Hebrew word for "beloved" (*yedidut*) is tender and rare. God feels Judah's betrayal the way Jeremiah feels his family's. The prophet's private pain mirrors God's own.
- **"My heritage has become to me like a lion in the forest" (v8).** Judah was meant to be a lion for God (Gen 49:9). Instead she roars at him. **"Therefore I hate her."** In covenant language "hate" often means to reject or turn away from, the opposite of choosing (Mal 1:2–3). It describes an action, God handing her over, not a cold or spiteful feeling, as the grief of v7 makes plain.
- **"Is my heritage to me like a hyena's lair? Are the birds of prey against her all around?" (v9).** The Hebrew is debated. The KJV has "a speckled bird," and the idea would be a bird with odd colors that other birds attack. The ESV follows the Greek translation and the meaning of the related Arabic word, "hyena." Either way, Judah is surrounded by scavengers, and God summons them: **"Go, assemble all the wild beasts; bring them to devour."** The nations around Judah are called in to feed (Isa 56:9).
- **"Many shepherds have destroyed my vineyard" (v10).** The vineyard is a familiar picture of God's people (2:21; Isa 5:1–7; Mark 12:1–9). The "shepherds" are probably the foreign kings and generals who will graze their armies on Judah (6:3), though Judah's own failed leaders had already done much of the damage. **"My pleasant portion"** becomes "a desolate wilderness." The repeated words "desolate… desolation… desolate" in vv10–11 make a heavy drumbeat in Hebrew, hard to catch in English.
- **"Desolate, it mourns to me… but no man lays it to heart" (v11).** The land grieves to God, and God grieves with it. The people do not. That is the tragedy: the only ones mourning are the land and its Maker.
- **"The sword of the LORD devours from one end of the land to the other" (v12).** The invaders are "destroyers," but the sword is the LORD's. The "bare heights in the desert" are the high, open places where Judah had worshiped idols (3:2, 21). Judgment comes exactly where the sin was committed.
- **"They have sown wheat and have reaped thorns" (v13).** All their work produces nothing. Leviticus 26:20 and Deuteronomy 28:38 had warned of this curse (compare Hag 1:6). They shall be "ashamed of their harvests" because God himself is against them.
- **"All my evil neighbors who touch the heritage that I have given my people" (v14).** The surrounding nations, such as Edom, Moab, Ammon and the Philistines, joined in plundering Judah (2 Kgs 24:2; Ezek 25). God will judge them too. Those who touch God's people touch "the apple of his eye" (Zech 2:8).
- **"I will pluck them up… and I will pluck up the house of Judah from among them" (v14).** The same verb is used two ways. The nations will be uprooted from their land in judgment. Judah will be uprooted from among the nations in rescue. "Pluck up" and "plant" are the key words of Jeremiah's call (1:10).
- **"I will again have compassion on them" (v15).** Even the evil neighbors. After judgment God will bring each nation back "to his heritage and… to his land." Chapters 46–49 end several oracles against nations with similar promises (46:26; 48:47; 49:6, 39).
- **"If they will diligently learn the ways of my people" (v16).** The neighbors had taught Judah to swear by Baal. Now, if they learn to swear "As the LORD lives," they will be "built up in the midst of my people." To swear by a god's name was to acknowledge him as the one true witness and judge (4:2). This is a remarkable promise: Gentiles brought into God's people, not as a second-class group but built up among them. Isaiah sees the same (Isa 56:6–8), and it opens the door to the mission to the nations in the New Testament (Eph 2:11–22).
- **"But if any nation will not listen, then I will utterly pluck it up and destroy it" (v17).** The offer is real, and so is the warning. God's mercy to the nations is on the same terms as his mercy to Israel: hear and turn.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, drawing on Morgan and Ryken, takes v5 as God training Jeremiah through lesser trials for greater ones, and passes on Morgan's line that God never sets his servants to contend with horses before he has trained them with footmen. JFB explains the "swelling of Jordan" as both the spring flood and the "wooded banks abounding in lions." On v6 JFB writes, "Godly faithfulness is sure to provoke the ungodly, even of one's own family," and points to Christ's own brothers. JFB calls "beloved of my soul" an "image from a wife," and notes that Judah, by blending paganism into Moses' worship, had become a "speckled" bird. Guzik cites Ryken that God's hatred in v8 means formally rejecting and disinheriting his people for a time, not a violent emotion. On vv14–17 JFB sees a preview of the oracles on the nations in chapters 47–49 and a promise that the nations, "on their conversion," share in the restoration.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah asked why the wicked prosper and the faithful suffer, and God did not explain. The fullest answer came at the cross, where the most faithful man who ever lived suffered and the wicked seemed to win, until God raised him and showed that the end belongs to him (Ps 73:17; Acts 2:23–24). Jesus, too, was betrayed by those close to him and rejected by his own family for a time, and he did not ask for his enemies to be pulled out like sheep for slaughter. He became the Lamb himself and prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). And the promise that the nations could learn to swear "As the LORD lives" and be built up among God's people is fulfilled in him, who has made Jew and Gentile "one new man" and "fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God" (Eph 2:15, 19).
