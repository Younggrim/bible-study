*Setting:* Gideon is dead, and Israel has already turned to "Baal-berith," Baal of the covenant (8:33). Gideon left seventy sons by his wives and one more by a concubine in Shechem, a son he named Abimelech, "my father is king" (8:31). Shechem lay in the central hill country between Mount Gerizim and Mount Ebal, the place where Abraham first built an altar in the land (Gen 12:6–7) and where Joshua renewed the covenant (Josh 24). By now it was a mixed city of Israelites and Canaanites, with a temple to Baal. Chapter 9 is the longest chapter in Judges, and it is unique: no foreign enemy, no cry to the LORD, no deliverer. It is the story of a man who makes himself king by murder, and of how God turns the evil of both king and city back on their own heads.

#### Abimelech's Conspiracy (vv1–21)
- **"I am your bone and your flesh" (v2).** Abimelech plays on family loyalty. His mother was a Shechemite, so he is "one of us," while Gideon's other sons are outsiders from Ophrah. His question, "seventy rulers or one?", is a lie in its framing. Gideon had refused to rule (8:23), and nothing suggests his sons were ruling anyone. JFB calls it "a false insinuation, artfully contrived to stir up jealousy and alarm."
- **"Seventy pieces of silver out of the house of Baal-berith" (v4).** The money comes from Baal's temple. Ancient temples often served as banks. Seventy pieces of silver for seventy sons: one coin per brother. It was a small sum, the price of a band of hired thugs. "Worthless and reckless fellows" is literally "empty and reckless men." Jephthah will gather the same kind of men (11:3), and David later gathers the debtors and discontented (1 Sam 22:2), though for a very different end.
- **"Killed his brothers… seventy men, on one stone" (v5).** Executing them one after another on a single stone suggests a public, perhaps ritual, killing, like a sacrifice. "Seventy" is likely a round number, since Jotham survives. Gideon's sons die at Ophrah, the town where Gideon built his altar "The LORD Is Peace" (6:24). The youngest, **Jotham**, hides, just as his father once hid (6:11). Joash's escape from Athaliah's massacre of the royal family is a later parallel (2 Kgs 11:1–3).
- **"Beth-millo" (v6).** "Millo" means "filling," probably a raised platform or fortified mound, the citadel of the city. The Tyndale notes see dry irony in it: a king without God's call, a priest or the people's real consent, crowned by the "house of filling."
- **"By the oak of the pillar at Shechem" (v6).** This is almost certainly the place where Joshua set up a great stone "under the terebinth that was by the sanctuary of the LORD" as a witness to Israel's covenant (Josh 24:26). Abimelech is crowned on the very spot where Israel once promised to serve the LORD alone.
- **"He went and stood on top of Mount Gerizim" (v7).** Gerizim was the mountain of blessing, facing Ebal, the mountain of cursing (Deut 11:29; 27:12–13). Its slopes form a natural amphitheater above Shechem, and a voice carries well there. Jotham speaks from the mount of blessing, and what he speaks is a curse.
- **The trees seek a king (vv8–15).** This is a fable, a story where plants talk, rare in the Bible (compare 2 Kgs 14:9). The olive, the fig and the vine were the three great crops of the land. Each refuses to "hold sway over the trees." The verb means to wave or sway back and forth: to stand above everyone else, waving in the wind, and produce nothing. Each tree would rather keep doing its fruitful work.
- **"Then all the trees said to the bramble" (v14).** The bramble (Hebrew *'atad*, a thornbush, possibly buckthorn) is low, prickly, fruitless and good for nothing but kindling. It offers its "shade" (v15), which is a joke: a thornbush has no shade worth sitting in. And it threatens fire, since dry thornbushes catch fire easily and could set a whole hillside burning, even the great cedars of Lebanon. The point: the worthy decline to rule, and the worthless grab the job, and a worthless ruler destroys those who choose him.
- **"If you acted in good faith and integrity" (vv16–20).** Jotham applies the story. He lists what Gideon did for them ("risked his life and delivered you") and what they did in return. If this was honest, enjoy each other. If not, "let fire come out from Abimelech and devour the leaders of Shechem… and let fire come out from the leaders of Shechem… and devour Abimelech." He calls Abimelech "the son of his female servant" (v18), a deliberate put-down.
- **"Jotham ran away and fled and went to Beer" (v21).** Beer means "well." Its location is unknown, though JFB guesses a village north of Jerusalem. Jotham's curse is the last thing he says. Not another word from him is recorded. The rest of the chapter shows God carrying it out.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "God was not consulted whether they should have any king, much less who it should be." He also says, "Those who bear fruit for the public good, are justly respected and honoured by all that are wise." Guzik points out that Abimelech was crowned where Joshua had set up the stone of the covenant, and that Shechem backed him because it served their interests, not because it was right. JFB calls the hired men "idle, worthless vagabonds, the scum of society" and favors the view that the seventy were killed as on an altar. The Tyndale notes add that the trees preferred a bad king to none at all, the same instinct Israel shows later (1 Sam 8:19–20).

#### The Downfall of Abimelech (vv22–57)
- **"Abimelech ruled over Israel three years" (v22).** The verb is not the usual word for reigning as king but one meaning to act as a ruler or prince. His "Israel" was probably only the area around Shechem. Three years was all the time it took.
- **"God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the leaders of Shechem" (v23).** This is a hard phrase. "Evil" can mean harmful or troubling, not necessarily morally evil. God sent a spirit of hostility and distrust, as he later sends a troubling spirit on Saul (1 Sam 16:14). The point is that God is in charge even of the falling-out between wicked partners. He uses their own treachery to judge them. The text gives the reason plainly: "that the violence done to the seventy sons… might come" (v24). Blood calls for justice (Gen 4:10; 9:6).
- **Ambushes on the mountaintops (v25).** Shechem sat on the main north–south road through the hills. Robbing travelers hurt Abimelech's income from trade and tolls and showed he could not keep order.
- **Gaal the son of Ebed (vv26–29).** Ebed means "slave," so his name sounds like "son of a slave," another joke at his expense. At the grape harvest festival in "the house of their god," with plenty of new wine, he boasts. "Serve the men of Hamor the father of Shechem" (v28) appeals to the old Canaanite founding family of the city (Gen 33:19; 34). Gaal mocks Abimelech as half an outsider, "the son of Jerubbaal," the same argument Abimelech used against his brothers. "Would that this people were under my hand!" is how Abimelech's own story began.
- **Zebul (vv30–33).** Abimelech's officer in the city hears the drunken talk and secretly sends Abimelech a plan: come by night, set an ambush, and strike at sunrise.
- **"You mistake the shadow of the mountains for men" (v36).** Zebul plays with Gaal at the city gate while Abimelech's men come down the hillsides in the early light, stalling him until it is too late. Then the taunt: "Where is your mouth now?" (v38). The "Diviners' Oak" (v37) was probably a tree where fortune-tellers practiced. Gaal goes out, loses, and is driven from the city.
- **Abimelech destroys Shechem (vv42–45).** The next day the people go out to the fields, maybe to finish the harvest, thinking the trouble is over. Abimelech ambushes them, takes the city, kills its people and "razed the city and sowed it with salt." Salt kills the soil. It was a symbolic curse of permanent ruin (compare Deut 29:23). The king destroys the city that crowned him. Fire has come out of Abimelech.
- **The Tower of Shechem (vv46–49).** Its leaders take refuge in "the stronghold of the house of El-berith," the temple of the "god of the covenant" (El and Baal are both used). Their god does not save them. Abimelech cuts brushwood on Mount Zalmon (probably part of Mount Ebal, the mount of cursing) and burns the stronghold over about 1,000 men and women. "What you have seen me do, hurry and do as I have done" (v48) is a dark echo of Gideon's "Look at me, and do likewise" (7:17). The son copies the father's leadership for slaughter. Archaeologists at Tell Balata, ancient Shechem, found a large fortress-temple destroyed in roughly this period, and the Tyndale notes connect it with this event.
- **Thebez (vv50–52).** Thebez was a town a few miles from Shechem. Abimelech tries the same trick at its tower, moving close to the door to burn it.
- **"A certain woman threw an upper millstone on Abimelech's head" (v53).** Women ground grain daily with a hand mill: a heavy lower stone and a smaller upper stone they rubbed back and forth. The upper stone could be carried, and dropped. The man who killed seventy brothers "on one stone" dies by one stone, thrown by a nameless woman.
- **"Lest they say of me, 'A woman killed him'" (v54).** To die at a woman's hand was a disgrace (compare Sisera in 4:9, 21). He has his armor-bearer finish him. It did not work. Generations later Joab remembers exactly how Abimelech died: "Did not a woman cast an upper millstone on him from the wall?" (2 Sam 11:21).
- **"Everyone departed to his home" (v55).** No funeral, no successor, no rest for the land. The kingdom just dissolves.
- **"Thus God returned the evil of Abimelech… and upon them came the curse of Jotham" (vv56–57).** The narrator states the meaning plainly. These verses and vv23–24 frame the whole story: what looked like politics, ambition and bad luck was God repaying evil. Fire came out from both, as Jotham said.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "The prosperity of the wicked is short and fickle," and "When God uses men as instruments in his hand to do his work, he means one thing, and they another." On the end: "Though wickedness may prosper a while, it will not prosper always." JFB reads the evil spirit providentially, as discontent and rebellion sent as punishment, and calls Gaal a "boastful, impudent, and cowardly person." JFB says the word for Abimelech's rule implies despotism. The lexicon supports a word for ruling as a prince, but "despotism" is JFB's reading of the story, not the meaning of the word itself. Guzik draws the lesson that those who follow a man who rises by violence will face that violence themselves, and that the only safe tower is the name of the LORD (Prov 18:10). He also notes God repays wickedness in this life, the next, or both.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jotham's fable asks what kind of king a people will choose. Shechem chose a bramble, a man who seized power by bloodshed and offered "shade" that was really fire, and he destroyed them. Jesus is the opposite in every way. Abimelech killed his brothers to take a throne. Jesus "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Heb 2:11) and laid down his life for them. Abimelech bought followers with temple silver. Jesus was betrayed for thirty pieces of it. Abimelech was crowned at the covenant stone and broke the covenant. Jesus made the new covenant in his own blood. The true King's shade is real: "the shade of a great rock in a weary land" (Isa 32:2). And God's justice, so plain in this chapter, fell on Christ for all who take refuge in him.
