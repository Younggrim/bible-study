*Setting:* Babylonia, around 586 BC, the year Jerusalem fell. This is the second of three oracles against Tyre (chs 26–28). Chapter 26 announced the city's destruction. Chapter 27 turns the same message into a *qinah*, a funeral lament, sung before the death has happened. Ezekiel pictures Tyre as a magnificent merchant ship, built from the finest materials of the known world, crewed by the best sailors and loaded with every kind of cargo, and then sunk by a storm with everyone aboard. In the middle of the poem (vv12–24) is one of the most detailed trade lists to survive from the ancient world, naming more than twenty peoples and places from Spain to Persia and from Turkey to Arabia. It shows how far Tyre's reach went. Lament songs like this were a known form. The prophets sometimes used them over enemies with a note of irony, mourning in advance for a fall that was certain.

#### A Lament for Tyre (vv1–36)
- **"Raise a lamentation over Tyre" (v2).** A lament praised the dead before mourning their loss. JFB notes that this one praises Tyre's greatness precisely to sharpen the contrast between what it was and what it will become.
- **"Who dwells at the entrances to the sea, merchant of the peoples to many coastlands" (v3).** Island Tyre had two harbors, one facing north and one facing south, so ships could enter from either direction. "Dwells" can also carry the sense of sitting enthroned. Tyre sat at the gates of the sea and ruled them. The words "merchant," "merchandise" and "trade" run through the chapter more than twenty times.
- **"O Tyre, you have said, 'I am perfect in beauty'" (v3).** This is the root sin. "Perfect in beauty" (*kelilat yophi*) is exactly the phrase used of Jerusalem: "Zion, the perfection of beauty" (Ps 50:2), "the city that was called the perfection of beauty" (Lam 2:15). God had made Jerusalem beautiful with his own splendor (16:14). Tyre claimed that title for itself and made it with its own wealth. Henry: "It is the folly of the children of this world to value themselves on the pomp and pleasure they live in… and, if in these they excel others, to think themselves perfect."
- **"Your borders are in the heart of the seas" (v4).** The island city really was in the sea. In the poem, Tyre becomes the ship itself.
- **The ship's timber (vv5–6).** Every part comes from the best source. The planks are "fir trees from Senir," an old name for Mount Hermon (Deut 3:9). The mast is "a cedar from Lebanon," the most prized timber in the region, used for Solomon's temple. The oars are "oaks of Bashan," the fertile region east of the Sea of Galilee famous for its oaks (Isa 2:13). The deck is "pines from the coasts of Cyprus, inlaid with ivory." The Hebrew of that last line is uncertain. The older KJV reading "the company of the Ashurites" comes from dividing the consonants differently. Most modern versions read a word for a kind of cypress or boxwood instead.
- **"Of fine embroidered linen from Egypt was your sail, serving as your banner" (v7).** Egyptian linen was the finest in the world. Ancient sails were sometimes embroidered or dyed, and the sail served as the ship's flag. The awning was "blue and purple from the coasts of Elishah," probably Cyprus or another island to the west. Purple dye was Tyre's own famous product, made from murex shellfish. It was so costly that purple became the color of kings.
- **The crew (vv8–9).** The rowers came from Sidon and Arvad, other Phoenician cities. Sidon was the older and once the greater city, and now its men pulled Tyre's oars. The pilots, who steered, were Tyre's own "skilled men." The caulkers, who sealed the seams with pitch, were "the elders of Gebal," the city the Greeks called Byblos, famous for its craftsmen (1 Kings 5:18). The best of the region all served this one ship.
- **"Persia and Lud and Put were in your army as your men of war" (v10).** Tyre hired soldiers from the edges of the known world: Persia far to the east, Lud (probably Lydia in western Turkey) and Put (Libya) far to the west and south. **"They hung the shield and helmet in you; they gave you splendor."** Soldiers hung their shields on city walls as decoration and a show of strength (Song 4:4). **"Men of Arvad and Helech… men of Gamad" (v11)** guarded the walls. Helech is probably Cilicia in southeast Turkey. Gamad is uncertain.
- **"They made perfect your beauty" (vv4, 11).** The phrase frames the first part of the poem. Everything Tyre had was beautiful, and all of it was bought.
- **The trade list (vv12–24).** Here the poem changes to prose and reads like a merchant's ledger. It runs roughly from the far west to the east and south:
- **"Tarshish… silver, iron, tin, and lead" (v12).** Tarshish was far to the west, most likely Tartessus in southern Spain, rich in metals. Jonah tried to sail there (Jonah 1:3), and "ships of Tarshish" became a name for large ocean-going trading vessels (v25; 1 Kings 10:22).
- **"Javan, Tubal, and Meshech… exchanged human beings and vessels of bronze" (v13).** Javan is Ionia, the Greek world. Tubal and Meshech were in central and eastern Turkey (they return in 38:2). Among the cargo are slaves. Amos had already condemned Tyre for delivering "a whole people to Edom" as slaves (Amos 1:9), and Joel for selling people of Judah to the Greeks (Joel 3:4–6). The list treats people as one more commodity, next to bronze bowls.
- **"Beth-togarmah… horses, war horses, and mules" (v14).** Togarmah was in eastern Turkey or Armenia, a region famous for horses.
- **"Dedan… ivory tusks and ebony" (v15).** Ivory and ebony came from Africa or India. The Greek text reads "Rhodes" for Dedan here, which may fit the westward context better, since the Arabian Dedan appears in v20.
- **"Syria… emeralds, purple, embroidered work, fine linen, coral, and ruby" (v16).** Many Hebrew manuscripts and the Greek read "Edom" instead of "Syria" (Hebrew *Aram*). The two names differ by one letter that is easily confused in Hebrew script. Several of the precious stones in this verse are uncertain.
- **"Judah and the land of Israel… wheat of Minnith, meal, honey, oil, and balm" (v17).** Israel's place in the list is modest: farm produce. Tyre, on its small island, always depended on its neighbors for food. Hiram received wheat and oil from Solomon (1 Kings 5:11), and centuries later Tyre and Sidon still depended on Herod's territory for food (Acts 12:20). Minnith was a town in the region of Ammon (Judg 11:33). The word translated "meal" (*pannag*) appears only here, and its meaning is uncertain.
- **"Damascus… wine of Helbon and wool of Sahar" (v18).** Helbon, north of Damascus, was famous for its wine. Greek writers say the Persian kings drank it. **"Casks of wine from Uzal… wrought iron, cassia, and calamus" (v19).** The Hebrew of this verse is uncertain. Older versions read "Dan also and Javan." Cassia and calamus were fragrant spices used in perfumes and in the holy anointing oil (Exod 30:23–24).
- **Arabia (vv20–22).** Dedan, an oasis in northern Arabia, sold saddlecloths. Kedar, the Arab tribes descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13), sold sheep and goats. Sheba and Raamah in southern Arabia sold "the best of all kinds of spices and all precious stones and gold." The queen of Sheba brought Solomon the same goods (1 Kings 10:2, 10).
- **Mesopotamia (vv23–24).** Haran, where Abraham's family settled (Gen 11:31), Canneh, Eden, Asshur (Assyria) and Chilmad traded in "choice garments… carpets of colored material, bound with cords." The Tyndale notes suggest Chilmad may be a copying slip for "all Media."
- **"So you were filled and heavily laden in the heart of the seas" (v25).** The poem returns from the ledger to the ship. It is full, low in the water, carrying the wealth of the world.
- **"Your rowers have brought you out into the high seas. The east wind has wrecked you" (v26).** In one line the voyage ends. The east wind, blowing hot and hard off the desert, was the most dangerous wind for Mediterranean ships. "By the east wind you shattered the ships of Tarshish" (Ps 48:7). Paul's ship was wrecked by a similar violent wind, the "northeaster" (Acts 27:14). In the poem the east wind stands for Babylon, coming from the east. The rowers who brought the ship into deep water brought it to its death. Henry: "It is ill with a people when those that sit at the stern, instead of putting them into the harbour, run them aground."
- **"Sink into the heart of the seas on the day of your fall" (v27).** Everything listed earlier goes down: riches, wares, sailors, pilots, caulkers, merchants, soldiers. The long list in v27 mirrors the long lists before. All the people and goods that made Tyre great go down with it.
- **"At the sound of the cry of your pilots the countryside shakes" (v28).** The pilots' last cry is heard on shore. The "countryside" may be the mainland villages and pastures near Tyre.
- **The mourners (vv29–31).** Sailors of other ships come ashore and perform every ritual of grief: crying aloud, throwing dust on their heads, rolling in ashes, shaving their heads, wearing sackcloth. Shaving the head for the dead was a pagan custom forbidden to Israel (Deut 14:1), which JFB notes as a Phoenician practice. The grief is real, but much of it is for themselves. Tyre's fall is their loss of business.
- **"Who is like Tyre, like one destroyed in the midst of the sea?" (v32).** A lament within the lament. "Who is like…?" was normally a question of praise, asked of God: "Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?" (Exod 15:11). Here it is asked of a wreck. The Hebrew word for "destroyed" may also be read "silenced." JFB renders it "one brought to death's stillness," in contrast to the noisy, crowded port of v27.
- **"You enriched the kings of the earth" (v33).** Tyre's trade made other kings rich through duties and profits. That is why they mourn.
- **"The hair of their kings bristles with horror… The merchants among the peoples hiss at you" (vv35–36).** Hissing or whistling was a sign of shock and dismay at a ruin (1 Kings 9:8; Jer 19:8), not necessarily of mockery. **"You have come to a dreadful end and shall be no more forever."** The same ending as 26:21 and 28:19 closes each of the three oracles.
- **Revelation 18.** John's vision of the fall of "Babylon the great" draws heavily on this chapter. Kings and merchants weep. A long list of cargo is given, "gold, silver, jewels, pearls, fine linen, purple… slaves, that is, human souls." Sea captains and sailors stand far off, throw dust on their heads and cry, "What city was like the great city?" (Rev 18:9–19). Ezekiel's Tyre becomes the pattern for every proud, God-forgetting economic power.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws several lessons. "The most mighty and magnificent kingdoms and states, sooner or later, have their day to come down." "Those who make creatures their confidence… will of course fall with them." And, more gently: "We ought to mourn for the miseries of other nations, as well as for our own," even those they brought on themselves. JFB works hard on the geography, identifying Tarshish with Tartessus in Spain, Javan with the Ionian Greeks, Tubal and Meshech with peoples near the Black Sea, and suggesting that tin came to Tarshish from as far as Cornwall. He explains the "east wind" as the violent Levanter. Guzik notes that God's name never appears in the whole merchant list: it is all trade, all self, with no thought of the Creator. He also draws the Revelation 18 parallel and, citing Taylor, observes that the kings' terror is really selfish fear of what Tyre's fall will mean for them.

#### Pointing to Christ
Tyre's ship had everything money could buy: cedar from Lebanon, linen from Egypt, the best crews in the world, and a hold full of silver, spices and slaves. One east wind sent it to the bottom. Jesus asked the question that hangs over this chapter: "What will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" (Matt 16:26). He also told of a rich man who filled his barns and said to his soul, "Relax, eat, drink, be merry," and God said, "Fool! This night your soul is required of you" (Luke 12:19–20). And in a boat on the Sea of Galilee, when the storm came and the disciples cried that they were perishing, Jesus "rebuked the wind and the raging waves, and they ceased" (Luke 8:24). Tyre went down because it trusted its own ship. The safety Jesus offers is to have him in the boat. Revelation's echo of this chapter adds one more contrast: among Babylon's cargo were "human souls," bought and sold. Christ is the one who buys people not to sell them but to set them free, "ransomed… by his blood… from every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:9).
