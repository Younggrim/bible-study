*Setting:* Israel is a few days' march out from Sinai, camped at Hazeroth (11:35), somewhere in the Sinai peninsula on the way north to Kadesh. Chapter 11 told of grumbling at the edges of the camp, a craving for meat, and seventy elders sharing the Spirit that was on Moses. Now the trouble comes from the inside of Moses' own family. Miriam and Aaron are his older sister and brother. Miriam led Israel's women in song at the Red Sea and is called a prophetess (Exod 15:20). Aaron is the high priest. Micah 6:4 remembers all three as the leaders God sent: "Moses, Aaron, and Miriam." This short chapter is about a challenge to Moses' unique place, God's answer to it, and the way Moses responds to the people who wronged him.

#### Miriam and Aaron Oppose Moses (vv1–16)
- **"Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses" (v1).** The verb is feminine singular in Hebrew, literally "she spoke, Miriam and Aaron." Miriam is also named first, which is unusual. Both details point to her as the one who started it, which explains why the judgment falls on her (v10).
- **"Because of the Cushite woman whom he had married" (v1).** Who was she? Two main views. (1) She is Zipporah, the Midianite wife of Exodus 2:21. "Cushan" is paired with Midian in Habakkuk 3:7, so "Cushite" may be an old name for a people in that region. (2) She is a second wife, a Cushite in the usual sense, from Cush south of Egypt (modern Sudan), who perhaps came out of Egypt with the "mixed multitude" (Exod 12:38). Zipporah had been sent back to her father for a time (Exod 18:2), and some think she had since died. The text does not settle it. Either way the complaint is about a foreign wife, and it is only the stated reason.
- **"Has the LORD indeed spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken through us also?" (v2).** Here is the real issue. The wife was a pretext. The question is status. Miriam and Aaron had both spoken for God (Exod 4:15–16; 15:20), and they had just watched seventy elders receive a share of Moses' Spirit (11:25). If God speaks through so many, why should Moses stand alone? Petty criticism about something a person cannot change is often cover for jealousy about something else.
- **"And the LORD heard it" (v2).** Four short words, and a warning. The same phrase came before the fire at Taberah (11:1). What is said quietly in the family tent is said in God's hearing.
- **"Now the man Moses was very meek, more than all people who were on the face of the earth" (v3).** "Meek" is *anav*, which the lexicon gives as "humble, lowly, meek," and in other places "poor" or "afflicted." It is the word behind "the meek shall inherit the land" (Ps 37:11), which Jesus echoes in Matthew 5:5. Meekness here is not weakness. Moses could be fierce (Exod 32:19). It means he did not defend his own position. That is why the next verse has God answering instead of Moses.
- **Did Moses write this about himself?** If he did, it is an odd thing for a humble man to say. Some answer that Moses wrote it as a plain fact, under inspiration, the way Paul could call himself "the very least of all the saints" and still describe his labors (Eph 3:8; 1 Cor 15:10). Many conservative scholars think it is a short note added later by an inspired editor, perhaps Joshua, just as Moses' death and burial are recorded after he died (Deut 34). Both views hold the verse to be true. The point it makes is clear: Moses would not fight back, so God did.
- **"Suddenly the LORD said… 'Come out, you three, to the tent of meeting'" (v4).** "Suddenly" stresses how quickly God acted. The tent of meeting at the center of the camp is where Moses usually spoke with God (Exod 33:7–11). All three are summoned, as to a court.
- **"The LORD came down in a pillar of cloud and stood at the entrance of the tent" (v5).** God does not invite Aaron and Miriam in. He comes out to the doorway and calls them forward, the way an accused person is called before a judge.
- **"If there is a prophet among you… in a vision… in a dream" (v6).** God does not deny that Miriam and Aaron have spoken for him. Prophets are real, and visions and dreams are real ways God speaks (Joel 2:28). But there are degrees.
- **"Not so with my servant Moses. He is faithful in all my house" (v7).** "My servant" is a title of honor, used of Abraham, David and finally the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah. God's "house" here is his household, his people Israel, which Moses was trusted to oversee. Hebrews 3:2–6 quotes this verse: Moses was faithful in God's house "as a servant," but Christ is faithful over God's house "as a son."
- **"Mouth to mouth, clearly, and not in riddles" (v8).** Elsewhere it is "face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exod 33:11; Deut 34:10). Both are idioms for direct, personal speech, not literal sight of God's face, which no one can see and live (Exod 33:20). "Riddles" is the word for dark sayings that need interpreting. Other prophets received visions to be decoded. Moses received plain words.
- **"He beholds the form of the LORD" (v8).** "Form" is *temunah*, "likeness" or "form." It is the word in the second commandment for images (Exod 20:4), and Deuteronomy 4:12, 15 says the people at Sinai "saw no form; there was only a voice." Moses saw more, something of God's glory passing by (Exod 33:18–23), though still not God's full being.
- **"Why then were you not afraid to speak against my servant Moses?" (v8).** To oppose the one God has appointed is to oppose God. The question hangs in the air, and then **"he departed"** (v9). The cloud lifts, and its absence is itself the verdict.
- **"Behold, Miriam was leprous, like snow" (v10).** The Hebrew *tsara'at*, traditionally "leprosy," covers a range of skin diseases (Lev 13), not only what doctors now call Hansen's disease. "Like snow" describes white, flaking skin, the same phrase used when Moses' hand turned diseased as a sign (Exod 4:6). A disease that made someone unclean and shut them out of the camp (5:2–3) was a fitting judgment on someone who tried to push her way to the center.
- **Why not Aaron?** He sinned too, and he says so ("we have done foolishly," v11). Miriam was the instigator, which is the simplest answer. Some also note that a diseased high priest could not serve (Lev 21:16–23), so Aaron's punishment was to watch his sister and know it should have been him as well.
- **"Oh, my lord, do not punish us" (v11).** Aaron, who had just questioned Moses' standing, now calls him "my lord" and asks him to intercede. It is a quick turnaround. Aaron has a history of going along with whoever is pressing him (Exod 32:1–5), and he does it here too, though this time in the right direction.
- **"Let her not be as one dead… half eaten away" (v12).** The picture is of a stillborn child whose body has already begun to decay. Advanced skin disease looked like living death, and that is how Aaron sees his sister.
- **"O God, please heal her—please" (v13).** The shortest prayer of Moses recorded in the Bible, five words in Hebrew, with "please" at both ends. He does not hesitate, lecture or wait. He prays at once for the sister who has just attacked him. It is the meekness of v3 in action.
- **"If her father had but spit in her face, should she not be shamed seven days?" (v14).** Spitting in someone's face was a deep public insult (Deut 25:9; Isa 50:6). God's point is from the lesser to the greater: a father's rebuke would bring a week of disgrace, so how much more a rebuke from God? He grants the healing, but the discipline stands. Seven days outside the camp was the normal period for a person being declared clean from a skin disease (Lev 13:4–5; 14:8). Forgiveness and consequence go together here.
- **"The people did not set out on the march till Miriam was brought in again" (v15).** The whole nation waited a week for her. Her sin was public and so was her shame, but she was not abandoned. The camp would not move on without her, and she was restored to her place. She lived on until Kadesh, where she died and was buried (20:1). Moses later tells Israel, "Remember what the LORD your God did to Miriam on the way" (Deut 24:9), as a warning in the laws about skin disease.
- **"Set out from Hazeroth, and camped in the wilderness of Paran" (v16).** This brings Israel near Kadesh, on the southern edge of Canaan (13:26). The next scene is the sending of the spies.
- **Commentators:** Henry frames the chapter as Moses' patience being "tried by his own relations," and concludes that "the best persons and families have both their follies and their crosses." On v4 he notes that Moses neither resented the wrong nor complained to God, yet God took it up, and draws the lesson that the more silent we are in our own cause, the more God is engaged to plead it. Guzik lays out the possible identities of the Cushite wife, points out that the complaint was about something she could not help, and thinks v3 was likely added by a later hand such as Joshua. He describes Aaron's confession as sincere but driven largely by fear for himself, and sees Moses' silence and prayer as a picture of Christ, who did not open his mouth (Isa 53:7). JFB says Miriam is named first as "the chief instigator and leader of the sedition," suggests v3 may have been added "as a gloss by Ezra or some later prophet," and reads "the form of the LORD" as "some unmistakable evidence of His glorious presence," not God's essence. JFB also suggests that Aaron was spared the disease because it would have interrupted his priesthood. The Tyndale notes favor Zipporah as the Cushite woman and point out that rebellion has now reached the highest level of Israel's leadership.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses was meek, faithful in God's house, and spoke with God as a friend. He took his siblings' attack without hitting back and prayed at once for the one who wronged him. Hebrews takes this very chapter and says Christ is greater: Moses was a faithful servant in the house, and Christ is the Son over it (Heb 3:5–6). Jesus called himself "gentle and lowly in heart" (Matt 11:29), was mocked and spit upon without answering, and prayed for those who did it (Luke 23:34). And where Miriam was shut outside the camp in shame and then brought back, Jesus "suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:12) so that people who deserve to be put outside can be brought in.
