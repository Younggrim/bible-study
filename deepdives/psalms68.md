*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David. A Song." Psalm 68 is one of the grandest and hardest psalms in the Bible. Adam Clarke, cited by Guzik, called it the most difficult in the Psalter, and Henry admits that "in many places the genuine sense is not easy to come at." Its Hebrew has more rare words than almost any other passage, some found nowhere else. But the big picture is clear. The psalm is a victory march. It follows God as the Divine Warrior from Sinai, through the wilderness, into Canaan, and up to his chosen mountain, Zion, where he takes his throne and the nations bring him tribute. Most interpreters, Henry and Guzik among them, connect it with the day David brought the ark of the covenant up to Jerusalem (2 Sam 6), because the opening line is Moses' prayer whenever the ark set out (Num 10:35). JFB thinks more generally of David's victories that gave the nation rest. Paul later quotes verse 18 of Christ's ascension (Eph 4:8), and the church has long read the psalm at Ascension and Pentecost. Guzik notes the Anglican lectionary set it for Whitsunday (Pentecost).

#### God Shall Arise (vv1–3)
- **"God shall arise, his enemies shall be scattered" (v1).** These are almost exactly the words Moses said each time the ark set out in the wilderness: "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered, and let those who hate you flee before you" (Num 10:35). The ark was God's throne on earth, and its march was his march. By starting here, David ties the ark's journey up to Zion to the whole exodus journey. Guzik compares it to a speaker opening with a famous line everyone recognizes.
- **"As smoke is driven away… as wax melts before fire" (v2).** Two pictures of things that seem solid and then simply vanish. The wicked can look strong, but before God they have no more substance than smoke in the wind or a candle in a furnace (37:20; Mic 1:4). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that wax is hard on its own and soft only when the fire comes near.
- **"But the righteous shall be glad" (v3).** The same presence that drives the wicked away makes the righteous rejoice. JFB catches the contrast: in v2 "before him" means fleeing from his presence as something dreaded; in v3 it means standing in his presence "as under His protection." The Hebrew piles up three words for joy: glad, exult, jubilant.
- **Commentators:** Henry says David "does… comment upon Moses's prayer… to direct us how to make use of scripture-prayers." He also draws a careful line: "Though we are to pray for our enemies as such, yet we are to pray against God's enemies as such, against their enmity to him." Guzik applies v1 to the resurrection, when God arose in Christ and his enemies were scattered.

#### Father of the Fatherless (vv4–6)
- **"Lift up a song to him who rides through the deserts" (v4).** Two puzzles hide in this line. The verb (*salal*) usually means to build up a road or highway, so the KJV and JFB read "cast up a highway for him who rides through the deserts," like Isaiah's "prepare the way of the LORD… make straight in the desert a highway" (Isa 40:3). VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, agrees on the root. The ESV takes it as "lift up" a song. Second, "rides through the deserts" (*'aravot*) sounds very like a famous title of Baal in Canaanite poetry from Ugarit: "Rider of the Clouds." Many scholars think the psalm deliberately takes Baal's title and gives it to the true God, and the KJV translates it "rideth upon the heavens." The Tyndale notes take this view (compare v33; 104:3; Deut 33:26). Either way the point is the same. The LORD, not Baal, is the one who rides out in power, whether over the desert march or across the sky.
- **"His name is the LORD" (v4).** Here the Hebrew is *Yah*, the short form of *YHWH*, the name in "Hallelu-*jah*" ("praise Yah"). It first appears in the Song of the Sea: "The LORD (*Yah*) is my strength and my song" (Exod 15:2).
- **"Father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (v5).** After the image of the cosmic rider comes the tenderest line in the psalm. The God who rides the heavens bends down to the people nobody else protects. "Protector" is literally "judge" (*dayyan*), one who takes up their case in court. In Israel, orphans and widows had no man to stand for them at the city gate, so God himself takes that place (Deut 10:18; Ps 146:9). James echoes this: true religion is "to visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (Jas 1:27). Boice, cited by Guzik, notes that earthly rulers surround themselves with the powerful, but God gathers the miserable around himself.
- **"God settles the solitary in a home; he leads out the prisoners to prosperity" (v6).** The lonely get a family, the captives get freedom. JFB: "literally, 'settleth the lonely'… 'at home.'" Behind this stands the exodus: Israel had been a nation of slaves in Egypt, and God led them out (Exod 20:2). **"But the rebellious dwell in a parched land"**, like the generation that refused to trust God and died in the desert (Num 14:29–35).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Observe how much God's goodness is his glory… He that rides upon the heavens is a Father worth having." JFB sees "allusion to the wandering and settlement of the Israelites." Henry adds a practical note from Hammond: giving the destitute a way to earn their living "is a very good way for man's charity, as it is of God's bounty."

#### When You Marched Through the Wilderness (vv7–10)
- **"O God, when you went out before your people, when you marched through the wilderness" (v7).** God led Israel himself, in the pillar of cloud and fire (Exod 13:21). "Marched" is a soldier's word. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes that the wilderness years were not aimless wandering but an ordered march. **Selah** pauses the music.
- **"The earth quaked, the heavens poured down rain, before God, the One of Sinai" (v8).** This verse closely echoes the Song of Deborah (Judg 5:4–5), as Kidner, cited by Guzik, points out. "The One of Sinai" (literally "this Sinai") is an old title for God as the one who appeared on that mountain in fire, smoke and earthquake (Exod 19:16–18). JFB notes the same wording in Judges 5:5.
- **"Rain in abundance… you restored your inheritance as it languished" (v9).** God's "inheritance" is his people and their land. Some take the rain as the manna and quail "rained" on the camp (78:24, 27); others as the rains that made Canaan fruitful (Deut 11:11). JFB: "a rain of gifts, as manna and quails." Henry and Poole (cited by Guzik) give both options.
- **"Your flock found a dwelling in it… you provided for the needy" (v10).** "Flock" (*chayyah*) usually means a living creature or a band of people. JFB says it means "troop," as in 2 Samuel 23:11, 13. God settled his troop in the land and cared for its poor.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If God bring his people into a wilderness, he will be sure to go before them in it and bring them out of it." He sees the plentiful rain as a picture of the Spirit and the gospel, and of Christ himself, who comes "like rain that falls on the mown grass" (72:6).

#### The Women Who Announce the News (vv11–14)
- **"The Lord gives the word; the women who announce the news are a great host" (v11).** The participle is feminine: women heralds. The verb (*basar*) means to bring good news, especially news of victory (1 Sam 31:9). It is the verb behind Isaiah's "herald of good news" to Zion (Isa 40:9), where the herald is also feminine, and in the Greek Old Testament it is the word that gives us "evangelize." In Israel, women met returning armies with songs, as Miriam did at the sea (Exod 15:20–21) and the women did after David killed Goliath (1 Sam 18:6–7). JFB and the Tyndale notes both see a "choir of females, celebrating victory." The KJV's "great was the company of those that published it" made this a favorite missionary text (Handel set it in *Messiah*).
- **"The kings of the armies—they flee, they flee!" (v12).** The women's song. The doubled verb pictures headlong panic.
- **"The women at home divide the spoil" (v12).** The victory was so complete that women who never left home share the plunder. JFB: "the ease of victory appears in that such, without danger, quietly enjoyed the spoils."
- **"Though you men lie among the sheepfolds" (v13).** Another echo of Deborah's song, which rebuked the tribe of Reuben for staying "among the sheepfolds" instead of joining the battle (Judg 5:16). The word is rare and could also mean "between the saddlebags" or "the cooking hearths" (KJV "pots"). JFB lists several options and concludes that "a state of quiet and peace is described by a beautiful figure."
- **"The wings of a dove covered with silver, its pinions with shimmering gold" (v13).** One of the most debated pictures in the Psalms. Kidner, cited by Guzik, lists several possibilities: Israel shining in new prosperity; a captured trophy, perhaps an ornament of silver and gold; the shimmer of a dove in sunlight; or the women's new finery from the spoil. The most natural reading is that people who lay in obscurity now shine with splendor. The Tyndale notes suggest herdsmen surprised to receive silver and gold from the battle.
- **"When the Almighty scatters kings there, let snow fall on Zalmon" (v14).** "Almighty" is *Shaddai*, the ancient name by which God appeared to Abraham (Gen 17:1). Zalmon is uncertain. A Mount Zalmon near Shechem appears in Judges 9:48, and the name sounds like the word for "dark" or "shadow." Some place it in Bashan. The picture may be the slain or their scattered weapons lying white on a dark hillside, or snow falling during the rout, or (Maclaren, cited by Guzik) a dark mountain turned white, sorrow turned to joy. Guzik calls the meaning unclear, and it is best to leave it there.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes "the word is feminine" and sees both women prophets and the women singing after victory. He then applies it to the resurrection: the victory "was first notified by the women… to the disciples" (Matt 28:7). Guzik makes the same link, and notes that the resurrection was entrusted first to women. JFB offers various readings of v13 and v14 without forcing one.

#### The Mount That God Desired (vv15–18)
- **"O mountain of God, mountain of Bashan; O many-peaked mountain" (v15).** Bashan was the high, fertile country east of the Sea of Galilee, today's Golan Heights, with Mount Hermon (over 9,000 feet) to its north. "Mountain of God" is probably a Hebrew way of saying "mighty mountain" (36:6). JFB suggests mountains stand for nations here, as often in the Psalms.
- **"Why do you look with hatred… at the mount that God desired for his abode?" (v16).** The rare verb means to look with envy or jealous hostility, like a rival glaring. The great peaks of Bashan are pictured scowling at little Zion, a low ridge barely higher than the hills around it. Zion's only glory is that God chose it (132:13–14). Henry: "It is much more honourable to be holy to God than to be high and great in the world." Trapp, cited by Guzik, compares Aaron's rod that budded: what makes the difference is God's choosing (Num 17).
- **"The chariots of God are twice ten thousand, thousands upon thousands" (v17).** Israel was forbidden to build up chariot forces (Deut 17:16), but God has an army beyond counting. "Thousands upon thousands" is literally "thousands of repetition." JFB notes that the KJV's "angels" is not in the Hebrew but implied (compare Deut 33:2; 2 Kgs 6:17).
- **"The Lord is among them; Sinai is now in the sanctuary" (v17).** The Hebrew reads tersely, "the Lord among them, Sinai, in holiness." The ESV's sense is that the holy presence that once blazed on Sinai has now moved to Zion. Henry: "the sanctuary was to Israel instead of Mount Sinai."
- **"You ascended on high, leading a host of captives in your train and receiving gifts among men" (v18).** The picture is of a conquering king going up to his capital after victory, leading prisoners of war in procession and receiving tribute. "Ascended on high" means God going up to Zion with the ark (47:5). "Even among the rebellious" means even defeated enemies must bring gifts.
- **Paul's use of v18.** Paul quotes this verse of Christ's ascension: "When he ascended on high he led a host of captives, and he gave gifts to men" (Eph 4:8). Paul says "gave" where the Hebrew has "received." Paul was not careless. An Aramaic paraphrase (Targum) and the Syriac version of the psalm also read "gave," which suggests a known Jewish reading. And a conqueror receives spoils in order to distribute them. Paul then lists the gifts: apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers (Eph 4:11). Readers differ on who the "captives" are in Paul's use: defeated spiritual powers (Col 2:15), or people rescued from captivity and now in Christ's train.
- **Commentators:** JFB: "The Psalmist uses 'receiving' as evincing the success, Paul 'gave' as the act, of the conqueror, who, having subdued his enemies, proceeds to reward his friends." Henry: "he received that he might give," and "He led captivity itself captive… As he was the death of death, so he was the captivity of captivity" (Hos 13:14). He notes the gifts were "for the rebellious also… This magnifies the grace of Christ exceedingly that through him rebels are… not only pardoned, but preferred." Guzik accepts Paul's change as Spirit-led without arguing from the Hebrew.

#### Our God Is a God of Salvation (vv19–23)
- **"Blessed be the Lord, who daily bears us up" (v19).** The KJV has "who daily loadeth us with benefits." The Hebrew verb means to carry a load, and it can be read either way: God carries our burdens, or God loads us with good things. Most modern translations take the first sense, picturing God carrying his people like a father (Deut 1:31; Isa 46:3–4). The Tyndale notes take it that way. **Selah.**
- **"To GOD, the Lord, belong deliverances from death" (v20).** Literally "the exits from death," the ways out. Guzik notes the unusual double name "Yahweh Adonai." The ways out of death are in God's hand alone (Rev 1:18).
- **"God will strike the heads of his enemies, the hairy crown of him who walks in his guilty ways" (v21).** Long, uncut hair could mark a warrior under a vow, or simply a fierce, wild appearance (Deut 32:42). Poole, cited by Guzik, thinks of enemies who grew their hair long to look frightening. Henry wonders about Absalom, "whose bushy hair was his halter." The Tyndale notes and Guzik hear an echo of Genesis 3:15, where the woman's offspring crushes the serpent's head.
- **"I will bring them back from Bashan… from the depths of the sea" (v22).** Bashan was the far north-east, the land of King Og (Num 21:33). The sea is the lowest depth. Wherever the enemy hides, high or low, God will bring them back for judgment (Amos 9:2–3). JFB instead reads it of God bringing his own people back from every extremity. The ESV's "them" leaves both readings possible.
- **"That you may strike your feet in their blood… the tongues of your dogs" (v23).** Harsh battlefield language, like the dogs that licked Ahab's blood (1 Kgs 22:38) and Jezebel's (2 Kgs 9:36). JFB: these figures "denote the completeness of the conquest, not implying any savage cruelty." The Bible's final victory scene uses the same picture of a robe dipped in blood (Isa 63:1–6; Rev 19:13).
- **Commentators:** Henry sets vv19–21 side by side as blessing and curse: "He that believes shall be saved; he that believes not shall be damned." On v20 he writes that "the keys of hell and death are put into the hand of the Lord Jesus." JFB: the enemy who "goeth on still" is "perseveringly impenitent," which matches Henry: those "who go on still in their trespasses, and hate to be reformed, God looks upon as his enemies."

#### The Procession of My God, My King (vv24–27)
- **"Your procession is seen, O God… into the sanctuary" (v24).** Now the march reaches its goal. The ark goes up into Zion, and the whole nation watches. "My God, my King" is personal. Kidner, cited by Guzik, calls this the final stage of the exodus: the march that began at the Red Sea now arrives home.
- **"The singers in front, the musicians last, between them virgins playing tambourines" (v25).** The order of the procession, as in 1 Chronicles 15:16–28, where David appointed singers and musicians for the ark. The young women with tambourines recall Miriam again (Exod 15:20).
- **"O you who are of Israel's fountain!" (v26).** Israel's "fountain" means the patriarch Jacob, from whom all the tribes descend (Deut 33:28; Isa 48:1). JFB: "lineal descendants of Jacob."
- **"There is Benjamin, the least of them, in the lead" (v27).** Benjamin was the smallest tribe, nearly wiped out in Judges 20–21, and the tribe of Saul. Yet it leads the march. Guzik sees David's grace to Saul's tribe. Next comes Judah, David's own tribe, then Zebulun and Naphtali from the far north, which led the fight in Deborah's day (Judg 5:18). Two southern and two northern tribes stand for the whole nation, united under God. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, calls the selection poetic.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Benjamin and Judah had once been at war (2 Sam 2–3) "but now they both join in praises." He suggests Zebulun and Naphtali may be named because, lying in the north, they were most exposed to raids and had most reason for thanks. JFB says one of the nearest tribes, one of the greatest, and two of the most remote represent "the whole nation of Israel, passing forward."

#### Kings Shall Bear Gifts to You (vv28–31)
- **"Summon your power, O God, the power, O God, by which you have worked for us" (v28).** The procession turns to prayer. What God has done, let him do again. Henry: "where he has given true grace he will give more grace."
- **"Because of your temple at Jerusalem kings shall bear gifts to you" (v29).** "Temple" (*hekal*) means palace. In David's time the "palace" was the tent he set up for the ark (2 Sam 6:17). Solomon's temple came later, and the Queen of Sheba and other rulers did bring gifts to Jerusalem (1 Kgs 10). Henry notes that David speaks here "in faith."
- **"Rebuke the beasts that dwell among the reeds" (v30).** The beast among the reeds is probably the hippopotamus or crocodile of the Nile, a picture of Egypt (Ezek 29:3). Guzik makes the same identification. The "bulls with the calves of the peoples" are the proud nations and their rulers. The rest of the verse is very hard Hebrew. The ESV's "Trample underfoot those who lust after tribute" differs sharply from the KJV's "till every one submit himself with pieces of silver," and both are attempts at a difficult line. The sense is clear enough: humble the warlike powers. **"Scatter the peoples who delight in war"** returns to the "scatter" of v1.
- **"Nobles shall come from Egypt; Cush shall hasten to stretch out her hands to God" (v31).** "Nobles" translates a word found only here. Its meaning is unknown, and the ancient versions guessed "ambassadors." Cush is the upper Nile region south of Egypt (modern Sudan), the far edge of the known world. Egypt was Israel's ancient oppressor, and Cush was distant and exotic. Both will come to God with hands stretched out in prayer and homage (Isa 19:21–25). The New Testament shows a first answer when an Ethiopian official reads Isaiah on the road from Jerusalem and is baptized (Acts 8:27–39).
- **Commentators:** JFB says "nobles" literally means "fat ones," but the lexicon does not support any certain meaning; the word appears only here and BDB calls it unknown. Henry reads the beasts as all who resist Christ and his gospel, and the procession of Egypt and Cush as "the conversion of the Gentile nations." Guzik quotes Spurgeon that former enemies can become friends, and Poole that Egypt and Ethiopia stand for all nations far from God.

#### O Kingdoms of the Earth, Sing to God (vv32–35)
- **"O kingdoms of the earth, sing to God" (v32).** The invitation to sing (v4) now goes out to every nation. Guzik: it is better to bow willingly now than later as conquered enemies. **Selah.**
- **"To him who rides in the heavens, the ancient heavens" (v33).** The rider of v4 returns, now clearly in the sky. "Ancient" (*qedem*) means from the beginning. **"He sends out his voice, his mighty voice"**, the thunder, the voice of the LORD (29:3–9), and perhaps the thunder at Sinai (Exod 19:19).
- **"Ascribe power to God, whose majesty is over Israel, and whose power is in the skies" (v34).** His power rules the storm. His majesty is over his people.
- **"Awesome is God from his sanctuary… he is the one who gives power and strength to his people" (v35).** The psalm ends not with Israel's strength but with God giving his strength away. "Sanctuary" is plural in Hebrew, "holy places," perhaps meaning both his earthly and heavenly dwelling. **"Blessed be God!"** closes the march.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "If all be from him, let all be to him." JFB sums up vv32–35: let all nations give honor to him who, "in nature, and still more in the wonders of His spiritual government… rules His Church, and, by His Church, rules the world in righteousness." Guzik passes on Kirkpatrick's remark that this psalm has been a battle song for many, from the Crusaders to Cromwell's soldiers, and adds that such earthly victories were all temporary. Only the Messiah's victory lasts.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 68 follows God's victory march from Sinai to Zion, and the New Testament shows where that march finally led. Jesus is the God who arose, scattering his enemies at the empty tomb. Women were the first to announce that news (Matt 28:7–8). Then he ascended on high, leading captivity captive, and having received gifts as the conquering King he gave them away to his people, pouring out the Spirit at Pentecost (Acts 2:33) and giving the church its leaders (Eph 4:8–12). The Father of the fatherless gathers the lonely into a family (Eph 2:19). And the procession is still going: from Egypt, from Cush, from every kingdom of the earth, people stretch out their hands to the God who "gives power and strength to his people."
