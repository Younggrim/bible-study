*Setting:* Israel is camped at Shittim ("acacia trees"), on the plains of Moab about eight miles east of the Jordan. This is the same camp where an earlier generation fell into sexual sin and idolatry with the women of Moab (Num 25:1). Joshua has just told the people to prepare to cross within three days (1:11). Across the river, about five miles west of the Jordan, stands Jericho. It was an oasis city fed by a strong spring, which Deuteronomy calls "the city of palm trees" (Deut 34:3). It lies roughly 800 feet below sea level and is one of the oldest continuously settled sites on earth. Jericho guarded the routes from the Jordan valley up into the central hill country, so Israel could not go around it. Forty years earlier, Moses sent twelve spies from Kadesh, and their report sent the nation into despair (Num 13–14). Joshua was one of those twelve. Now he sends two.

#### Rahab Hides the Spies (vv1–24)
- **"Joshua… sent two men secretly" (v1).** The contrast with Numbers 13 is deliberate. That time twelve tribal leaders went out publicly and reported to the whole assembly, and ten of them talked the nation out of obeying. This time two men go quietly and report to Joshua alone (v23). Sending scouts was not a lack of faith. God's promise ("I have given," 1:3) did not rule out ordinary wisdom.
- **"Go, view the land, especially Jericho" (v1).** Oddly, nothing in the spies' report is used in the battle plan for Jericho, which God gives directly (6:2–5). The mission's lasting result is not military information but the rescue of one Canaanite family.
- **"The house of a prostitute whose name was Rahab" (v1).** The Hebrew word is *zonah*, the normal word for a prostitute, and the New Testament keeps it ("Rahab the prostitute," Heb 11:31; Jas 2:25). Josephus and some later writers call her an innkeeper, which may also be true: in the ancient Near East, inns run by women often doubled as brothels. Her house was a place where strange men could come and go without questions, which is likely why the spies chose it. The text says only that they "lodged there."
- **"It was told to the king of Jericho" (v2).** Canaan in this period was a patchwork of small city-states, each with its own "king." Jericho was on alert. Foreign men stood out, perhaps by their accent or dress.
- **"Bring out the men who have come to you" (v3).** The king assumes Rahab will cooperate. She is a citizen of Jericho, and refusing a royal order was treason.
- **"True, the men came to me, but I did not know where they were from" (vv4–5).** Rahab lies, and lies well: she admits enough to be believed and sends the pursuers away in a hurry. The Bible reports her lie without praising it. Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25 commend her faith in receiving the spies and sending them off another way, not her words to the king's men. Christians have long divided over the case. Augustine and Calvin held that the lie was still sin, though God forgave it along with her other sins. Others argue that, like the Hebrew midwives who misled Pharaoh (Exod 1:15–21), she faced a moment where protecting innocent life outweighed telling the truth to men seeking to kill. Either way, she was saved by faith, not by her lie.
- **"The stalks of flax that she had laid in order on the roof" (v6).** Flax was harvested in spring (compare 3:15 and 5:10–11, Passover season). The stalks were soaked and spread out on flat roofs to dry before being beaten into fiber for linen. Rahab may have spun or traded linen, an honest trade alongside the other. A stack of drying stalks made a good hiding place.
- **"On the way to the Jordan as far as the fords" (v7).** The pursuers assume the spies are heading back east to Israel's camp. The city shuts its gate behind them, trapping anyone still inside, which is exactly where the spies are.
- **"I know that the LORD has given you the land" (v9).** Rahab uses the divine name, *Yahweh*, and she says "I know" before the spies say anything. She has reached her conclusion before meeting a single Israelite.
- **"All the inhabitants of the land melt away before you" (v9).** This echoes, almost word for word, Moses' song at the Red Sea forty years earlier: "all the inhabitants of Canaan have melted away. Terror and dread fall upon them" (Exod 15:15–16). That prophecy was being fulfilled while Israel was still wandering. In verse 11 "our hearts melted" uses *masas*, the same word Moses used of Israel's own fear at Kadesh: "Our brothers have made our hearts melt" (Deut 1:28). The first generation melted before the Canaanites. Now the Canaanites melt before the second.
- **"We have heard" (v10).** News traveled along the trade routes. Rahab cites two events: the Red Sea, forty years ago, and the defeat of Sihon and Og east of the Jordan, only months ago (Num 21:21–35). Jericho knew the same facts she knew. Everyone in the city heard; one household believed.
- **"Whom you devoted to destruction" (v10).** This is the Hebrew *charam*, to put something under the ban, giving it over wholly to God, usually by destruction. It is the key term for the conquest of Canaan (Deut 7:1–2; 20:16–18), and it will hang over Jericho in chapter 6.
- **"The LORD your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath" (v11).** This is nearly the same confession Moses taught Israel: "the LORD is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath; there is no other" (Deut 4:39). A Canaanite prostitute speaks Israel's creed. Her gods were local gods of city and fertility. She confesses a God who rules everywhere.
- **"As I have dealt kindly with you, you also will deal kindly" (v12).** The word is *chesed*, loyal, covenant kindness, the great Old Testament word for faithful love. The spies answer in kind with "kindly and faithfully" (*chesed ve'emet*, v14), the pairing used of God himself (Exod 34:6). Rahab asks Israel's men to swear "by the LORD," putting herself under the protection of Israel's God.
- **"My father and mother, my brothers and sisters, and all who belong to them" (v13).** She thinks at once of her family. No husband or children are mentioned.
- **"Our life for yours even to death!" (v14).** The spies put their own lives on the line as a pledge. The condition is secrecy: "If you do not tell this business of ours."
- **"Her house was built into the city wall" (v15).** Some ancient cities had double walls with houses built across the gap, or houses backed against the outer wall. Archaeologists have long debated what Jericho's walls looked like in Joshua's day, since the site was badly eroded and dug over in the 20th century. The point here is simple: her window looked out over the outside of the wall.
- **"Go into the hills… and hide there three days" (v16).** West of Jericho the ground rises steeply into barren limestone hills full of caves, the area tradition later linked with Jesus' temptation. The pursuers were searching east toward the Jordan, so Rahab sends the men the opposite way.
- **"We will be guiltless with respect to this oath" (v17).** The spies now spell out the terms, though oddly while hanging from a rope or about to go down it. Some think the conversation in vv17–21 happened at the window before they climbed down.
- **"This scarlet cord" (v18).** The word for cord is *tiqvah*. The same spelling is the common Hebrew word for "hope" (Jer 29:11; Job 7:6). BDB lists them as two words, though both are often traced to a root meaning to twist, stretch or wait. Whether the writer intended a play on words cannot be proved, but readers have noticed it for centuries: Rahab's hope literally hung from her window.
- **"Gather into your house your father and mother" (vv18–19).** Safety had a sign and a place. Anyone inside the marked house would live. Anyone who went out into the street was responsible for his own death. The parallel to the Passover is hard to miss: blood on the doorposts, and "none of you shall go out of the door of his house until the morning" (Exod 12:22).
- **"According to your words, so be it" (v21).** She tied the cord in the window at once, perhaps weeks before Israel arrived. She did not wait to see the army before declaring which side she was on.
- **"Remained there three days" (v22).** The pursuers searched "all along the way" and gave up. The spies came down, crossed the river, and reported.
- **"Truly the LORD has given all the land into our hands" (v24).** This is the opposite of the report at Kadesh, "We are not able to go up against the people" (Num 13:31). The facts were the same: walled cities, strong peoples. What changed was where the spies looked. The enemy's fear confirmed God's promise.
- **Commentators:** All three refuse to soften "prostitute." JFB says Scripture usage, the Septuagint and the apostles establish "the propriety of the term," and that women did not keep Eastern inns. On the lie, Henry writes, "It is plain that she deceived the officers," and "divines generally conceive that it was a sin," but adds that the case "was altogether extraordinary, and therefore cannot be drawn into a precedent," and that "God accepts what is sincerely and honestly intended, though there be a mixture of frailty and folly in it." JFB calls it "a sinful expedient," pardoned because it was joined with faith. Guzik also says Scripture records the lie without approving it, and the Tyndale notes are more open, suggesting that saving life may have outweighed truth-telling here. Henry says her faith outshone Israel's elders, who had seen the wonders themselves, and he sees in her plea for her family a model for seeking the salvation of our own. JFB compares the scarlet line to the blood on the doorposts in Egypt. Guzik notes that early writers such as Clement of Rome read it as a picture of Christ's blood, and he stresses that the real fruit of the spies' mission was one woman's rescue.

#### Pointing to Christ
Rahab is the first person in the promised land to be saved, and she is the least likely: a Canaanite, a prostitute, a citizen of a city under judgment. She heard the same news as everyone in Jericho, believed it, sided with God's people before the battle, and hung out the sign she was told to show. She did not become one of Israel by birth, but by faith. Matthew names her in Jesus' family tree as the mother of Boaz and the great-great-grandmother of King David (Matt 1:5). The scarlet cord in the window, like the blood on the doorposts, points to the way God has always saved: those who shelter under the sign he gives are spared when judgment falls, and the Savior who came from Rahab's line shed his own blood to be that sign for all who believe.
