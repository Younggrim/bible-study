*Setting:* Psalm 131 is the twelfth of the fifteen "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120–134), the pilgrim songs sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the festivals. It is one of the shortest psalms in the Bible, only three verses, and one of four Songs of Ascents with David's name in the title (with 122, 124 and 133). Psalm 130 cried out of the depths of guilt and ended with the call, "O Israel, hope in the LORD!" Psalm 131 ends with the same call, but it reaches it by a different road: not through guilt forgiven but through pride laid down. If David wrote it, its humility stands out. He was a shepherd boy anointed king, a war hero, and the founder of a dynasty. Matthew Henry suggests he wrote it while Saul and his court accused him of scheming for the throne. Others connect it to Michal's scorn when David danced before the ark and answered, "I will make myself yet more contemptible than this, and I will be abased in your eyes" (2 Sam 6:20–22). Some scholars think the title means "in David's style" and date the psalm later. Whoever first sang it, it pictures a soul that has stopped grasping and found rest in God. Some interpreters note that the speaker's image of a mother and child suggests the psalm could have been written or sung by a woman, though the text does not say. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### My Heart Is Not Lifted Up (vv1–2)
- **"O LORD, my heart is not lifted up" (v1).** The psalm is spoken directly to God. It is not a boast to other people but an honest report to the one who sees the heart. "Lifted up" (*gabah*) means high or haughty. Proverbs warns that "haughtiness" of heart comes before a fall (Prov 16:18; 18:12). Uzziah was a strong king who "grew proud" (literally, his heart was lifted up, *gabah*), and he ended his life a leper (2 Chron 26:16–21). In the Bible the heart is the center of a person, thoughts, desires and will together. Pride starts there.
- **"My eyes are not raised too high" (v1).** Pride shows in the eyes, in looking down on others or looking up enviously at what one wants. "Haughty eyes" are first on the list of seven things the LORD hates (Prov 6:16–17). David himself sang, "haughty eyes you bring down" (Ps 18:27).
- **"I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me" (v1).** The Hebrew is literally "I do not walk in great things." It means not chasing positions, plans or knowledge that lie beyond what God has given. "Too marvelous" (*nifla'ot*) is the word often used for God's wonders, his miraculous works (Ps 136:4; Exod 3:20). Some things belong to God alone. "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us" (Deut 29:29). Job learned this at the end of his trials: "I have uttered what I did not understand, things too wonderful for me, which I did not know" (Job 42:3), using the same word.
- **Not a rejection of all ambition.** The psalm does not forbid hard work, hope or growth. Paul pressed on toward the goal (Phil 3:12–14). What it rejects is ambition centered on self, the restless grasping that refuses to accept limits or wait for God's timing. David did not seize the throne from Saul, though he had chances (1 Sam 24; 26). He waited for God to give it.
- **"But I have calmed and quieted my soul" (v2).** The Hebrew begins with an oath formula, literally "Surely I have..." or "If I have not...," a strong way of saying "I truly have." "Calmed" (*shawah*) means to smooth or level, like flattening rough ground. "Quieted" (*damam*) means to be still or silent, the word in "Be still before the LORD" (Ps 37:7). The soul was not calm by nature. The psalmist calmed it, by a choice, the way someone settles a restless child.
- **"Like a weaned child with its mother" (v2).** In Israel children were often weaned at around two or three years old. Hannah kept Samuel at home until he was weaned and then brought him to the house of the LORD (1 Sam 1:22–24), and Abraham held a great feast the day Isaac was weaned (Gen 21:8). A nursing baby cries and frets in its mother's arms because it wants to be fed. A weaned child can rest on its mother's lap without demanding anything. It is with her for her own sake, not just for what she gives. The image is of a soul that has learned to be with God without fretting over what it wants from him.
- **"Like a weaned child is my soul within me" (v2).** The line repeats the image, making it the center of the psalm. Weaning is often hard for a child. There are tears before there is rest. The picture suggests that God sometimes weans his people from things they cling to, so that they learn to rest in him.
- **God as mother.** The psalm does not call God a mother directly, but the picture invites the comparison. Isaiah uses it openly: "As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you" (Isa 66:13), and "Can a woman forget her nursing child...? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isa 49:15).
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks David was answering those who called him an ambitious man, and says "it is our wisdom, and will be our praise, to keep within our sphere." On v2 he writes, "When our condition is not to our mind we must bring our mind to our condition," and notes that "our Saviour has taught us humility by this comparison" (Matt 18:3–4). JFB says the psalm "teaches the humble, submissive temper of a true child of God," reads v2 as an oath, and notes that "submission is denoted by the figure of a weaned child." He also notes that Hebrew children were often not weaned until three. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's line that the weaned child rests *on* its mother rather than being weaned *from* her, and draws the distinction between godly aspiration, which looks to God, and selfish ambition, which looks to self (Gal 5:20; Phil 2:3).

#### O Israel, Hope in the LORD (v3)
- **"O Israel, hope in the LORD" (v3).** The same call that ended Psalm 130:7. The person who has quieted his own soul turns outward and invites the whole people to do the same. "Hope" (*yachal*) means to wait with expectation. Pride grasps; hope waits. A people tempted to anxiety, rivalry and grasping after power is called to rest in the LORD instead.
- **"From this time forth and forevermore" (v3).** The same phrase as Psalm 121:8 and 125:2. The rest is not for a moment but for life, beginning now. This quiet trust is not a mood for a peaceful day. It is a settled way of living before God.
- **The connection with Psalm 132.** The next psalm remembers David's hardships and God's oath to David. Read together, they show two sides of David: the humble soul who would not grasp (Ps 131) and the king to whom God gave a lasting throne (Ps 132). God lifts up those who do not lift themselves up.
- **Commentators:** Henry closes, "Thus it is good to hope and quietly to wait for the salvation of the Lord," echoing Lamentations 3:26. JFB has no separate note on v3. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's observation that a soul weaned from self naturally begins to care for others, which is why David turns from his own heart to all Israel, and Morgan's point that hope in the Lord is the cure for restless ambition.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 131 describes a heart that is not lifted up, a soul that has stopped grasping and rests in God like a child with its mother. Jesus is the one in whom this is perfectly true. "Though he was in the form of God," he "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant" (Phil 2:6–7). He said, "I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls" (Matt 11:29). He did nothing on his own, but only what he saw the Father doing (John 5:19), and he prayed in Gethsemane, "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). Because he humbled himself, "God has highly exalted him" (Phil 2:9). He also told his disciples that "unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 18:3). The quiet, trusting soul of Psalm 131 is the soul that Christ gives to those who come to him, and the rest it describes is found in him.
