*Setting:* For forty years Jeremiah has warned that Babylon would take Jerusalem and burn it. In this chapter it happens. The siege that began in January 588 BC ends with a breach in the walls in the summer of 587 or 586 BC. Scholars differ by a year over the date, depending on how Judah's calendar is matched to Babylon's. The fall of Jerusalem is told four times in the Old Testament, here, in Jeremiah 52, in 2 Kings 25 and in 2 Chronicles 36, and it is mourned in the book of Lamentations. It was the great catastrophe of Israel's history: the end of David's kingdom, the destruction of Solomon's temple and the start of the Babylonian exile. This chapter tells it briefly and plainly. Then it turns to two men who trusted God's word and were spared, the prophet himself and the foreign servant who pulled him out of the cistern.

#### The Fall of Jerusalem (vv1–10)
- **"In the ninth year of Zedekiah king of Judah, in the tenth month, Nebuchadnezzar… came against Jerusalem and besieged it" (v1).** The tenth month, Tebeth, fell in December–January. Ezekiel, in Babylon, was told to write down that very day, because "the king of Babylon has laid siege to Jerusalem this very day" (Ezek 24:1–2). Later Jews kept the date as a fast day (Zech 8:19).
- **"In the eleventh year of Zedekiah, in the fourth month, on the ninth day of the month, a breach was made in the city" (v2).** The fourth month, Tammuz, fell in June–July. The siege had lasted about eighteen months, with a break when the Egyptians came (37:5). By then "the famine was so severe in the city that there was no food for the people of the land" (52:6). Lamentations remembers mothers boiling their own children (Lam 4:10). The day of the breach also became a fast (Zech 8:19).
- **"All the officials of the king of Babylon came and sat in the middle gate" (v3).** Sitting in the gate was the posture of rule and judgment. The conquerors took the seats of Jerusalem's elders. The "middle gate" is not mentioned elsewhere. It may have been an inner gate between parts of the city.
- **The officials' names (v3).** Babylonian names and titles are run together here, and translations divide them differently. **"Nergal-sar-ezer"** (Nergal-shar-usur, "Nergal, protect the king") is very likely the man known from Babylonian records as Neriglissar, Nebuchadnezzar's son-in-law, who later seized Babylon's throne himself (560–556 BC). **"Nebu-sar-sekim the Rab-saris."** In 2007 a researcher at the British Museum read a small clay tablet, a receipt for gold given to a temple in 595 BC, which names "Nabu-sharrussu-ukin, the chief eunuch." That is very close to the name and exactly the title here. "Rab-saris" means "chief of the court officials" or "chief eunuch." **"Rab-mag"** is another high title. Older commentators linked it to the magi, but most scholars now take it as a Babylonian court office whose exact duties are unknown. The names matter because they show the account is rooted in real people and real events.
- **"When Zedekiah king of Judah and all the soldiers saw them, they fled, going out of the city at night by way of the king's garden through the gate between the two walls" (v4).** The Babylonians had broken through on the north, the city's only side not protected by deep valleys. Zedekiah fled the other way, through a gate at the southeast corner near the royal gardens and the Pool of Siloam (Neh 3:15), where two lines of wall met. **"They went toward the Arabah."** The deep Jordan valley, heading east toward Jericho and the Jordan crossings, perhaps hoping to reach Ammon, whose king had sided against Babylon (40:14). Ezekiel had acted out this very scene in Babylon years before, digging through a wall and carrying out baggage at dusk with his face covered: "the prince who is among them shall lift his baggage upon his shoulder at dusk, and shall go out" (Ezek 12:3–12).
- **"The army of the Chaldeans pursued them and overtook Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho" (v5).** About 15 miles from Jerusalem and close to the river. 2 Kings 25:5 adds that "all his army was scattered from him." The "trusted friends" of 38:22 had abandoned him, just as Jeremiah's vision said. **"They brought him up to Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, at Riblah, in the land of Hamath."** Riblah lay on the Orontes River in Syria, more than 200 miles north, where Nebuchadnezzar kept his field headquarters, a good base for controlling both Judah and Phoenicia. Pharaoh Neco had used the same place to depose Jehoahaz in 609 BC (2 Kgs 23:33). **"And he passed sentence on him."** Literally "spoke judgments with him." Zedekiah was tried as a rebel vassal who had broken his oath (Ezek 17:15–20).
- **"The king of Babylon slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah at Riblah before his eyes" (v6).** Zedekiah was only about thirty-two (2 Kgs 24:18), so his sons were young. Killing a rebel king's heirs ended any hope of his line returning. **"And the king of Babylon slaughtered all the nobles of Judah."** The officials who had demanded Jeremiah's death and urged resistance (38:4) died at Riblah. More leaders were executed later (52:24–27).
- **"He put out the eyes of Zedekiah and bound him in chains to take him to Babylon" (v7).** Blinding was a known punishment for rebels and captives in the ancient Near East (compare Samson, Judg 16:21). The last thing Zedekiah saw was his sons' death. This explains two prophecies that seemed to clash. Jeremiah said Zedekiah would see the king of Babylon "eye to eye" (32:4; 34:3), and he did, at Riblah. Ezekiel said, "I will bring him to Babylon… yet he shall not see it, and he shall die there" (Ezek 12:13). Blind, he never saw Babylon. He died there in prison (52:11).
- **"The Chaldeans burned the king's house and the house of the people, and broke down the walls of Jerusalem" (v8).** "The house of the people" is uncertain. It may mean the people's homes in general or some public building. The burning of the temple is told in 52:12–13, about a month after the breach, when Nebuzaradan arrived to finish the job.
- **A note on the text.** The ancient Greek translation of Jeremiah has a much shorter version of this chapter, lacking vv4–13. Many scholars think those verses were added from the parallel account in 52:7–16 and 2 Kings 25. Others think the Greek shortened it. The events themselves are told in the same words in those other passages.
- **"Then Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard, carried into exile to Babylon the rest of the people who were left in the city, those who had deserted to him, and the people who remained" (v9).** "Captain of the guard" is literally "chief of the cooks" or "slaughterers," an old title for the commander of the royal bodyguard (compare Potiphar, Gen 37:36). He was a senior general. The exiles included the survivors of the city and the deserters who had followed Jeremiah's counsel to surrender. They lived, as he had promised, but in exile.
- **"Nebuzaradan… left in the land of Judah some of the poor people who owned nothing, and gave them vineyards and fields" (v10).** A striking reversal. The rich who had held their fellow Judeans in slavery (34:8–11) were dead or exiled, and the poor who "owned nothing" were given the land. Babylon's reason was practical: someone had to farm the land and pay tribute. But the result echoes a deep thread in Scripture, God lifting up the poor (1 Sam 2:7–8; Luke 1:52–53).
- **Commentators:** Henry says simply, "now was fulfilled what Jeremiah prophesied long since." JFB explains the blinding as the key that reconciles Jeremiah 32:4 with Ezekiel 12:13, and says Zedekiah was tried "as a common criminal, not as a king." On v10 he observes, "The poor have least to lose," and calls their gift of land a providential compensation. JFB reads "Rab-mag" as "chief of the magi," a view most scholars now doubt. Guzik ties the flight and capture closely to Ezekiel 12 and stresses that it was ultimately God, not only Babylon, who was closing in on Zedekiah. Citing Maclaren, he notes that blinding the king and killing his sons also had a cold political purpose, since a blind and childless captive could never lead a revolt.

#### The LORD Delivers Jeremiah (vv11–18)
- **"Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon gave command concerning Jeremiah through Nebuzaradan" (v11).** How did the king of Babylon know about Jeremiah? Deserters would have reported that a prophet in Jerusalem was urging surrender (38:19), and Jeremiah's letter to the exiles (ch 29) may have been known in Babylon. To the Babylonians he looked like a friend of their cause. In fact he was simply God's servant, who had told the truth to both sides.
- **"Take him, look after him well, and do him no harm, but deal with him as he tells you" (v12).** "Look after him well" is literally "set your eyes on him," that is, watch over him for good (24:6). God had promised at Jeremiah's call, "They will fight against you, but they shall not prevail against you, for I am with you… to deliver you" (1:19). He also promised during his darkest complaint, "I will make the enemy plead with you in the time of trouble" (15:11). Now the enemy's own king orders his protection.
- **"Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard, Nebushazban the Rab-saris, Nergal-sar-ezer the Rab-mag, and all the chief officers" (v13).** The senior officers themselves see to it. Nebushazban may have succeeded the chief official named in v3, or the two names may be variants.
- **"They entrusted him to Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, son of Shaphan, that he should take him home" (v14).** Gedaliah came from the family that had protected Jeremiah for decades. His father Ahikam saved Jeremiah from being killed (26:24), and his grandfather Shaphan was Josiah's secretary (2 Kgs 22:3). Babylon appointed Gedaliah governor of what remained of Judah (40:5). A clay seal impression from Lachish reads "belonging to Gedalyahu, who is over the house," a high palace office, and many identify it with him. **"So he lived among the people."** The prophet who had been alone and rejected chose to stay with the poor remnant of his people.
- **Two accounts of the release.** Chapter 40 tells that Nebuzaradan found Jeremiah "bound in chains" among the captives at Ramah and released him there (40:1–6). Most interpreters think both are true: Jeremiah was taken from the court of the guard, then swept up in the general roundup of prisoners, and finally recognized and freed at Ramah, five miles north. Chapter 39 gives the summary, chapter 40 the details.
- **"The word of the LORD came to Jeremiah while he was shut up in the court of the guard" (v15).** The narrative steps back in time. This word came before the city fell, soon after the rescue from the cistern (38:13). It is placed here, after the fall, to show that God's promise to Ebed-melech came true.
- **"Behold, I will fulfill my words against this city for harm and not for good, and they shall be accomplished before you on that day" (v16).** Ebed-melech would see the destruction with his own eyes, but he would not be destroyed by it.
- **"I will deliver you on that day… and you shall not be given into the hand of the men of whom you are afraid" (v17).** Ebed-melech had reason to be afraid. He had opposed powerful officials in public (38:7–9). He may also have feared the Babylonians, as a servant of the rebel king's household. God promised protection from both.
- **"You shall have your life as a prize of war, because you have put your trust in me" (v18).** The same promise given to those who surrendered (21:9; 38:2), and later to Baruch (45:5). "Put your trust" is *batach*, to rely on, to feel secure in. The reason given for his rescue is not his courage or his kindness, real as they were, but his faith. His kindness to Jeremiah was the fruit of trusting the God whose prophet Jeremiah was. Jesus said, "The one who receives a prophet because he is a prophet will receive a prophet's reward" (Matt 10:41).
- **Commentators:** On v12 JFB explains the Hebrew "set thine eyes upon him" as providing for Jeremiah's welfare, and says Gedaliah was chosen as "one likely to remain faithful to Nebuchadnezzar." Henry notes that Nebuchadnezzar "had given particular orders that care should be taken of him," and on Ebed-melech says that he "trusted in God that he would own him, and stand by him." Guzik, with Kidner, stresses the same point: what marks Ebed-melech in God's eyes is his trust, not only his bravery. Guzik also observes, with Thompson, that word of Jeremiah's counsel probably reached Babylon through deserters, and he presents Jeremiah's protection as grace shown even in the middle of judgment.

#### Pointing to Christ
On the day Jerusalem fell, God's word proved true in both directions. Every warning came to pass, and every promise to those who trusted him held fast. Zedekiah, who heard the word and would not obey it, lost his sons, his sight and his throne. Ebed-melech, a foreign servant who trusted the word, walked out alive. That is the shape of the gospel. Jesus also wept over Jerusalem and foretold its fall to Rome in AD 70, with a siege, a breach and the temple burned (Luke 19:41–44; 21:20–24). He offered the same way of escape that Jeremiah offered, not surrender to Babylon, but trust in him: "Whoever believes in him is not condemned" (John 3:18). On the great day of judgment, those who have put their trust in him will have their lives "as a prize of war," saved through the fire.
