*Setting:* Jerusalem in the eighth century BC, probably late in the prosperous reigns of Uzziah and Jotham or early in the reign of Ahaz, around 740–730 BC. Chapter 2 ended with the command to stop trusting "man in whose nostrils is breath" (2:22). Chapter 3 shows what happens when God takes away the people they trusted. It is one long judgment speech running to 4:1. First the leaders are removed and society falls apart (vv1–12). Then God puts the leaders on trial for robbing the poor (vv13–15). Finally he turns to the wealthy women of Jerusalem, whose finery will be stripped away (vv16–26). The disaster came in stages: Assyrian invasions under Isaiah's own eyes, and finally the Babylonian conquest of 597–586 BC, when the king, the officials and the skilled workers were carried off (2 Kgs 24:14–16).

#### Judgment on Judah and Jerusalem (vv1–26)
- **"The Lord GOD of hosts" (v1).** "Lord GOD" is the Hebrew *Adonai YHWH*, "the Master, the LORD." "Of hosts" means the commander of heaven's armies. The opening title tells the reader that what follows is not a forecast but a decision by the one who rules everything.
- **"Support and supply" (v1).** The Hebrew pairs a masculine and a feminine form of the same word (*mash'en umash'enah*), a way of saying "every kind of support," like "staff and stay" in the older translations. First God removes the basics, bread and water, which happened in the sieges of Jerusalem (2 Kgs 25:3; Jer 38:9; Lev 26:26 had warned of it).
- **"The mighty man and the soldier, the judge and the prophet" (vv2–3).** Then God removes the people a nation leans on: military men, judges, prophets, elders, officers ("captain of fifty"), counselors and skilled craftsmen. The list mixes legitimate leaders with forbidden ones, the "diviner," the "skillful magician" and the "expert in charms." Judah leaned on both kinds equally, and God would take both away. When Babylon came, it deported exactly these classes and left "only the poorest people of the land" (2 Kgs 24:14).
- **"I will make boys their princes, and infants shall rule over them" (v4).** The word for "infants," *ta'alulim*, is rare. The lexicon gives "caprice" or "wantonness," so it may mean "capricious ones" or "whims shall rule over them." Either way, the point is immature, unstable rule. Some think of Ahaz, who began to reign young and acted foolishly, or of the boy kings of Judah's last years. Ecclesiastes says, "Woe to you, O land, when your king is a child" (Eccl 10:16).
- **"The people will oppress one another" (v5).** When leadership collapses, everyone turns on everyone: neighbor against neighbor, the young against the old, the low against the honored. Respect for elders was a basic duty in Israel (Lev 19:32), and its loss is a sign of a society coming apart.
- **"You have a cloak; you shall be our leader" (vv6–7).** A grim, almost comic scene. Things are so bad that owning a decent coat makes a man qualified to rule, and a relative grabs him and begs him to take charge of "this heap of ruins." He refuses: "I will not be a healer," literally "a binder of wounds." The country is the beaten body of 1:5–6, and no one wants to bandage it.
- **"Defying his glorious presence" (v8).** Literally "rebelling against the eyes of his glory." The root of the collapse is not bad policy but open defiance of God, "their speech and their deeds," in front of his face.
- **"They proclaim their sin like Sodom; they do not hide it" (v9).** Isaiah already compared Jerusalem to Sodom (1:9–10). The worst stage of sin is not doing wrong but doing it with no shame at all, so that it shows on people's faces. Jeremiah says the same of his day: "they did not know how to blush" (Jer 6:15).
- **"Woe to them! For they have brought evil on themselves" (v9).** "Woe" is a cry of grief at a funeral, used by the prophets to announce coming judgment. The people are not victims of bad luck. Their own choices are coming back on them.
- **"Tell the righteous that it shall be well with them" (v10).** In the middle of the gloom, a word for the faithful. God never lumps the righteous in with the wicked (Gen 18:25). The principle in vv10–11 is reaping what you sow: the righteous "eat the fruit of their deeds," while the wicked receive what their own hands "dealt out" (Gal 6:7–8).
- **"Infants are their oppressors, and women rule over them" (v12).** In that culture this described weak, immature government, rulers who were pushed around by others. Some think of court women influencing weak kings. An interesting textual point: the Hebrew letters for "women" (*nashim*) can also be read as "creditors" or "money-lenders" (*noshim*). The Greek Septuagint took it that way, and some scholars follow it, giving "creditors rule over them," which fits a chapter about the poor being exploited. The Hebrew vowels, added much later, read "women."
- **"Your guides mislead you" (v12).** The word for "guides" is the same one used for those who "lead" in 9:16: "those who guide this people have been leading them astray." The leaders who should have shown the way had "swallowed up the course of your paths," erased the road altogether.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry reads vv1–8 as God taking away "all the supports both of their life and of their government," and v10 as a promise "to assure good people that it should be well with them." JFB calls "stay and staff" an idiom for every kind of support, and sees it fulfilled in the sieges under both Nebuchadnezzar and the Roman general Titus. JFB reads "children" as children in ability, not age, and offers a vivid line on v10: the faithful few are still there, even when only "croaking of frogs" can be heard. Guzik argues that one of God's simplest judgments is to give a people the poor leaders their hearts want. On v9 he presses the point that shameless sin is worse than sin a society still feels it must hide.
- **"The LORD has taken his place to contend" (v13).** The scene shifts to a courtroom. God rises from his seat to bring a case, and he is both prosecutor and judge. "Contend" is the Hebrew *rib*, a lawsuit, the same kind of trial scene that opened the book (1:2, 18).
- **"It is you who have devoured the vineyard" (v14).** The charge is aimed at "the elders and princes." Israel is God's vineyard, a picture Isaiah develops fully in 5:1–7. The leaders were supposed to tend it, and instead they ate it. "Devoured" is the word for grazing animals stripping a field bare.
- **"The spoil of the poor is in your houses" (v14).** "Spoil" is the word for plunder taken in war. The leaders treated their own poor as a conquered enemy. Their wealth had been stripped from the people they were meant to protect (1:23; Mic 3:1–3).
- **"Grinding the face of the poor" (v15).** The image is of a millstone. The rich ground the poor down like grain until nothing was left. "What do you mean by crushing my people?" Notice "my people." God takes the oppression of the poor personally (Prov 14:31).
- **"The daughters of Zion are haughty" (v16).** Now Isaiah turns to the wealthy women of Jerusalem, the wives and daughters of the leaders just condemned, living on what was taken from the poor. Amos says much the same to the rich women of Samaria (Amos 4:1). The picture is close observation: heads held high, eyes flirting, "mincing along" with small steps, "tinkling with their feet." The tinkling came from ankle bracelets, sometimes joined with little chains that forced short, dainty steps.
- **"The LORD will lay bare their secret parts" (v17).** The punishment answers the pride. Women who displayed themselves will be shamed. Scabbing of the scalp probably points to disease and starvation in siege. Conquering armies stripped captives and marched them off naked or nearly so (Isa 20:4; Nah 3:5), the deepest humiliation in that culture.
- **The list of finery (vv18–23).** Isaiah names about twenty-one items, many found only here, so their exact meaning is often a guess. Archaeology has turned up many of them: anklets, crescent-shaped pendants, earrings, bracelets, signet rings, nose rings, perfume bottles, small mirrors of polished bronze. Some were "amulets," charms worn to ward off evil, which connects back to the magicians of v3. The length of the list is the point. It reads like an inventory of a jewelry box, and God will "take away" every piece.
- **"Instead of perfume there will be rottenness" (v24).** Five reversals in a row: perfume becomes stench, a fine belt becomes a captive's rope, styled hair becomes the shaved head of mourning or slavery, a rich robe becomes sackcloth, and beauty becomes "branding." The word *ki* (branding) is rare, from a root meaning "burn." It probably means the burn mark put on slaves, though some take it as skin scorched by the sun on the forced march into exile.
- **"Your men shall fall by the sword" (v25).** The final blow is the death of the men in battle, leaving the women without husbands, sons or protectors. This leads straight into 4:1, where seven women beg one man to marry them.
- **"Her gates shall lament and mourn; empty, she shall sit on the ground" (v26).** The city gate was where business, courts and public life happened. Now the gates mourn, and Jerusalem is pictured as a woman sitting in the dust. After the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70, they struck coins inscribed *Iudaea Capta*, "Judea captured," showing a grieving woman sitting under a palm tree, an almost exact picture of this verse.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the leaders' sin was turning their power into oppression, and that judges who should have been the shelter of the oppressed were "themselves the greatest oppressors." JFB reads "the vineyard" as "the Jewish theocracy," and on v26 points to the Roman images of a captive woman beneath a palm. Guzik follows the women's sin from pride, to seduction, to fixation on luxury, and he sets it against Peter's call to the hidden beauty "of a gentle and quiet spirit" (1 Pet 3:3–4). He stresses that God is not against beauty but against pride that tramples the poor to pay for it. The Tyndale notes add that many of the ornaments were charms that could not protect anyone on the day of judgment.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is a portrait of failed leadership: shepherds who fed on the flock, rulers too weak or too greedy to heal the wounds of the people, and a city left in the dust. Isaiah's answer comes in the chapters ahead. The child of 9:6 will not be a capricious boy-king but the "Wonderful Counselor" who rules with justice. The Branch of 11:1–5 will judge the poor with righteousness, the opposite of the elders who ground their faces. Jesus is the healer the man of v7 refused to be: the shepherd who "binds up the injured" (Ezek 34:16) and who came to proclaim good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). And he too was stripped bare and shamed outside the city, so that those clothed in pride could be clothed instead in his righteousness (Isa 61:10).
