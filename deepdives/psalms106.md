*Setting:* Psalm 106 is the second half of a pair. Psalm 105 retold Israel's story from Abraham to Canaan as a record of God's faithfulness and left out every sin. Psalm 106 tells the same story again as a confession, and leaves almost nothing out. Its closing prayer, "gather us from among the nations" (v47), and its mention of captors showing pity (v46) suggest a setting in or after the Babylonian exile (586–538 BC), when God's people had good reason to ask how they had ended up scattered. Yet its first and last verses appear in 1 Chronicles 16:34–36, in David's song for the ark, so the core of it may be older, or the Chronicler may have drawn on it. Psalm 106 closes Book 4 of the Psalter (Pss 90–106), which opened with Moses' prayer, "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations" (90:1). Book 4 began with God's people facing his wrath and ends with them confessing it, still clinging to his steadfast love. The psalm walks through seven failures in the wilderness and then Israel's failure in the land. Henry sums up its spirit: "Our badness makes his goodness appear the more illustrious." The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### Remember Me, O LORD (vv1–5)
- **"Praise the LORD! Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good" (v1).** The psalm opens with *Hallelu-Yah*, as the last one closed. A confession of national sin starts with praise. Henry: "even sorrow for sin must not put us out of tune for praising God." **"For his steadfast love endures forever"** is the refrain of Psalm 136, sung at the temple dedication (2 Chr 7:3) and at the laying of the second temple's foundation (Ezra 3:11). Everything that follows is a long proof of it.
- **"Blessed are they who observe justice, who do righteousness at all times!" (vv2–3).** No one can fully tell God's mighty deeds (v2). This beatitude sets the standard the rest of the psalm shows Israel missing. "At all times" is the hard part. Israel kept faith for moments (v12) but not "at all times."
- **"Remember me, O LORD, when you show favor to your people" (v4).** The psalmist prays for himself before he confesses for the nation. He wants to be included when God restores his people, to "look upon the prosperity of your chosen ones" (v5). The criminal on the cross prayed something similar: "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom" (Luke 23:42).
- **"That I may glory with your inheritance" (v5).** God's people are his "inheritance" (Deut 9:29), his own treasured possession. The psalmist's hope is entirely bound up with theirs.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv1–5 a preface of praise, encouragement and longing for God's favor. JFB reads the "I" of vv4–5 as the present generation, longing to share the good God once gave his chosen people, and notes Israel would have been blessed had it kept the standard of v3. Guzik compares the prayer of v4 to a patient too sick to visit the doctor, who needs the doctor to come to him.

#### Both We and Our Fathers Have Sinned (vv6–12)
- **"Both we and our fathers have sinned; we have committed iniquity; we have done wickedness" (v6).** The psalmist does not blame only his ancestors. "We" stand with them. The three verbs echo Solomon's prayer for an exiled people (1 Kgs 8:47) and Daniel's confession (Dan 9:5), and JFB calls them "a rising gradation of sinning." Nehemiah and Ezra prayed the same way (Neh 9; Ezra 9).
- **"Our fathers, when they were in Egypt, did not consider your wondrous works" (v7).** They had just seen ten plagues, but it did not sink in. **"They did not remember the abundance of your steadfast love."** Forgetting is the root of every failure in this psalm (vv7, 13, 21), as remembering was the theme of Psalm 105.
- **"But rebelled by the sea, at the Red Sea" (v7).** With Pharaoh's army behind them, the people said to Moses, "Is it because there are no graves in Egypt that you have taken us away to die in the wilderness?" (Exod 14:11–12). That was their first rebellion after leaving Egypt, and it came within days.
- **"Yet he saved them for his name's sake, that he might make known his mighty power" (v8).** "Yet" is the first of the psalm's turns. God saved them not because they deserved it but for his own name's sake, to show who he is (Exod 14:4, 18; Ezek 20:9, 14). That is good news for sinners: God's reasons for saving rest on his character, not theirs.
- **"He rebuked the Red Sea, and it became dry" (v9).** As in Psalm 104:7, the sea obeys God's rebuke. **"He led them through the deep as through a desert,"** on dry ground (Exod 14:21–22).
- **"Then they believed his words; they sang his praise" (v12).** Exodus says the same: "the people feared the LORD, and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses" (Exod 14:31), and then they sang the Song of the Sea (Exod 15). JFB adds that it was "a faith which they immediately afterwards lost."
- **Commentators:** Henry heads this section "their provocations at the Red Sea," set against God's great deeds. JFB traces the three verbs of v6 to Solomon's prayer and Daniel. Guzik observes that the hope of restoration is what makes the confession so unsparing, and passes on Spurgeon's comment that Israel's belief at the sea came slowly and only after the evidence was overwhelming.

#### They Soon Forgot His Works (vv13–23)
- **"But they soon forgot his works; they did not wait for his counsel" (v13).** "Soon" is literally "they hurried, they forgot." Three days after singing at the sea, they grumbled about bitter water at Marah (Exod 15:22–24). They would not wait for God's plan to unfold. JFB: "The haste of our desires is such that we can scarcely allow God one day."
- **"They had a wanton craving in the wilderness, and put God to the test" (v14).** Literally "they craved a craving." This is the episode in Numbers 11, when the people wept for the meat, fish, cucumbers and onions of Egypt and despised the manna. "Put God to the test" means demanding that he prove himself on their terms (Exod 17:2; 1 Cor 10:9).
- **"He gave them what they asked, but sent a wasting disease among them" (v15).** God sent quail until the meat came "out at your nostrils" (Num 11:20), and then a plague struck while it was still between their teeth (Num 11:33). The place was named Kibroth-hattaavah, "graves of craving" (Num 11:34). "Wasting disease" (*razon*) means leanness or emaciation. The KJV's famous rendering, "sent leanness into their soul," takes the word in an inward sense, and many have rightly drawn from it that getting what one craves without God can leave the soul starved. The Greek translation read the word differently, as "fullness."
- **"When men in the camp were jealous of Moses and Aaron, the holy one of the LORD" (v16).** This is Korah's rebellion (Num 16). Korah, a Levite, and Dathan and Abiram, from the tribe of Reuben, challenged Moses' leadership and Aaron's priesthood: "All in the congregation are holy… Why then do you exalt yourselves?" (Num 16:3). Aaron is called "the holy one of the LORD" because God had set him apart as priest.
- **"The earth opened and swallowed up Dathan, and covered the company of Abiram" (v17).** The ground split beneath their tents (Num 16:31–33). The psalm does not mention Korah by name here, perhaps because Korah's sons did not die (Num 26:11) and their descendants became temple singers who wrote psalms (Pss 42–49, 84–88).
- **"Fire also broke out in their company; the flame burned up the wicked" (v18).** Fire from the LORD consumed the 250 men offering incense (Num 16:35).
- **"They made a calf in Horeb and worshiped a metal image" (v19).** Horeb is another name for Sinai. While God was giving Moses the law on the mountain, the people below made a golden calf (Exod 32). They did it at the very place where God had said, "You shall not make for yourself a carved image" (Exod 20:4).
- **"They exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox that eats grass" (v20).** The irony is sharp. God's "glory" was his visible presence among them, the cloud and fire. They traded it for a statue of an animal that chews grass. Paul echoes this line in Romans 1:23: they "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals." Jeremiah says the same (Jer 2:11). The psalm generously does not mention that Aaron made the calf.
- **"Had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him" (v23).** God told Moses, "Let me alone, that my wrath may burn hot against them and I may consume them" (Exod 32:10). Moses refused to let him alone and pleaded for the people (Exod 32:11–14, 31–32). "Breach" is a military picture: a gap broken in a city wall, where a soldier stands alone to stop the enemy pouring in. Ezekiel later says God looked for someone to "stand in the breach" and found no one (Ezek 22:30). Moses stood between a holy God and a sinful people. JFB sees him here as a type, a foreshadowing, of the greater Mediator to come.
- **Commentators:** Henry lists these as Israel's sins of craving, mutiny and idolatry, each set against God's mercies. JFB reads v15 as spiritual emptiness that came with the meat, names Kibroth-hattaavah, and says Korah's Levite followers died by fire while Dathan and Abiram were swallowed. Guzik draws from "graves of craving" the warning that unchecked cravings dig their own graves, notes how gently the psalm passes over Aaron's part in the calf, and explains "the breach" as the gap in a broken wall where a defender stands.

#### Then They Despised the Pleasant Land (vv24–33)
- **"Then they despised the pleasant land, having no faith in his promise" (v24).** This is Kadesh-barnea (Num 13–14). Twelve spies explored Canaan. Ten said the people were giants and the cities unbeatable, and the nation believed them over God. "The pleasant land" (also Jer 3:19; Zech 7:14) is a land of desire, and they rejected it.
- **"Therefore he raised his hand and swore to them that he would make them fall in the wilderness" (v26).** Raising the hand was the gesture of taking an oath. God swore that the whole generation over twenty, except Caleb and Joshua, would die in the wilderness over forty years (Num 14:28–35). Hebrews 3–4 uses this as a warning against hardening the heart.
- **"And would make their offspring fall among the nations, scattering them among the lands" (v27).** Numbers 14 does not mention scattering among the nations. The psalmist joins Kadesh to later warnings that disobedience would lead to exile (Lev 26:33; Deut 28:64; Ezek 20:23). If the psalm was written in exile, its first hearers were living in the fulfilment of this verse.
- **"Then they yoked themselves to the Baal of Peor" (v28).** On the plains of Moab, just before entering Canaan, Israelite men were drawn into sexual immorality with Moabite women and into worshipping their god, Baal of Peor (Num 25:1–3). Balaam had advised this strategy (Num 31:16; Rev 2:14).
- **"And ate sacrifices offered to the dead" (v28).** Numbers 25:2 says "sacrifices of their gods." "The dead" may be a scornful name for lifeless idols, as opposed to the living God (Jer 10:10). Or it may refer to rites for the dead, which were part of some Canaanite worship. Either fits.
- **"Then Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was stayed" (v30).** A plague killed 24,000 (Num 25:9). Phinehas, Aaron's grandson, saw an Israelite man bring a Midianite woman into his tent in full view of the mourning congregation, and he killed them both (Num 25:6–8). "Intervened" (*palal*) can mean to judge or execute judgment, and it is related to the word for prayer. He stood in the gap, as Moses did in v23.
- **"And that was counted to him as righteousness from generation to generation forever" (v31).** These are almost the exact words of Genesis 15:6, where Abraham "believed the LORD, and he counted it to him as righteousness." Paul builds his teaching on justification by faith on Abraham's verse (Rom 4:3). Phinehas's case is different: God rewarded his zeal with "a covenant of a perpetual priesthood" (Num 25:12–13). JFB is careful here: it was "a just and rewardable action," not the righteousness by which a sinner is justified before God. Phinehas's act was a unique judgment in a unique moment under Israel's theocracy, not a pattern for private violence, as Trapp, cited by Guzik, warns.
- **"They angered him at the waters of Meribah, and it went ill with Moses on their account" (v32).** Meribah means "quarrelling." At Kadesh, the people quarrelled with Moses over water (Num 20:2–13). God told Moses to speak to the rock. Moses, exasperated, said, "Hear now, you rebels: shall we bring water for you out of this rock?" and struck it twice. God gave the water but told Moses he would not enter the land.
- **"For they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips" (v33).** "Made his spirit bitter" can also be read "they rebelled against his Spirit," meaning God's Spirit (compare Isa 63:10), and some translations take it that way. "Spoke rashly" (*bata'*) is the word for a thoughtless oath (Lev 5:4) and for careless words that wound "like sword thrusts" (Prov 12:18). The greatest leader in Israel's history was provoked into a few unguarded words, and it cost him the promised land. The people's sin and Moses' sin are both real. Provocation explains his failure but does not excuse it.
- **Commentators:** Henry heads these sections Israel's "murmuring," "joining themselves to Baal-peor" and provoking Moses. JFB says Phinehas's act was rewarded with a perpetual priesthood but was not justifying righteousness, and that Moses' rash words cost him entry into Canaan. Guzik notes that "raised his hand" is the idiom for an oath. He says God still held Moses responsible though he was provoked, and passes on Spurgeon's warning that congregations can provoke their leaders into sin. Clarke, cited by Guzik, takes "spoke rashly" as stammering, but the lexicon gives only "speak rashly, thoughtlessly," which is what the ESV has.

#### They Mixed with the Nations (vv34–43)
- **"They did not destroy the peoples, as the LORD commanded them" (v34).** The story moves from the wilderness into the land. God had commanded the Canaanite nations to be removed because of their practices, including child sacrifice (Deut 7:1–5; 12:29–31; 18:9–12). Judges 1–2 records how Israel left them in place, and the warning came true: "they shall become thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare to you" (Judg 2:3).
- **"They sacrificed their sons and their daughters to the demons" (v37).** The worst depth of Canaanite religion was child sacrifice, especially to Molech (Lev 18:21; 2 Kgs 16:3; 21:6; Jer 7:31). Kings of Judah did it, including Ahaz and Manasseh. "Demons" is *shedim*, a word that occurs only here and in Deuteronomy 32:17, "they sacrificed to demons that were no gods." The lexicon traces it to an Assyrian word for a protective spirit. Paul makes the same point: what pagans sacrifice, "they offer to demons and not to God" (1 Cor 10:20). Idols are nothing in themselves, but there is real spiritual evil behind them. Trapp, cited by Guzik, notes the sound-play between *shedim* and *Shaddai* ("the Almighty"), which is a play on sound only. The two words come from different roots.
- **"They poured out innocent blood… and the land was polluted with blood" (v38).** The word "blood" appears three times in the verse, hammering it home. Shed blood defiles the land (Num 35:33). The very sin for which the Canaanites lost the land, Israel now commits.
- **"Thus they became unclean by their acts, and played the whore in their deeds" (v39).** The prophets regularly picture idolatry as adultery: Israel was God's bride and went after other lovers (Hos 2; Jer 3; Ezek 16). The marriage picture shows that idolatry is not a technical breach of rules but betrayal of a love.
- **"Then the anger of the LORD was kindled against his people, and he abhorred his heritage" (v40).** "Abhorred" is a strong word. The God who had chosen Israel as his inheritance (v5) was revolted by what they had become. The Tyndale notes are careful: he abhorred their sin, as he abhors all sin.
- **"He gave them into the hand of the nations" (v41).** This is the cycle of Judges in brief: Israel sins, God hands them to oppressors, they cry out, he raises a deliverer, and they sin again (Judg 2:11–19). The nations they imitated became their masters.
- **"Many times he delivered them, but they were rebellious in their purposes" (v43).** The whole period of the judges and kings is summed up in one verse: repeated rescue, repeated rebellion. **"And were brought low through their iniquity"** points toward the final collapse, the exile.
- **Commentators:** Henry heads vv34–39 "mixing with the Canaanite nations" and vv40–46 "how God had rebuked them for their sins, and yet saved them from ruin." JFB calls the child sacrifice spiritual adultery and notes that the land was polluted with blood. Guzik connects v37 to Molech worship, ties the defiled land to the cry of Abel's blood (Gen 4:10), and passes on Spurgeon's point that Israel's former partners in sin proved harsh masters.

#### Nevertheless, He Looked Upon Their Distress (vv44–48)
- **"Nevertheless, he looked upon their distress, when he heard their cry" (v44).** After thirty-eight verses of sin, this is the great turn. "Nevertheless" is the hinge of the whole psalm, matching the "yet" of v8. Israel's history is sin upon sin, but God's response is not finally to give up.
- **"For their sake he remembered his covenant" (v45).** The key word from Psalm 105 returns. Israel forgot (vv7, 13, 21). God remembered (Ps 105:8, 42; Lev 26:42–45).
- **"And relented according to the abundance of his steadfast love" (v45).** "Relented" (*nacham*) means to change course, to turn from a threatened judgment. It does not mean God is fickle. It means his steadfast love is greater than his people's sin, and he responds to their cry (Exod 32:14; Jonah 3:10). "The abundance of his steadfast love" answers "they did not remember the abundance of your steadfast love" in v7.
- **"He caused them to be pitied by all those who held them captive" (v46).** Solomon had prayed for exactly this: that God would "grant them compassion in the sight of those who carried them captive" (1 Kgs 8:50). It happened when Evil-merodach released King Jehoiachin from prison (2 Kgs 25:27–30), when Cyrus let the exiles return (Ezra 1), and when Persian kings favoured Ezra and Nehemiah.
- **"Save us, O LORD our God, and gather us from among the nations" (v47).** The psalm ends as a prayer from the scattered. They have confessed the whole history, and now they ask to be gathered home so that they "may give thanks to your holy name and glory in your praise." The goal of restoration is worship. 1 Chronicles 16:35 gives the same prayer.
- **"Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting!" (v48).** This doxology closes Book 4 of the Psalms, as similar lines close Books 1, 2 and 3 (41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52). "From everlasting to everlasting" echoes Psalm 90:2, the opening of Book 4. **"And let all the people say, 'Amen!'"** "Amen" means "it is true, so be it." In 1 Chronicles 16:36, "all the people said, 'Amen!' and praised the LORD." **"Praise the LORD!"** The psalm that began with Hallelujah ends with it.
- **Commentators:** Henry ends with prayer and praise and points to the mercy "which even rebellious Israel often found with God." JFB suggests the psalm was written during the captivity, given v46. Guzik calls "nevertheless" the psalm's gracious turning point, notes that the psalmist begins praising before the deliverance arrives, and connects v46 to Cyrus, Darius and Artaxerxes, following Clarke. The Tyndale notes observe that themes of sin and wrath open and close Book 4 (90:7–9; 106:40), but the book ends with hope.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 106 is the most honest history a nation ever sang about itself, and it proves one thing: God's people cannot keep their side of the covenant. Again and again someone has to stand between a holy God and a guilty people, Moses "in the breach" (v23) and Phinehas who "intervened" (v30), and even Moses fails at Meribah. Jesus is the Mediator they foreshadowed and could not be. He stood in the breach not for a moment but on the cross, bearing the wrath that would have destroyed his people, and unlike Moses he never spoke one rash word (1 Pet 2:22–23). He resisted the very temptations Israel fell to in the wilderness, quoting Deuteronomy each time (Matt 4:1–11). And through him God answers the psalm's last prayer: Jesus died "to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad" (John 11:52). The "nevertheless" of v44 is, in the end, the gospel.
