*Setting:* Jerusalem, about AD 30, ten days after the ascension. About 120 believers have been praying together in an upper room (1:14–15), waiting as Jesus told them for "the promise of the Father" (1:4). The city is packed with pilgrims for the Feast of Weeks. This chapter is the hinge of the whole Bible story: the Spirit is poured out, the first Christian sermon is preached, and the church is born in a single day.

#### The Coming of the Holy Spirit (vv1–13)
- **"When the day of Pentecost arrived" (v1).** Pentecost ("fiftieth") was the Feast of Weeks, held fifty days after Passover (Lev 23:15–16). It was a harvest festival, when the firstfruits of the wheat were offered to God. Fitting, then, that this is the day of the first great harvest of souls. Later Jewish tradition also linked the feast with the giving of the Law at Sinai, though it is not clear that link was made as early as Peter's day.
- **"All together in one place" (v1).** Probably the same group from 1:14, still "with one accord." Where they were is not certain: "the entire house" (v2) suggests a home, but a crowd gathers so quickly that some think they had moved to the temple courts.
- **"A sound like a mighty rushing wind" (v2).** In both Hebrew (*ruach*) and Greek (*pneuma*) the word for "spirit" also means "breath" or "wind." Jesus used the picture with Nicodemus (John 3:8), and Ezekiel saw the breath of God bring dry bones to life (Ezek 37:9–14). Luke is careful: a sound *like* wind, tongues *as of* fire. He describes, he does not overexplain.
- **"Divided tongues as of fire… rested on each one" (v3).** Fire signals God's presence: the burning bush, the pillar of fire, Sinai wrapped in smoke and flame (Exod 3:2; 19:18). John had promised that Jesus would baptize "with the Holy Spirit and fire" (Luke 3:16). Under the old covenant the fire came down on the tabernacle and the temple. Now it rests on each believer. They are the new temple.
- **"Began to speak in other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance" (v4).** "Tongues" here are real languages, as vv6–11 make plain. "Gave them utterance" (*apophthengomai*) is a word for weighty, inspired speech. Luke uses it again of Peter's sermon (v14) and of Paul's defense before Festus (26:25).
- **"Devout men from every nation under heaven" (v5).** These are Jews of the dispersion, scattered across the Roman and Parthian worlds over centuries, some living in Jerusalem, many visiting for the feast.
- **"Are not all these who are speaking Galileans?" (v7).** Galileans were looked down on by Jerusalem people as rustic, and their accent gave them away (Matt 26:73). That these men speak polished foreign languages is the shock.
- **The list of nations (vv9–11).** It runs roughly from east to west: from Parthia and Persia (modern Iran and Iraq), through the provinces of Asia Minor (modern Turkey), to Egypt and Libya in Africa, to Rome itself, then Crete and Arabia. Many of these places later hear the gospel, and 1 Peter is written to believers in Pontus, Cappadocia and Asia (1 Pet 1:1). "Proselytes" are Gentiles who had fully converted to Judaism.
- **Babel turned around.** At Babel God confused human languages to scatter a proud people (Gen 11:1–9). At Pentecost he does not erase the languages. He makes each one carry "the mighty works of God" (v11). Unity comes without uniformity.
- **"They are filled with new wine" (v13).** Some mock. The Spirit-filled joy looked like drunkenness to outsiders. Paul makes the same contrast: "do not get drunk with wine… but be filled with the Spirit" (Eph 5:18).
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the fire a "beautiful visible symbol of the burning energy of the Spirit" and holds that the tongues were "real, living languages." Guzik agrees they were known languages, but argues the crowd was mostly overhearing praise to God, not being preached to in those languages, since Peter then preaches to everyone in a common tongue, probably Greek or Aramaic. Matthew Henry, in his shorter commentary, says the Spirit "like fire, melts the heart, burns up the dross, and kindles pious and devout affections in the soul," and notes how the confusion of Babel "much hindered the spread of knowledge and religion."

#### Peter's Sermon at Pentecost (vv14–41)
- **"Peter, standing with the eleven" (v14).** Seven weeks earlier he had denied Jesus to a servant girl by a fire. Now he stands before thousands in the city that crucified Jesus. The Twelve, restored to full number (1:26), stand with him.
- **"It is only the third hour of the day" (v15).** About 9 a.m. Jews did not usually eat or drink until after the morning prayer, so drunkenness at that hour was unlikely.
- **"This is what was uttered through the prophet Joel" (v16).** Peter quotes Joel 2:28–32. He changes Joel's "afterward" to **"in the last days"** (v17). The last days are not only the end of history. They began with the coming of the Messiah and the Spirit, and they run until his return (Heb 1:2; 1 John 2:18).
- **"On all flesh… sons and daughters… male servants and female servants" (vv17–18).** Under the old covenant the Spirit came on prophets, priests and kings. Moses wished "that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Num 11:29). Now the Spirit comes regardless of age, sex or social rank.
- **Wonders, blood, fire, darkened sun (vv19–20).** The cosmic signs are harder to place. Some see them partly fulfilled in the darkness at the cross (Luke 23:44–45) and in Pentecost's wind and fire. Others hold them for the end before the Lord returns. Peter's point is that the age they describe has begun.
- **"Everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved" (v21).** The sermon's goal. In Joel, "Lord" is the LORD, God's own name. By v36 Peter will show that Jesus is that Lord, and Paul reads Joel the same way (Rom 10:9–13).
- **"Attested to you by God with mighty works" (v22).** Peter appeals to what his hearers knew: Jesus' miracles were public.
- **"Delivered up according to the definite plan… you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (v23).** Both are true at once. God planned the cross, and people were fully guilty for it. "Lawless men" means men outside the law of Moses, the Romans. Acts holds the same two truths in the church's prayer (4:27–28).
- **"Loosing the pangs of death" (v24).** "Pangs" is *ōdin*, the word for the pains of childbirth. The Greek Old Testament used it in Psalm 18:4, where the Hebrew can mean either "cords" or "pangs" of death. So death is pictured as a mother in labor that could not keep its child. **"It was not possible for him to be held by it."**
- **Psalm 16 (vv25–28).** David says, "You will not abandon my soul to Hades, or let your Holy One see corruption." Hades is the realm of the dead, and "corruption" the decay of the body. Peter's argument is simple: David died, was buried and decayed, and "his tomb is with us to this day" (v29), a known site in Jerusalem (Josephus mentions it). So David spoke as a prophet about one of his descendants (v30; 2 Sam 7:12–16), the Messiah, whose body did not decay. Paul makes the same argument (13:35–37).
- **"Of that we all are witnesses" (v32).** The Twelve standing beside Peter are the evidence.
- **"He has poured out this that you yourselves are seeing and hearing" (v33).** The exalted Jesus has received the promised Spirit from the Father and given him to his people. What the crowd sees is proof Jesus now reigns.
- **Psalm 110 (vv34–35).** "The Lord said to my Lord, 'Sit at my right hand.'" This is the Old Testament verse most quoted in the New. Jesus himself used it to show the Messiah is more than David's son (Luke 20:41–44). David did not ascend, so the one invited to sit at God's right hand must be someone greater.
- **"God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified" (v36).** The sentence ends with the sting. "Lord" is the title Peter has just used for God, and "Christ" means Messiah, the anointed King. He was always both, but now God has publicly installed him as such by raising and exalting him.
- **"They were cut to the heart" (v37).** *Katanyssō*, used only here in the New Testament, means to strike or prick violently. Peter's words pierced them. **"Brothers, what shall we do?"**
- **"Repent and be baptized… for the forgiveness of your sins" (v38).** To "repent" (*metanoeō*) is to change one's mind and turn around, not simply to feel bad. Baptism "in the name of Jesus Christ" marked public allegiance to the Jesus they had rejected. The Spirit is promised as a gift, not a reward.
- **Readings differ on baptism.** Churches that hold baptism to be the means by which forgiveness is given (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Churches of Christ) read v38 at face value. Most Baptist, Reformed and evangelical readers hold that forgiveness comes through faith and repentance, and baptism is the expected sign and seal of it, pointing to verses where forgiveness comes before baptism (10:43–48). All agree that in Acts no one is converted and left unbaptized.
- **"For you and for your children and for all who are far off" (v39).** "Far off" echoes Isaiah 57:19 and is used by Paul of the Gentiles (Eph 2:13, 17). The promise already reaches past the crowd in front of Peter.
- **"Save yourselves from this crooked generation" (v40).** Moses used the same phrase of Israel in the wilderness (Deut 32:5).
- **"About three thousand souls" (v41).** At Sinai, after the golden calf, about three thousand died (Exod 32:28). At this new beginning, about three thousand are given life. Whether Luke intends the contrast is uncertain, but Paul does set the letter that kills against the Spirit who gives life (2 Cor 3:6). Jerusalem had many large ritual immersion pools near the temple, so baptizing so many was possible.
- **Commentators:** JFB quotes Bengel on v36, "The sting is at the close," and calls baptism "the visible seal of that remission," a sign of the forgiveness it marks. On Psalm 16, JFB says reading it as only about David would "nullify the whole argument of the apostle." Matthew Henry sees in the sermon proof that Peter "was thoroughly recovered from his fall," and writes that sinners whose eyes are opened "cannot but be pricked to the heart for sin." Guzik plays on the birth-pain image in v24, making the tomb a womb, which the lexicon supports for *ōdin*. He also cites Longenecker that baptism was the expected sign of conversion but not the condition of salvation, and contrasts Peter's sword in the garden with the Word that now pierces hearts.

#### The Fellowship of the Believers (vv42–47)
- **"They devoted themselves" (v42).** The same word as 1:14 (*proskartereō*): steady, persistent attention. Four things follow.
- **"The apostles' teaching."** The new believers sat under the teaching of the men who had been with Jesus. That teaching is now found in the New Testament.
- **"The fellowship."** *Koinōnia* means sharing in something together. It covers shared life, shared faith and, as vv44–45 show, shared money.
- **"The breaking of bread."** It likely means shared meals that included the Lord's Supper, which in the first churches was eaten as part of a real meal (1 Cor 11:20–26). Luke uses the phrase at Emmaus, where the risen Jesus "was known to them in the breaking of the bread" (Luke 24:35).
- **"The prayers."** The definite article suggests set prayer times, likely including the temple's daily prayers, which Peter and John attend in 3:1.
- **"Awe came upon every soul" (v43).** "Awe" is *phobos*, fear in the sense of reverent wonder. God was plainly at work.
- **"Had all things in common… selling their possessions… as any had need" (vv44–45).** This was voluntary and need-driven, not a law. The verbs are ongoing: they kept selling as needs arose. Peter later tells Ananias his land and money remained his own to keep (5:4). Many pilgrims may have stayed in Jerusalem after the feast to learn, which would explain urgent needs. The result fulfills the ideal of Deuteronomy 15:4: "there will be no poor among you" (compare 4:34).
- **"Attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes" (v46).** They did not leave Judaism behind. They still worshiped at the temple, and they met in homes. The church had no buildings for its first two centuries.
- **"Glad and generous hearts" (v46).** "Generous" can mean simple or sincere, an undivided heart.
- **"Having favor with all the people" (v47).** For now. Opposition comes from the leaders in chapter 4, but ordinary Jerusalem people liked them.
- **"The Lord added to their number day by day" (v47).** It is the Lord who grows the church. They prayed, worshiped and loved one another, and he added "those who were being saved."
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry describes the sharing as "not by destroying property, but doing away selfishness, and causing charity." Guzik likewise argues it was not early communism, since it was voluntary and seems not to have lasted, and the Jerusalem church later needed help from others (11:29; Rom 15:26). JFB reads "breaking of bread" as including common meals as well as the Lord's Supper. Guzik defines *koinōnia* as sharing in life, love and mission, which matches the Tyndale notes and the lexicon.

#### Pointing to Christ
Pentecost is about Jesus. The Spirit is "the promise" he received from the Father and poured out (v33). Peter's sermon is all Christ: his miracles, his death by God's plan, his resurrection foretold by David, his throne at God's right hand. The crowd's question, "What shall we do?", is answered with his name, and the new community gathers around his apostles' teaching and his table. The fire that once filled the temple now rests on his people, because the risen Lord has made them his dwelling.
