*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC. Chapter 42 ended on a grim note: God himself had handed Jacob over to plunderers, "it set him on fire all around, but he did not understand" (42:25). Chapter 43 opens with two of the most important words in the book: "But now." Nothing in Israel has changed. They are still blind and deaf (v8), still weary of God (v22). What changes is that God speaks. The chapter is built like the trial scenes in chapter 41, but this time Israel is called as God's witness. It also holds some of the most loved promises in Scripture, a "new thing" bigger than the Exodus, and one of the clearest Old Testament statements that God forgives sin for his own sake.

#### Israel's Only Savior (vv1–28)
- **"But now thus says the LORD, he who created you, O Jacob, he who formed you, O Israel" (v1).** "Created" (*bara'*) is the verb of Genesis 1:1, used only with God as subject. "Formed" (*yatsar*) is the potter's word, used of God shaping Adam from the dust (Gen 2:7). Isaiah applies creation language to the nation. God made Israel as surely as he made the world, so he has a maker's claim on them.
- **"Fear not, for I have redeemed you" (v1).** "Redeemed" (*ga'al*) is the work of the *go'el*, the next of kin who buys back a relative sold into slavery or recovers lost family land (Lev 25:25, 47–49; Ruth 4). The verb is in the past tense even though the exile is not over. God speaks of the rescue as already done because his word makes it certain.
- **"I have called you by name, you are mine" (v1).** To name something in the ancient world was to claim it. This is personal ownership, not ownership of property. Jesus says the good shepherd "calls his own sheep by name" (John 10:3).
- **"When you pass through the waters, I will be with you" (v2).** Note "when," not "if." God does not promise his people a path that goes around the waters and the fire. He promises to be with them in it. Waters recall the Red Sea and the Jordan (Exod 14; Josh 3). Isaiah has used a flooding river as a picture of an invading army (8:7–8). Fire recalls the exile as God's burning judgment (42:25). Later, three young men of Judah walked in a Babylonian furnace with a fourth figure beside them (Dan 3:25).
- **"I am the LORD your God, the Holy One of Israel, your Savior" (v3).** "Savior" (*moshia'*) is from the same root as the names Joshua, Isaiah and Jesus (*Yeshua*). It is a key word in this chapter (vv3, 11, 12).
- **"I give Egypt as your ransom, Cush and Seba in exchange for you" (v3).** "Ransom" (*kopher*) is the price paid to set a person free (Exod 21:30; 30:12). Cush is the upper Nile region (modern Sudan), and Seba was probably nearby in Africa (Gen 10:7). It is a picture of how much Israel is worth to God: he would give whole nations for them. Some see a historical fulfillment too. Persia, which freed the Jews, went on to conquer Egypt in 525 BC, as if Egypt were Cyrus's payment for releasing Israel. That is a scholarly suggestion, not something the text spells out.
- **"Because you are precious in my eyes, and honored, and I love you" (v4).** This is the reason behind everything. It is not that Israel had earned anything, because v22–24 shows they had not. They are precious because God has set his love on them (Deut 7:7–8). The New Testament uses the same pattern: "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Rom 5:8).
- **"I will bring your offspring from the east, and from the west I will gather you" (v5).** The exile scattered Israel well beyond Babylon. God promises to call them back from every direction: "I will say to the north, Give up, and to the south, Do not withhold" (v6). He speaks to the compass points like a master giving orders.
- **"My sons… my daughters… everyone who is called by my name, whom I created for my glory" (vv6–7).** "Sons and daughters" makes the promise include everyone. And here is the reason God made his people at all: for his glory. This is not God using people. It is the highest dignity they could have, to show what God is like (Eph 1:12).
- **"Bring out the people who are blind, yet have eyes" (v8).** Now the courtroom opens again (41:1, 21). God calls Israel as a witness, and the first thing he says is that his witnesses are blind and deaf (42:18–20). It is a striking scene: the Lord's case rests on people who have barely seen what he did.
- **"Who among them can declare this, and show us the former things?" (v9).** The nations are called too, with their gods' witnesses. The test is the same as in 41:22–23: predict the future, or explain what the past meant. No one comes forward.
- **"You are my witnesses," declares the LORD, "and my servant whom I have chosen" (v10).** Israel is called to testify. The word "witnesses" (*'edim*) is a legal word for someone who saw something and can swear to it. Israel's whole history was evidence: the God who foretold the exile through his prophets would also foretell its end. Jesus says the same to his disciples: "You are witnesses of these things" (Luke 24:48; Acts 1:8). Henry and some others take "my servant" here as the Messiah of 42:1, but most read it of Israel, as the parallel with "you" suggests.
- **"That you may know and believe me and understand that I am he" (v10).** Being a witness does as much for the witness as for the court. Israel will come to know God as they testify about him. "I am he" (*'ani hu'*) is a near-name for God in these chapters (41:4; 43:13, 25; 46:4). Jesus picks it up: "unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins" (John 8:24; 13:19).
- **"Before me no god was formed, nor shall there be any after me" (v10).** "Formed" is the word used for idols, which are made by craftsmen. God was not made. No god came before him and none will come after. This is among the plainest statements of monotheism in the Bible (Deut 6:4).
- **"I, I am the LORD, and besides me there is no savior" (v11).** The repeated "I" is emphatic. In the New Testament, Jesus is called "Savior" (Luke 2:11; Titus 2:13), and Peter says "there is salvation in no one else" (Acts 4:12). Christians have read this as one of many places where the New Testament gives Jesus what the Old Testament reserves for the LORD alone.
- **"When there was no strange god among you" (v12).** God's past acts of rescue, at the Exodus and at Sinai, happened before Israel took up foreign gods. No idol could take the credit.
- **"There is none who can deliver from my hand; I work, and who can turn it back?" (v13).** No one can snatch from God's hand, and no one can reverse what he does. Jesus says of his sheep, "no one will snatch them out of my hand" (John 10:28).
- **"For your sake I send to Babylon" (v14).** This is the first time in these chapters that Babylon is named as the enemy. God will send an army against it, the Medes and Persians under Cyrus (13:17; 45:1).
- **"The Chaldeans, in the ships in which they rejoice" (v14).** The Chaldeans were the people who ruled the Neo-Babylonian empire. Babylon sat on the Euphrates and carried on river and gulf trade, and its boats were a source of pride. The Hebrew of this line is difficult, and translations vary. The ESV's picture is of the proud boatmen of Babylon fleeing in the same ships.
- **"I am the LORD, your Holy One, the Creator of Israel, your King" (v15).** Four titles in one line. Jerusalem had lost its human king. Israel's real King had not lost his throne.
- **"Who makes a way in the sea… who brings forth chariot and horse… they are extinguished, quenched like a wick" (vv16–17).** This is the Red Sea, the greatest rescue in Israel's memory (Exod 14–15). Pharaoh's army lies down and never gets up. "Quenched like a wick" is the image of 42:3. The servant will not quench a faint wick, but the oppressors' army is snuffed out like one.
- **"Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old" (v18).** After telling them to look back at the Exodus, God tells them not to dwell on it. This is not forgetting God's past works, which the Psalms command people to remember (Ps 105:5). It means not living in the past as if God's best days were over. What is coming is so great it will make even the Red Sea look small.
- **"Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it?" (v19).** "Springs forth" is a gardener's word for a plant sprouting (42:9). It is already starting to grow, if they have eyes to see it. In the first Exodus God made a way through the sea. In the new one he will make "a way in the wilderness and rivers in the desert," the hundreds of miles of desert between Babylon and home. Paul uses the same language for new life in Christ: "the old has passed away; behold, the new has come" (2 Cor 5:17), and the risen Christ says, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Rev 21:5).
- **"The wild beasts will honor me, the jackals and the ostriches" (v20).** Jackals and ostriches are desert animals, often linked in Isaiah with ruin and desolation (13:21–22; 34:13). Now even they have water and honor God. The new Exodus refreshes creation as well as people (Rom 8:19–21).
- **"The people whom I formed for myself that they might declare my praise" (v21).** This verse sums up why God redeems: so that his people will praise him. Peter quotes the idea: "a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness" (1 Pet 2:9).
- **"Yet you did not call upon me, O Jacob; but you have been weary of me, O Israel!" (v22).** The tone turns sharply. After the soaring promises comes an honest charge. God formed Israel to praise him, and they would not even pray. Worse, they were tired of him. Malachi records the same attitude: "What a weariness this is" (Mal 1:13).
- **"You have not brought me your sheep for burnt offerings" (v23).** This could mean that in exile they could not offer sacrifices, because there was no temple. It could also mean that even before the exile their offerings were not truly *to God*, empty ritual that did not count (1:11–15). Either way, the point is that Israel's relationship with God was not resting on their worship.
- **"I have not burdened you with offerings, or wearied you with frankincense" (v23).** God had never asked for anything crushing. The sacrifices of the law were not meant as a heavy yoke (Mic 6:3, 6–8). Jesus says, "my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matt 11:30).
- **"You have not bought me sweet cane with money" (v24).** "Sweet cane" (*qaneh*) was an aromatic reed imported from far away (Jer 6:20). It went into the holy anointing oil (Exod 30:23). It was costly, and they had not spent their money on God.
- **"But you have burdened me with your sins; you have wearied me with your iniquities" (v24).** Here is a wordplay English cannot fully show. "Burdened" in v23 and v24 is from *'avad*, the root of "servant" (*'eved*). God says: I did not make you *serve* me with offerings, but you have made *me* serve with your sins. JFB catches it: "Our sin made the Son of God to become 'a servant.'" The Servant of chapters 42–53 is the one who will finally carry that burden (53:11–12).
- **"I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake" (v25).** After the charge, the verdict is not condemnation but forgiveness, and it comes without any change in Israel first. "Blots out" (*machah*) means to wipe away. It is used of the flood wiping out life (Gen 6:7) and of erasing writing from a scroll (Exod 32:32–33; Num 5:23). The record of their sins is wiped clean. "For my own sake" means the reason lies in God, not in them. That is what makes it secure.
- **"And I will not remember your sins" (v25).** God does not suffer from memory loss. Not remembering means he chooses not to hold them against his people or bring them up again. Jeremiah's new covenant promise says the same (Jer 31:34), and Hebrews quotes it twice (Heb 8:12; 10:17).
- **"Put me in remembrance; let us argue together; set forth your case, that you may be proved right" (v26).** It is a dare. If Israel has a defense, let them present it. The point is that they have none. Their only hope is the forgiveness just offered (1:18).
- **"Your first father sinned, and your mediators transgressed against me" (v27).** Who is the "first father"? Some, including Guzik, take it as Adam, the head of the whole human race. Many think it is Jacob, Israel's own ancestor, whose scheming is held up in Hosea 12:2–4. Others take it as the forefathers in general. In every reading the point is the same: sin runs back to the very beginning of Israel's story. "Mediators" (*melitsim*) means interpreters or go-betweens. It is the word for Joseph's interpreter (Gen 42:23). It probably means Israel's priests and prophets, who were supposed to stand between God and the people, and failed.
- **"Therefore I will profane the princes of the sanctuary, and deliver Jacob to utter destruction" (v28).** "Princes of the sanctuary" are the leading priests (1 Chr 24:5). God stripped them of their holy standing when the temple fell. "Utter destruction" is *cherem*, the word for something devoted to destruction, as Jericho was (Josh 6:17). The chapter ends with judgment that has already happened, but in the light of v25. The forgiveness is greater than the sin.
- **Commentators:** Henry on v3: "if this was reckoned so great a thing, to give Egypt for their ransom, what reason have we to admire God's love to us in giving his own Son to be a ransom for us!" JFB notes that *kopher* comes from a root meaning "to cover," as Noah's ark was covered with pitch, and so it becomes the word for what covers sin. Guzik and Henry both link the furnace in Daniel 3 to v2. Guzik observes that the walking pace in v2 is a calm one: God can steady his people enough that they walk, not run, through the fire. On vv18–19, Henry warns against thinking "the former days were better than these," and Guzik balances that: remember God's past works, but do not get stuck there. On v25, Henry says the pardon "comes in very strangely" right after such a charge, and draws the lesson that "where sin has been exceedingly sinful grace appears exceedingly gracious." The commentators differ on v27. Guzik reads Adam. JFB rejects both Adam and Abraham and takes it as the earliest ancestors in general. Henry takes it as the forefathers but adds that it should "put us in mind" of Adam.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter shows two burdens. Israel was weary of God, and God was made to serve under the weight of their sins. The God who forgives "for my own sake" and blots out transgressions does not do it by ignoring them. Isaiah goes on to show the Servant who carries that weight: "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:6). The Savior beside whom there is no other came as Jesus, whose name means "the LORD saves." He gave himself as the ransom (Mark 10:45), and the people formed to declare God's praise are now gathered from east and west, north and south (Luke 13:29).
