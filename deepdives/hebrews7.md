*Setting:* The sermon to the Hebrews, probably written in the 60s to Jewish Christians tempted to drift back to the old ways, finally gets to the subject it has been circling. Back in 5:6–10 the writer called Jesus "a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek," quoting Psalm 110:4, and then broke off because his hearers had grown "dull of hearing" (5:11). Chapter 6 was the interruption: a rebuke, a warning, and God's oath to Abraham, ending with Jesus gone behind the curtain "as a forerunner… a high priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" (6:20). Now comes the solid food. Melchizedek appears in only two Old Testament passages, a short scene in Genesis 14:18–20 and one line in Psalm 110:4. The writer reads the two together to show that Israel's own Scriptures had foretold a priest greater than Aaron, from outside the tribe of Levi, appointed by an oath and serving forever. For hearers who missed the temple, its priests and its sacrifices, this is the heart of the argument: in Jesus they have not lost a priesthood but gained a far better one.

#### The Priestly Order of Melchizedek (vv1–10)
- **"For this Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of the Most High God" (v1).** The writer retells Genesis 14. Four eastern kings had raided the cities of the plain and carried off Abraham's nephew Lot. Abraham chased them with 318 trained men, beat them and brought everyone back (Gen 14:1–16). On the way home a king nobody has heard of steps out, brings bread and wine, and blesses him. "Salem" is almost certainly Jerusalem (Ps 76:2), the city where David's son would one day reign and where the temple would stand. "God Most High" (Hebrew *El Elyon*) is the name Abraham himself takes up in the next scene for "the LORD, God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth" (Gen 14:22). So this is a priest of the true God, long before Moses, Aaron or Levi.
- **King and priest at once.** Israel kept those two offices apart. When King Uzziah tried to burn incense in the temple, he was struck with leprosy (2 Chr 26:16–21). Melchizedek holds both, and so does the promised Messiah. Psalm 110 makes the same figure both king at God's right hand (110:1) and priest forever (110:4), and Zechariah pictures one who "shall be a priest on his throne" (Zech 6:13).
- **"Met Abraham… and blessed him" (v1).** The meeting has only two actions, and the writer will build his whole case on them: Melchizedek blesses, and Abraham gives.
- **"Abraham apportioned a tenth part of everything" (v2).** A tenth of the spoils of the battle (v4). Giving a tenth was an old way of honoring a god or a king, and Abraham does it centuries before the law of Moses commanded tithes (Lev 27:30; Num 18:21).
- **"King of righteousness… king of peace" (v2).** The writer translates the names. *Melek* is Hebrew for "king" and *tsedeq* for "righteousness." Salem sounds like *shalom*, "peace." Jewish teachers often drew meaning out of names this way. Notice the order: righteousness first, then peace. That is the order of the gospel too (Isa 32:17; Rom 5:1).
- **"Without father or mother or genealogy" (v3).** This is the line that puzzles readers most. The writer is arguing from what Genesis leaves out. Almost every important figure in Genesis comes with a family line, and every priest of Israel had to prove his descent from Aaron. Some who could not find their names in the records were barred from the priesthood (Ezra 2:61–63). Melchizedek arrives in Genesis with no parents, no birth and no death recorded. He walks into the story and out of it. The text does not say he had no parents. It says Scripture presents him without them, and so he serves as a picture of a priesthood that does not depend on ancestry and does not end.
- **"Resembling the Son of God he continues a priest forever" (v3).** "Resembling" is *aphomoioō*, "to make like," used only here in the New Testament. The direction matters. Melchizedek is made to look like the Son, not the other way round. Jesus is the original and Melchizedek is the sketch.
- **Who was Melchizedek?** Three main answers have been given. Jewish tradition often identified him with Shem, Noah's son, who was still alive in Abraham's day by Genesis's numbers. Some Christians have thought he was an appearance of Christ himself before his birth. Most interpreters, ancient and modern, take him as a real Canaanite king-priest whom God placed in the story as a type, a living picture, of Christ. The third view fits the writer's own wording best: if Melchizedek *resembles* the Son of God, he is not the Son. It is also worth knowing that Jews of this era were already fascinated by Melchizedek. One of the Dead Sea Scrolls (11QMelchizedek) pictures him as a heavenly deliverer at the end of days, so the writer's hearers may have come with ideas of their own about him.
- **"See how great this man was" (v4).** Now the argument. Abraham is "the patriarch," the father of the whole nation, the man to whom God made the promises. If even Abraham gave a tenth to Melchizedek, Melchizedek ranks above Abraham.
- **The Levites collected tithes from their brothers (v5).** Under the law the tribe of Levi received the tithes of the other tribes (Num 18:21–26). But that was a right among equals. All of them were "descended from Abraham," and the Levites had it only because a commandment gave it to them.
- **"This man who does not have his descent from them received tithes from Abraham" (v6).** Melchizedek had no commandment and no tribe, yet he received a tithe from the forefather of them all, and he "blessed him who had the promises."
- **"The inferior is blessed by the superior" (v7).** In the Bible a blessing flows down: a father blesses his children (Gen 49), and a priest blesses the people (Num 6:22–27). The person who gives the blessing stands above the one who receives it. So Abraham, great as he was, stood below Melchizedek in that moment.
- **"One of whom it is testified that he lives" (v8).** The Levitical priests were "mortal men," literally "men who die." Each one served for a while and then was buried. Of Melchizedek, Scripture records no death, so in the record he simply goes on living. The writer is again reading the silence of Genesis in the light of Psalm 110's "forever." The real fulfilment is Jesus, who truly "always lives" (v25).
- **"One might even say that Levi himself… paid tithes through Abraham" (vv9–10).** "One might even say" is the writer's own signal that this is a figure of speech, a way of putting it. Levi was Abraham's great-grandson and not yet born. But in the Bible's way of thinking, a forefather acts for the descendants "still in his loins." When Abraham bowed to Melchizedek, the whole priestly tribe bowed with him. So the priests who received tithes had, in a sense, already paid them to a greater priest.
- **Commentators:** Henry weighs the same three views on Melchizedek's identity. He doubts the Shem theory and rejects the idea that he was Christ, since that would make "Christ a type of himself," and he sides with the common view of a Canaanite king made a type of Christ. On v3 he insists the phrase "must not be understood according to the letter." JFB agrees that "Melchisedec was, in his priesthood, 'made like' Christ" and denies that he was a superhuman being. On "without genealogy" JFB notes that "a Levitical priest could not dispense with the proof of his descent." Guzik also draws attention to the rare verb for "resembling," and stresses that the comparison runs from Christ to Melchizedek. He reports the range of views on who Melchizedek was without settling it, and points out that Abraham's tithe was freely given, while the Levites' tithes were required by law. On vv9–10 Henry uses the same idea of being in a forefather's loins to explain how all humanity was in Adam when he sinned, and JFB adds a sharp point: Christ never paid tithes in Abraham in this sense, "for He never was in the loins of an earthly father." The Tyndale notes, like Guzik, read "so to speak" as the writer admitting this is a figurative way of saying it.

#### Jesus Compared to Melchizedek (vv11–28)
- **"If perfection had been attainable through the Levitical priesthood" (v11).** "Perfection" (*teleiōsis*) in Hebrews does not mean flawlessness. It means reaching the goal, being brought all the way to God with sins truly dealt with and the conscience made clean (9:9; 10:1, 14). The argument is simple. Psalm 110 was written by David, centuries after Aaron's priesthood was set up. If that priesthood had been enough, why would God announce a different priest, of a different order? The promise of a new priest is itself proof that the old one could not finish the job.
- **"For under it the people received the law" (v11).** The writer's aside shows how tightly the law and the priesthood were bound together. The whole Mosaic system of sacrifice, purity and atonement rested on the priests.
- **"A change in the law as well" (v12).** "Change" is *metathesis*. It is the word Hebrews uses later for Enoch being "taken up" (11:5) and for the "removal" of things that can be shaken (12:27). If the priesthood moves to a new priest, the law that built everything around Aaron's priesthood cannot stay as it was.
- **"Our Lord was descended from Judah" (v14).** Jesus came from the royal tribe, David's line (Matt 1:1–16; Rev 5:5). Under the law of Moses that made him ineligible to serve at the altar. As Hebrews says later, "if he were on earth, he would not be a priest at all" (8:4). The writer does not hide this. He uses it. Jesus' priesthood has to rest on something other than the law of Moses, and Psalm 110 says what: the order of Melchizedek.
- **"Not on the basis of a legal requirement concerning bodily descent" (v16).** Literally "a law of a fleshly commandment." Aaron's sons became priests by being born into the right family. Jesus became priest on a completely different basis.
- **"By the power of an indestructible life" (v16).** "Indestructible" is *akatalytos*, used only here: "indissoluble," a life that cannot be broken up or dissolved. Death broke up every Levitical priesthood. It could not break this one. Jesus truly died, but death could not hold him (Acts 2:24). The phrase points to the resurrection. He is priest forever because he lives forever.
- **"You are a priest forever" (v17).** The writer quotes Psalm 110:4 a second time, and this time the stress falls on "forever."
- **"A former commandment is set aside because of its weakness and uselessness" (v18).** "Set aside" is *athetēsis*, a legal word for annulling or cancelling something. Hebrews uses it once more, of Christ appearing "to put away sin" (9:26). The "former commandment" is the law about priests descending from Aaron. "Weak" and "useless" sound harsh, but the writer is precise about what he means.
- **"For the law made nothing perfect" (v19).** The law was good and from God. What it could not do was bring anyone all the way to God. It could point out sin and picture its cleansing, but it could not take sin away (10:4). Paul says something similar: the law was "weakened by the flesh" (Rom 8:3).
- **"A better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God" (v19).** "Better" is one of the key words of Hebrews: a better hope here, a better covenant (v22), better promises (8:6), better sacrifices (9:23). "Draw near" is the language of worship. Under the old system only priests came near, and only the high priest once a year went into the Most Holy Place. Now every believer is invited near (4:16; 10:19–22).
- **"It was not without an oath" (vv20–21).** Aaron's sons became priests by birth and by ritual, with no oath from God. The priest of Psalm 110 is appointed with a sworn promise: "The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind." Chapter 6 has just explained that God added an oath to his promise to Abraham to show it could never change (6:17–18). The same is true here. This priesthood will never be replaced.
- **"This makes Jesus the guarantor of a better covenant" (v22).** "Guarantor" is *engyos*, another word used only here. In the ancient world a guarantor, or surety, stood behind someone else's debt or promise and took responsibility if it failed. Jesus stands behind the new covenant with his own life. It is "better" because it does not rest on the people's faithfulness, which kept failing, but on his. This is the first time Hebrews says "covenant," and the next chapter will quote Jeremiah's promise of a new one (8:8–12). Some readers also hear a sound-play in the Greek between *engizomen*, "we draw near" (v19), and *engyos*, "guarantor."
- **"The former priests were many in number" (v23).** Josephus, the Jewish historian, counted dozens of high priests between Aaron and the fall of the temple, and his total runs to more than eighty. Each one's service was ended by death, and someone else had to take over. Aaron himself had to hand his robes to his son on a mountain before he died (Num 20:25–28).
- **"He holds his priesthood permanently" (v24).** The word means unchangeable, and it can also carry the sense of "not passing to another." Jesus has no successor because he needs none.
- **"Able to save to the uttermost" (v25).** "To the uttermost" is *eis to panteles*, "completely" or "entirely." The lexicon notes it may also have a time sense, "for all time," and both fit. He saves fully, and he saves forever. The only other use of the word is the woman bent over for eighteen years who could not "fully" straighten herself, until Jesus did it for her (Luke 13:11–13).
- **"Those who draw near to God through him" (v25).** The salvation is for those who come, and the way is through him. There is no approach to God that goes around Jesus (John 14:6; 1 Tim 2:5).
- **"He always lives to make intercession for them" (v25).** "Intercession" is *entynchanō*, to approach someone on another's behalf, to petition. Paul uses the same verb: "Christ Jesus… is interceding for us" (Rom 8:34). John calls him "an advocate with the Father" (1 John 2:1). It does not mean the Father is unwilling and has to be talked round, since the Father sent the Son. It means the risen Jesus, who bears our nature and finished the sacrifice, stands before God for his people continually. On earth he prayed for Peter before Peter's fall (Luke 22:31–32). He has not stopped.
- **"Holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners" (v26).** Five descriptions. "Holy" is devoted to God and faithful. "Innocent" means without evil or guile. "Unstained" means undefiled, the word for ritual purity, which the high priest guarded carefully before the Day of Atonement. Jesus' purity is moral, not just ritual. "Separated from sinners" does not mean he kept away from them. He was called "a friend of tax collectors and sinners" (Matt 11:19). It means he is unlike them, untouched by their sin, and now set apart in glory, "exalted above the heavens."
- **"To offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins" (v27).** On the Day of Atonement the high priest had to offer a bull for his own sins before he could offer for the people (Lev 16:6, 11). Jesus had no sins of his own, as Hebrews has already said (4:15). "Daily" is a puzzle, since the high priest's offering for his own sin came once a year. The writer may be combining the daily offerings of the priesthood with the yearly one (Exod 29:38–42), or speaking of the high priest as head of a system whose sacrifices never stopped. Either way, the point is the endless repetition.
- **"He did this once for all when he offered up himself" (v27).** Here for the first time the priest is also the sacrifice. "Once for all" (*ephapax*) means once, never to be repeated, and it rings through the next chapters (9:12; 10:10). Nothing more needs to be offered.
- **"The word of the oath, which came later than the law, appoints a Son" (v28).** The chapter ends by summing up the contrast. The law appointed men "in their weakness," sinful and mortal. The oath of Psalm 110, spoken by David centuries after Moses, appoints the Son. A later word from God sets the terms of what came earlier. And this Son "has been made perfect forever," brought through suffering, death and resurrection to be fully qualified as the priest his people need (2:10; 5:8–9).
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Christ's priesthood "a priesthood of a higher order," and on v12 says plainly "there must of necessity be a change of the law." On v16 he writes, "The life and immortality which he had in himself were his right and title to the priesthood." On v22 Henry and JFB give almost the same picture: Christ is "a surety for us to God and for God to us" (Henry), "God's surety for man, and man's surety for God" (JFB). Guzik explains the surety as a cosigner, so that the covenant depends on what Jesus has done and not on us, and he notes that the word for covenant is closer to a will written by one party than a contract the two sides negotiate. On v25 Henry says Christ is "able to save to the utmost—in all times, in all cases, in every juncture," and JFB, "altogether, perfectly, so that nothing should be wanting afterwards for ever." Guzik makes the point that Jesus saves people *to* the uttermost, all the way, and is careful to describe his intercession not as calming an angry Father but as standing for his people against every accusation (Rom 8:33–34). On v26 JFB reads "undefiled" as "not defiled by stain contracted from others," and on v27 Henry notes that Christ "needed only to offer up once for the people, never at all for himself." JFB adds that the one sacrifice could cover all sins for ever because of "its absolute spotlessness." The Tyndale notes add that "once for all" means once and never repeated, not "once for all people."

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter points to Christ throughout. Melchizedek is a shadow cast backward in Genesis, a king of righteousness and peace with no recorded beginning or end. Jesus is the one who casts it. He is the king-priest of Psalm 110, from the tribe of Judah, appointed by God's oath and holding office by a life death could not break. Israel's priests changed with every funeral, and their sacrifices never stopped. Jesus offered himself once and still stands before God for his people. For believers tempted to go back to the temple, the writer's point is that going back would mean leaving the better priest for the weaker one. Those who come to God through Jesus have a priest who saves completely, does not die, and does not stop praying for them.
