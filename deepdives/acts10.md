*Setting:* About AD 37–40. The gospel has spread from Jerusalem to Judea and Samaria (chs 8–9), and an Ethiopian official has believed on a desert road, but the church is still Jewish in its members and its habits. Samaritans and the eunuch were all in some way inside the circle of Israel's faith. Peter is staying in Joppa on the coast with Simon, a tanner (9:43), and Saul, the future apostle to the Gentiles, is waiting in Tarsus (9:30). This chapter is the great turning point of Acts: God opens the door of faith to the Gentiles, and he does it through Peter. Luke thinks it so important that Peter retells it in chapter 11 and appeals to it at the Jerusalem council (15:7–11).

#### Peter and Cornelius (vv1–8)
- **"At Caesarea" (v1).** Caesarea Maritima, the grand port city Herod the Great built on the coast and named after Caesar Augustus. It was the seat of the Roman governor of Judea and very much a Gentile city, with temples to Rome and the emperor. To devout Jews it was the heart of the occupation.
- **"A centurion of what was known as the Italian Cohort" (v1).** A centurion commanded about a hundred men. A cohort was about six hundred, a tenth of a legion. Inscriptions confirm that an Italian cohort of volunteers was in Syria in the first century. Centurions in Luke-Acts are often good men (Luke 7:1–10; 23:47; Acts 27:43). Cornelius was a Roman officer of the army that crucified Jesus.
- **"A devout man who feared God with all his household" (v2).** "God-fearers" were Gentiles who worshiped the God of Israel, attended synagogue and kept many of its practices, but did not become full converts through circumcision. **"Gave alms generously to the people, and prayed continually."** Alms and prayer were the marks of Jewish piety (Matt 6:2–6). "The people" means the Jewish people. He was generous to the nation his army occupied.
- **"About the ninth hour" (v3).** About 3 p.m., the hour of the afternoon sacrifice and prayer at the temple (3:1). Cornelius keeps Israel's prayer times far from the temple.
- **"What is it, Lord?" (v4).** Here "Lord" (*kyrie*) is respectful address to the angel, "Sir."
- **"Your prayers and your alms have ascended as a memorial before God" (v4).** "Memorial" is temple language. Part of a grain offering was burned "as its memorial portion," rising as a pleasing aroma to God (Lev 2:2, 9, 16). Cornelius could not offer sacrifices at the temple, but God counted his prayers and gifts as an offering (compare Ps 141:2; Heb 13:16).
- **Was Cornelius already saved?** Luke shows him sincere, devout and heard by God, yet the angel sends him to Peter, and Peter later says the angel told him Peter would "declare to you a message by which you will be saved" (11:14). The best reading is that he was a seeker whom God was drawing, as Lydia later is (16:14). His prayers were heard and answered with the gospel. Good works did not make him a Christian, but God honored his seeking by sending the message he needed.
- **"Send men to Joppa and bring one Simon who is called Peter" (v5).** The angel does not preach the gospel himself. God gives that job to people. Joppa is about thirty miles south of Caesarea, a day's journey.
- **"Two of his servants and a devout soldier" (v7).** Cornelius's faith has spread through his household and his men.
- **Commentators:** JFB calls the Italian cohort Italian soldiers "as distinguished from native soldiers," possibly a guard for the governor, and takes the memorial as an acceptable offering before God. Guzik notes that patriotic Jews would have despised Cornelius as an officer of the occupation, and that for all his goodness he still needed Jesus. Matthew Henry: "Pure and undefiled religion is sometimes found where we least expect it."

#### Peter's Vision (vv9–33)
- **"Peter went up on the housetop about the sixth hour to pray" (v9).** Noon. Flat roofs were used as living space, and a quiet place to pray. Some Jews prayed three times a day (Ps 55:17; Dan 6:10). The timing is precise: as Cornelius's men approach the city, God prepares Peter.
- **"He fell into a trance" (v10).** The Greek is *ekstasis*, which gives English "ecstasy." The lexicon gives "trance": a state in which a person is taken out of their usual awareness. Here God uses it to show Peter something, while his hunger shapes what he sees.
- **"Something like a great sheet… let down by its four corners" (v11).** The four corners may suggest the four corners of the earth: all nations.
- **"All kinds of animals and reptiles and birds of the air" (v12).** Clean and unclean mixed together. Leviticus 11 divided animals into clean, which Israelites could eat, and unclean, which they could not. These rules marked Israel off from the nations around them (Lev 20:24–26).
- **"Rise, Peter; kill and eat" (v13).** "Kill" is the word for slaughtering for food or sacrifice.
- **"By no means, Lord; for I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean" (v14).** "Common" (*koinos*) meant ordinary, not set apart, and therefore profane. "Unclean" means ritually impure. Peter's answer echoes Ezekiel, who also protested an order to eat what was unclean (Ezek 4:14). It is a contradiction: "No, Lord." The man who said "Lord" while refusing had done it before (Matt 16:22; John 13:8).
- **"What God has made clean, do not call common" (v15).** The heart of the vision. Jesus had already taught that food cannot make a person unclean, and Mark comments, "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). Peter, whose memories most scholars believe lie behind Mark's Gospel, apparently had not taken it in.
- **"This happened three times" (v16).** Repetition confirms (Gen 41:32). It may also recall Peter's three denials and three restorations (John 21:15–17).
- **What did the vision mean?** At first it is about food. But Peter soon applies it to people: "God has shown me that I should not call any *person* common or unclean" (v28). The food laws were a fence around Israel, and God was taking the fence down. If no food is unclean, then eating with Gentiles is no longer defiling, and the main social barrier between Jews and Gentiles falls. Christians have generally read this as setting aside the food laws for believers (Rom 14:14; Col 2:16). Some Messianic Jewish readers hold that the vision was only about people, and that Jewish believers may still keep kosher. Either way the main point is clear: God accepts Gentiles as Gentiles.
- **"While Peter was pondering the vision, the Spirit said to him" (v19).** Vision, then circumstances, then the Spirit's direct word. **"Without hesitation, for I have sent them"** (v20). The verb (*diakrinomai*) can mean to doubt or to make distinctions. Both fit: Peter is not to hesitate, and not to discriminate.
- **"Cornelius, a centurion, an upright and God-fearing man, who is well spoken of by the whole Jewish nation" (v22).** The messengers stress his good name among Jews, to reassure Peter.
- **"So he invited them in to be his guests" (v23).** A first step. A Jew hosting Gentiles in a tanner's house, and eating with them.
- **"Some of the brothers from Joppa accompanied him" (v23).** Six, according to 11:12. Peter brings witnesses. He knows this will need explaining in Jerusalem.
- **"Cornelius was expecting them and had called together his relatives and close friends" (v24).** He had faith that something was about to happen, and he did not want to keep it to himself.
- **"Fell down at his feet and worshiped him" (v25).** The Roman officer bows to a Galilean fisherman. Peter refuses: **"Stand up; I too am a man"** (v26). Compare Paul and Barnabas at Lystra (14:14–15) and the angel in Revelation (Rev 22:8–9).
- **"You yourselves know how unlawful it is for a Jew to associate with or to visit anyone of another nation" (v28).** "Unlawful" here is not a command from the Old Testament itself but the strong custom built on it. Jewish writings of the time warned against eating with Gentiles or entering their homes, which were thought to be unclean. Jesus' accusers would not enter Pilate's headquarters for that reason (John 18:28). Peter enters a Roman officer's house in the governor's city.
- **"I came without objection" (v29).** Literally "without speaking against," a nice contrast with his "By no means, Lord" on the roof.
- **"Four days ago" (v30).** Counting inclusively: the vision, the messengers' journey to Joppa, the return journey and the arrival.
- **"We are all here in the presence of God to hear all that you have been commanded by the Lord" (v33).** Few preachers have had a better audience: ready, gathered and expecting God to speak.
- **Commentators:** JFB points out that after refusing Cornelius's worship, those who later claimed Peter's succession "have not imitated this part of his conduct," a Protestant jab at the papacy of his day. Guzik contrasts Peter's refusal on the roof with Cornelius's ready question to the angel, and says the only fitting answer to the Lord is yes. Both Guzik and the Tyndale notes read the vision as finally about people, not food. Matthew Henry: "God knows what services are before us, and how to prepare us," and on Cornelius gathering his friends, "We should not covet to eat our spiritual morsels alone."

#### Gentiles Hear the Good News (vv34–43)
- **"Truly I understand that God shows no partiality" (v34).** "Shows partiality" is from a Hebrew idiom, "to lift up the face," meaning to favor someone for their looks, status or nation. The Greek word (*prosōpolēmptēs*) appears only here. The truth itself is old (Deut 10:17; 2 Chron 19:7). Peter says "truly I understand" as if seeing it for the first time.
- **"In every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him" (v35).** This does not mean anyone sincere is saved without Christ. If so, Peter would not need to preach Jesus. It means nationality is no barrier: a Gentile who seeks God is welcome to come on the same terms as a Jew. Peter's sermon goes on to give those terms.
- **"Preaching good news of peace through Jesus Christ (he is Lord of all)" (v36).** Peace with God, and between Jew and Gentile (Eph 2:14–17). "Lord of all" was the kind of title Rome gave its emperor. In a Roman officer's house, Peter calls Jesus Lord of all, Jews and Gentiles alike.
- **The sermon's outline (vv37–43).** It follows the shape of Mark's Gospel closely: beginning in Galilee after John's baptism, Jesus' anointing, miracles and healings, his death in Jerusalem, his resurrection on the third day. Many early writers said Mark wrote down Peter's preaching, and this summary fits that tradition.
- **"How God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power" (v38).** At his baptism (Luke 3:22), fulfilling Isaiah 61:1, which Jesus read in Nazareth (Luke 4:18). "Christ" means "anointed one," and here Peter explains the title for a Gentile audience. **"He went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him."** A beautiful one-line summary of Jesus' ministry.
- **"They put him to death by hanging him on a tree" (v39).** The same phrase as 5:30, echoing the curse of Deuteronomy 21:23, which Christ bore for us (Gal 3:13).
- **"Not to all the people but to us who had been chosen by God as witnesses, who ate and drank with him" (v41).** The risen Jesus appeared to chosen witnesses, not to the crowds. Eating together proved he was not a ghost (Luke 24:41–43). Peter had eaten breakfast with him by the lake (John 21:12–15).
- **"The one appointed by God to be judge of the living and the dead" (v42).** Jesus is not only Savior but Judge of all nations, Romans included. Paul says the same in Athens (17:31).
- **"To him all the prophets bear witness that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name" (v43).** "Everyone," Jew or Gentile. "Believes" is the only condition named. Circumcision and the law are not mentioned.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Peter means God regards "personal character and state," not national lines, and that Peter was sent not to be "the instrument of his conversion" but to show God's way more fully. That differs from Guzik's view. Guzik insists that v35 does not mean such people are already saved, only that their nationality does not shut them out. JFB calls the sermon "all Gospel truth in brief." Matthew Henry: "wherever true religion is found, God will accept it without regarding names or sects."

#### The Holy Spirit Falls on the Gentiles (vv44–48)
- **"While Peter was still saying these things, the Holy Spirit fell on all who heard the word" (v44).** God interrupts the sermon. Peter later says, "as I began to speak" (11:15), so it felt to him as though he had barely started. No altar call, no laying on of hands, no baptism first. They believed as they heard, and God acted.
- **"The believers from among the circumcised… were amazed" (v45).** The six Jewish believers from Joppa are stunned. **"Even on the Gentiles."** "Even" says everything about how unthinkable this was.
- **"Speaking in tongues and extolling God" (v46).** The same sign as Pentecost (2:4, 11). Peter later says, "the Holy Spirit fell on them just as on us at the beginning" (11:15). The Gentile Pentecost proves to the Jewish witnesses that God has accepted Gentiles fully, not as second-class members.
- **"Can anyone withhold water for baptizing these people, who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?" (v47).** "Withhold" is the same word the eunuch used (8:36, "prevents"). If God has given the Spirit, who can refuse the sign? The order here, Spirit then baptism, is the reverse of 2:38 and shows that God is not bound to one sequence.
- **Readings on order.** Those who see baptism as the moment forgiveness is given see this as an exception God made to convince Jewish believers. Those who see baptism as the sign of a salvation already received see this as a clear case of faith and the Spirit coming before baptism. All agree the Gentiles were baptized at once, and that the Spirit's coming settled the question of their acceptance.
- **"He commanded them to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ" (v48).** Peter commands it, but probably did not do it himself (compare 1 Cor 1:14–17). Gentiles are baptized without being circumcised, which will lead to the great debate of chapter 15.
- **"Then they asked him to remain for some days" (v48).** And he did, which meant eating with Gentiles in their home. Jerusalem believers will complain about exactly this (11:3).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "The Holy Ghost fell upon those who were neither circumcised nor baptized," and on baptism, "can we deny the sign to those who have received the things signified?" Guzik says they were saved as they believed during the sermon and that baptism recognized what God had already done. JFB says their "living discipleship" made baptism fitting, and imagines the days that followed as "golden days" of teaching. JFB also notes that Peter likely did not baptize them himself, as Paul seldom did.

#### Pointing to Christ
God takes apart the oldest wall in the Bible story, between Israel and the nations, and he does it with a sheet of animals, a Roman officer's prayers and a fisherman who learns to say yes. The center of it all is Jesus, "Lord of all," anointed with the Spirit, crucified on a tree, raised and appointed Judge, the one to whom all the prophets bear witness. In him "everyone who believes" receives forgiveness. God's promise to Abraham that all nations would be blessed in his offspring (3:25) comes true in a centurion's living room, as the same Spirit who came on Jewish believers at Pentecost now comes "even on the Gentiles."
