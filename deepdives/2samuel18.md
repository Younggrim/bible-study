*Setting:* Gilead, east of the Jordan, around 976 BC, some days or weeks after David fled Jerusalem. Thanks to Hushai's advice, Absalom took time to gather a national army, which gave David time to cross the Jordan, reach the walled town of Mahanaim and be fed and supplied by loyal friends (17:24–29). Now Absalom has crossed the river with "all Israel," commanded by his cousin Amasa (17:25–26). David's force is smaller but full of hardened veterans. The battle that follows decides the kingdom. But the chapter's real weight falls on one question David asks twice, and on the cry he gives when he hears the answer. In the Hebrew Bible, 18:33 is numbered 19:1.

#### Absalom Killed (vv1–18)
- **"David mustered the men who were with him and set over them commanders of thousands and commanders of hundreds" (v1).** The old soldier knows what to do. He organizes his army and divides it into three, a common battle formation (Judg 7:16; 1 Sam 11:11), under **Joab, Abishai and Ittai the Gittite** (v2). The foreigner who swore to stay with David "whether for death or for life" (15:21) is now one of his three generals.
- **"You shall not go out… you are worth ten thousand of us" (v3).** The men refuse to let David fight. Ahithophel had planned to kill only the king (17:2). If David falls, the war is lost. "Send us help from the city" means keep reserves ready at Mahanaim. The Tyndale notes suggest age may also have been a factor: years later David nearly dies in battle and his men again keep him back (21:15–17).
- **"Whatever seems best to you I will do" (v4).** The king accepts advice. This time, unlike 11:1, staying behind is the right thing.
- **"Deal gently for my sake with the young man Absalom" (v5).** "Young man" is the word David keeps using for Absalom, as if he were still a boy. The order is given publicly: "all the people heard." Absalom wants his father dead. David wants his son alive.
- **"The battle was fought in the forest of Ephraim" (v6).** Although Ephraim's tribal land was west of the Jordan, this forest was in Gilead, east of the river. Some think it was named after Ephraimites who had settled there or were defeated there (Judg 12:1–6).
- **"Twenty thousand men" (v7).** Absalom's army is crushed. **"The forest devoured more people that day than the sword" (v8).** The rough, wooded hill country, with thick oak and terebinth, thickets, ravines and pits, broke up Absalom's larger army and made it easier for David's experienced men to fight in small groups. Fleeing men were trapped and killed. The forest itself seems to fight for David.
- **"Absalom was riding on his mule" (v9).** Mules were royal mounts (13:29; 1 Kings 1:33). He has run into David's men and is fleeing.
- **"His head caught fast in the oak, and he was suspended between heaven and earth, while the mule that was under him went on" (v9).** The text says his *head* caught, not specifically his hair, though readers have always connected it with his famous hair (14:26). The picture is vivid and full of meaning. His royal mount, the sign of his kingship, rides away from under him. He hangs "between heaven and earth," rejected by both. Deuteronomy 21:23 says that one hanged on a tree is "cursed by God."
- **"Why then did you not strike him there to the ground?" (v11).** Joab offers a reward: "ten pieces of silver and a belt," perhaps a soldier's belt of honor (compare 1 Sam 18:4).
- **"Even if I felt in my hand the weight of a thousand pieces of silver, I would not reach out my hand against the king's son" (v12).** The ordinary soldier obeys the king when the general will not. He also knows Joab: "if I had dealt treacherously… you yourself would have stood aloof" (v13). Joab would have let him take the blame.
- **"He took three javelins in his hand and thrust them into the heart of Absalom while he was still alive" (v14).** "Javelins" is literally "sticks" or "rods" (*shebaṭim*). "Heart" can mean the middle of the body. Then **"ten young men, Joab's armor-bearers… struck him and killed him" (v15).** Joab disobeys David's direct command, as he did when he killed Abner (3:26–27). He probably believed it was the only way to end the rebellion and keep the kingdom safe, and he may also have feared what Absalom's survival meant for him. David will not forget it (1 Kings 2:5).
- **"Joab blew the trumpet, and the troops came back from pursuing Israel" (v16).** With the rebel dead, Joab stops the killing. Those who followed Absalom are still Israelites, and David will need to rule them again.
- **"They took Absalom and threw him into a great pit in the forest and raised over him a very great heap of stones" (v17).** A heap of stones over a body was a mark of shame. It was done for Achan (Josh 7:26) and the king of Ai (Josh 8:29). It also echoes the law for a stubborn, rebellious son, who was to be stoned (Deut 21:18–21).
- **"Absalom in his lifetime had taken and set up for himself the pillar that is in the King's Valley" (v18).** He built his own memorial, because "I have no son to keep my name in remembrance." His three sons (14:27) must have died. The King's Valley was near Jerusalem (Gen 14:17). The monument in the Kidron valley known today as "Absalom's Tomb" is from much later, around the time of Jesus, so it is not this pillar. The contrast is sharp: he built a pillar for his glory and ended in a pit under a heap of stones.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says Absalom was "at his wit's end first, and then at his life's end," hung up as a warning to rebellious children. He notes the soldier's sound reasoning, "Those that love the treason hate the traitor," and admits, "I know not whether Joab can be justified in this direct disobedience." JFB defends Joab more firmly: he acted from "a sincere regard to the interests of David," and Absalom "deserved to die by the divine law." Guzik agrees that Absalom got what he deserved, and that Joab knew David would never punish his son. He also points out, with Adam Clarke, that the text says his head caught, not his hair. He passes on Trapp's observation that ten men killed the man who had violated ten concubines. The Tyndale notes add that Joab may have let his armor-bearers finish the job so he could say he did not strike the fatal blow, and that Absalom had replaced Joab with Amasa, giving Joab another motive.

#### David Hears of Absalom's Death (vv19–30)
- **"Let me run and carry news to the king that the LORD has delivered him" (v19).** Ahimaaz, Zadok's son, had already risked his life carrying news to David (17:17–21). He is eager to bring the news of victory.
- **"Today you shall carry no news, because the king's son is dead" (v20).** Joab knows David. Messengers who brought bad news to David had not fared well (1:15; 4:10–12). Joab may be protecting the priest's son.
- **"Go, tell the king what you have seen" (v21).** He sends instead "the Cushite," a man from Cush, the land south of Egypt (modern Sudan). He was probably a foreign servant or soldier. The Tyndale notes suggest Joab thought it safer to risk a foreigner. The Cushite bows and runs.
- **"Come what may, let me also run" (vv22–23).** Ahimaaz will not give up. Joab warns him he will get no reward, then lets him go. **"Ahimaaz ran by the way of the plain, and outran the Cushite."** "The plain" is *kikkar*, the word for the Jordan valley. He took a longer but flatter route and got there first.
- **"David was sitting between the two gates" (v24).** City gates were often built with an outer and inner gate and a space between. The king waits there for news, as old Eli once sat waiting by the road for news of the ark (1 Sam 4:13).
- **"If he is alone, there is news in his mouth" (v25).** A single runner meant a messenger. A crowd of runners would mean a rout. When the watchman says the first is Ahimaaz, David says, **"He is a good man and comes with good news" (v27).**
- **"All is well" (v28).** One word in Hebrew: *shalom*. Ahimaaz bows and blesses the LORD for the victory.
- **"Is it well with the young man Absalom?" (v29).** Literally "Is there *shalom* for the young man Absalom?" Absalom's name means "father of peace" (*Avshalom*). David has one concern. Victory means nothing to him until he knows if his son is safe.
- **"I saw a great commotion, but I do not know what it was" (v29).** Ahimaaz loses his nerve, or wants to spare the king. He knew (v20). He evades. David tells him to stand aside.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the reasons Joab first refused Ahimaaz "are of no importance," but the scene's urgency is vividly told. Guzik says Ahimaaz was the better runner but the worse messenger, because he arrived without a clear message. Henry quotes Bishop Hall on David's order in v5: "Even in the holiest parents, nature may be guilty of an injurious tenderness." The Tyndale notes observe that David's question put his son ahead of the men who had fought for him, and they highlight the wordplay between *shalom* and Absalom's name.

#### David's Grief (vv31–33)
- **"Good news for my lord the king! For the LORD has delivered you this day" (v31).** The Cushite announces the victory as a gift from God. In a sense he is right. God had promised David's throne would stand (7:16).
- **"May the enemies of my lord the king and all who rise up against you for evil be like that young man" (v32).** A careful, polite way of saying Absalom is dead. To the Cushite, as to most of the army, Absalom was an enemy. To David, he is a son.
- **"The king was deeply moved and went up to the chamber over the gate and wept" (v33).** "Deeply moved" means shaken, trembling all over. He goes up to the room above the gate to be alone.
- **"O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son!" (v33).** "My son" five times. It is one of the most heartbreaking cries in the Bible. David grieves a son who wanted him dead. He may also grieve his own part in it: the sin with Bathsheba that brought "the sword" on his house (12:10), his failure to deal with Amnon, his half-reconciliation with Absalom. And he grieves that Absalom died a rebel. **"Would I had died instead of you"** is love that would trade places, but cannot.
- **Commentators:** JFB says that in this cry "all thoughts of the victory he had won as a king were completely sunk in the painful loss he had sustained as a father." Guzik counts the five "my son"s and sees David's own sins reflected back at him in his son. He sees in David's wish to die in Absalom's place a picture of what God has actually done in Christ. Matthew Henry wishes he could think the cry came from concern for Absalom's eternal state, but judges that "it rather seems to have been spoken inconsiderately, and in a passion, and it was his infirmity." Joab will tell David much the same, more bluntly, in the next chapter (19:5–7).

#### Pointing to Christ
Absalom hung on a tree "between heaven and earth," under the curse of Deuteronomy 21:23, and died for his own rebellion. Paul quotes that same law of the Son of David: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us, for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'" (Gal 3:13). Absalom was buried under stones in shame. Jesus was laid in a rich man's tomb and rose again. David cried, "Would I had died instead of you," and could not. God the Father gave his Son to die instead of rebels, "while we were enemies" (Rom 5:10). The love David felt for his rebellious son, God has turned into a rescue, and the Son who died in our place brings us home as sons and daughters.
