*Setting:* Jerusalem, 588–586 BC, with a closing scene in Babylon in 561 BC. Ten years earlier Nebuchadnezzar took King Jehoiachin, the leading citizens and the craftsmen to Babylon and put Jehoiachin's uncle Zedekiah on the throne (24:10–17). Zedekiah swore loyalty in the LORD's name, then broke his oath and rebelled, trusting Egypt (24:20; Ezek 17:15). Jeremiah told him again and again to surrender and live (Jer 38:17–18). He would not. This is the last chapter of Kings, and it tells the end of everything the book has followed: the city, the temple, the line of kings and Israel's life in the land. Jeremiah 39 and 52 tell the same story, almost word for word in places, and Lamentations is the poetry of grief written over the ruins.

#### Fall and Captivity of Judah (vv1–21)
- **"In the ninth year of his reign, in the tenth month, on the tenth day of the month" (v1).** January 588 BC. Kings rarely gives exact days, but in this chapter it gives them over and over, as if each date were burned into memory. They were. After the exile the Jews kept fasts on the anniversaries: the tenth month for the siege's start, the fourth month for the wall breached, the fifth for the temple burned, the seventh for Gedaliah's murder (v25). Zechariah names exactly these four fasts (Zech 8:19). Jewish communities still keep the fast of the fifth month, Tisha B'Av, the ninth of Av, as a day of mourning.
- **"They built siegeworks all around it" (v1).** A siege wall and towers ringed the city, so no one could get out and no food could get in. Ezekiel, in Babylon, was told to act out this very siege on a clay brick on the day it began (Ezek 4:1–3; 24:1–2). Ezekiel 24:1–2 records the same date.
- **"So the city was besieged till the eleventh year" (v2).** About eighteen months. There was a break when an Egyptian army marched up and the Babylonians withdrew for a time (Jer 37:5–11). The people thought they were saved. Jeremiah told them the Babylonians would be back. They came back.
- **"The famine was so severe in the city that there was no food" (v3).** Lamentations describes the horror in detail: children begging for bread, and mothers so starved they cooked their own children (Lam 4:4, 10). This was one of the curses Moses had warned would come if Israel broke the covenant (Deut 28:52–57). JFB notes the same link to the curses on apostasy.
- **"A breach was made in the city" (v4).** July 586 BC, on the ninth day of the fourth month. Some scholars date the fall to 587 BC, depending on how the regnal years are counted.
- **"By the way of the gate between the two walls, by the king's garden… in the direction of the Arabah" (v4).** The escape route ran out the southeast corner of the city near the Pool of Siloam, as JFB says, down the Kidron Valley and east toward the Jordan Valley (the Arabah). Ezekiel had acted this out too, digging through a wall and leaving at night with his baggage (Ezek 12:3–12).
- **"Overtook him in the plains of Jericho, and all his army was scattered" (v5).** Guzik points out the irony: Jericho was where Israel first entered the land under Joshua (Josh 6). The last king of Judah is caught on the same ground where the conquest began. The story of Israel in the land ends where it started.
- **"Brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, and they passed sentence on him" (v6).** Riblah in Syria was Nebuchadnezzar's field headquarters, the same place where Pharaoh Neco had put Jehoahaz in chains (23:33). "Passed sentence" is literally "spoke judgment with him." Zedekiah was tried as a vassal who broke his oath. Chronicles stresses that the oath had been sworn by God (2 Chr 36:13).
- **"They slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes, and put out the eyes of Zedekiah" (v7).** This was a common punishment for rebel kings, and the Tyndale notes observe how deliberately cruel it was: the last thing he saw was his sons killed. Two prophecies that seemed to contradict each other both came true. Jeremiah said Zedekiah would "see the king of Babylon eye to eye" (Jer 32:4; 34:3). Ezekiel said he would be brought to Babylon, "yet he shall not see it, and he shall die there" (Ezek 12:13). He saw Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah, was blinded, and never saw Babylon. JFB makes the same point from Ezekiel.
- **"In the fifth month, on the seventh day… Nebuzaradan, the captain of the bodyguard" (v8).** About a month after the wall fell, Nebuchadnezzar sent his senior officer to finish the work. Nebuzaradan's name appears on a Babylonian list of Nebuchadnezzar's officials, the Tyndale notes say. His title, *rab-tabbachim*, is the same one Potiphar held in Egypt (Gen 37:36). The word *tabbach* can mean "cook" or "butcher," and by extension a royal guard or executioner. Jeremiah 52:12 gives the date as the tenth day. JFB suggests one date is when he arrived and the other when he set the fire.
- **"He burned the house of the LORD" (v9).** Kings says it in one plain line. Solomon's temple, about 375 years old, the place God said he would put his name (1 Kings 9:3), is gone. Henry: "Solomon's temple is burnt to the ground." Ezekiel had seen the glory of the LORD leave the temple before this happened, rising from the threshold and departing east over the Mount of Olives (Ezek 10:18–19; 11:22–23). The building that burned was already empty.
- **"Broke down the walls around Jerusalem" (v10).** The walls would lie in ruins for almost 140 years, until Nehemiah rebuilt them (Neh 1:3; 6:15).
- **"The deserters who had deserted to the king of Babylon" (v11).** Jeremiah had urged people to surrender to save their lives (Jer 21:9; 38:2). Some did. They went into exile with the rest, but alive.
- **"Some of the poorest of the land to be vinedressers and plowmen" (v12).** Babylon needed someone to keep the land producing. Jeremiah adds that Nebuzaradan gave these poor people vineyards and fields (Jer 39:10). The landless ended up with the land.
- **The bronze (vv13–17).** Solomon's great bronze work, made by Hiram of Tyre (1 Kings 7:13–47), is broken up and carted away as scrap. The two pillars had names, Jachin ("he establishes") and Boaz ("in him is strength") (1 Kings 7:21). The "sea" was an enormous basin for the priests' washing. The narrator lingers over these pieces, giving their height, the capitals and the pomegranates, the way a family lists what was lost in a fire. "Beyond weight" (v16) is what was said when Solomon made them (1 Kings 7:47). What Solomon could not weigh, Babylon hauls away. The ESV footnote gives a cubit as about 18 inches, so each pillar stood about 27 feet high. The Tyndale notes say 1 Kings 7:16 and Jeremiah 52:22 give the capital as five cubits; the "three" here may measure only the lattice part. Guzik notes, citing an older writer, that verse after verse begins with "and," like a clock counting down.
- **No mention of the ark.** The ark of the covenant is not in any list of things taken or destroyed. Scripture does not say what happened to it. A later Jewish tradition says Jeremiah hid it (2 Maccabees 2:4–8), but that is legend, not Scripture. Jeremiah himself had said a day would come when no one would miss it or make another (Jer 3:16).
- **"Seraiah the chief priest and Zephaniah the second priest" (v18).** Seraiah was a grandson of Hilkiah, who found the book of the law under Josiah (1 Chr 6:13–14). He is executed, but his son Jehozadak is taken into exile (1 Chr 6:15), and Jehozadak's son Joshua becomes high priest after the return (Hag 1:1). Ezra, the great scribe, was also a descendant of this Seraiah (Ezra 7:1). JFB calls him Ezra's grandfather, which the dates do not allow, since Ezra came to Jerusalem 128 years later. Zephaniah the second priest had carried messages between Zedekiah and Jeremiah (Jer 21:1; 37:3). The "keepers of the threshold" were, in JFB's words, "not mere porters, but officers of high trust."
- **"Put them to death at Riblah" (vv19–21).** In 597 BC the leaders had been deported. Now they are executed. "The people of the land" were the landowning men of Judah who had made and unmade kings (21:24; 23:30). Sixty of them die here.
- **"So Judah was taken into exile out of its land" (v21).** Short and final, and a deliberate echo of the verdict on the northern kingdom: "So Israel was exiled from their own land" (17:23). Moses had warned that if Israel turned to other gods, "you shall be plucked off the land" (Deut 28:63–64). God did what he said he would do.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes Jerusalem as both the royal city and the holy city, and says both glories departed here, writing "Ichabod—Where is the glory?" over it. Guzik reads Zedekiah's capture near Jericho as a grim bookend to the conquest, and notes that the exile ended about 860 years in the land, lost through idolatry. JFB explains the escape route by the Pool of Siloam and sees Ezekiel 12:13 fulfilled in a king who came to Babylon but "did not see" it. JFB skips the bronze furnishings (vv13–17) altogether.

#### Gedaliah Made Governor of Judah (vv22–26)
- **"Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, son of Shaphan" (v22).** This family appears all through Josiah's reform and Jeremiah's ministry. Shaphan read the found book to Josiah (22:10). Ahikam saved Jeremiah's life from a mob (Jer 26:24). Now Ahikam's son is made governor, and Nebuchadnezzar puts Jeremiah in his care (Jer 39:14; 40:5–6). A clay seal impression found at Lachish reads "belonging to Gedaliah, who is over the house," probably the same man before the fall.
- **"At Mizpah" (v23).** Jerusalem was rubble, so the government moved to Mizpah, a few miles north in Benjamin. It was an old place of national gathering, where Samuel called Israel to repent (1 Sam 7:5–6).
- **The captains (v23).** These were Judean army officers who had scattered into the countryside during the siege. Jeremiah 40–41 tells the longer story. A seal with the name "Jaazaniah, servant of the king" was found at Tell en-Nasbeh, which many think is Mizpah, as the Tyndale notes mention.
- **"Do not be afraid… serve the king of Babylon, and it shall be well with you" (v24).** Gedaliah repeats Jeremiah's message almost word for word (Jer 27:11; 29:7). Babylon was the rod of God's judgment, and the way to live was to accept it, not fight it. Guzik makes the same point: resisting Babylon now meant resisting God's discipline.
- **"Ishmael… of the royal family, came with ten men and struck down Gedaliah" (v25).** Johanan had warned Gedaliah that Ishmael was sent by the king of Ammon to kill him, but Gedaliah refused to believe it (Jer 40:13–16). Ishmael killed him at a meal (Jer 41:1–2). He was of David's family and may have resented a non-royal governor serving Babylon. JFB suggests envy, or a bribe from Baalis king of Ammon. The fast of the seventh month, still kept by Jews as the Fast of Gedaliah, remembers this day.
- **"All the people… went to Egypt, for they were afraid of the Chaldeans" (v26).** They asked Jeremiah to pray for guidance and promised to obey whatever God said. God told them to stay in the land, and they went to Egypt anyway, dragging Jeremiah with them (Jer 42–43). Kings tells it in one verse, and the irony is crushing. Moses had warned that if Israel broke the covenant, God would bring them back to Egypt (Deut 28:68). The people the LORD brought out of Egypt walk back into it by their own choice.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the remnant under Gedaliah as scattered and broken. Guzik calls Gedaliah a godly friend of Jeremiah and says Egypt proved a worse choice than submission to Babylon. JFB notes that they went "in spite of Jeremiah's dissuasions."

#### Jehoiachin Released from Prison (vv27–30)
- **"In the thirty-seventh year of the exile of Jehoiachin" (v27).** That is 561 BC, twenty-five years after the temple burned. Jehoiachin is about fifty-five, after thirty-seven years as a prisoner. Nebuchadnezzar has just died after a reign of forty-three years.
- **"Evil-merodach king of Babylon" (v27).** His Babylonian name was *Amel-Marduk*, "man of Marduk." He reigned only two years before he was murdered. New kings sometimes released prisoners at the start of their reign as a show of goodwill.
- **"Graciously freed" (v27).** The ESV footnote gives the Hebrew idiom: he "lifted up the head of" Jehoiachin. It is the same phrase used when Pharaoh restored his cupbearer in Joseph's story (Gen 40:13, 20). There, too, a man in a foreign prison was raised up by a king.
- **"Spoke kindly to him and gave him a seat above the seats of the kings who were with him" (v28).** Babylon held several captive kings. David's heir is given the place of honor among them. Babylonian ration tablets found in the ruins of Nebuchadnezzar's palace list oil supplied to "Yaukin, king of the land of Judah" and his five sons, so the text's picture of a royal prisoner with a household is confirmed.
- **"Jehoiachin put off his prison garments… dined regularly at the king's table" (v29).** Compare Mephibosheth, Saul's grandson, who ate at David's table "like one of the king's sons" (2 Sam 9:11–13). The roles are turned around: now David's heir eats at a pagan king's table, by grace.
- **"As long as he lived" (v30).** That is where the book of Kings ends. There is no word of return, no new temple, no restored throne. But David's line is still alive, and a foreign king has lifted its head. The Tyndale notes suggest the scene was meant as a quiet hope that God would restore a repentant people, as Solomon had prayed (1 Kings 8:46–53) and Jeremiah promised after seventy years (Jer 29:10–14). Jeremiah 52 ends with the very same paragraph.
- **Commentators:** Henry speaks of "the countenance which, after thirty-seven years' imprisonment, was given to Jehoiachin." JFB sees God's providence toward the house of David in it, and says Jehoiachin had "a seat at the royal table." Guzik reads the ending as a deliberate note of grace: the land is empty and the people are in exile, but God is not finished with David's house.

#### Pointing to Christ
Kings ends in ashes, with the temple burned, the walls down, the king blinded, the people scattered and a remnant walking back to Egypt. The one light left is a son of David let out of prison to eat at a king's table. It is a small light, but it is a real one, because God had sworn that David's line would not fail (2 Sam 7:16). Jehoiachin's name appears in the genealogy that ends with "Jesus… who is called Christ" (Matt 1:12, 16). The true Son of David did more than eat at a king's table by favor. He became the temple that was destroyed and raised in three days (John 2:19–21), and he spreads his own table for exiles, people raised up from prison and given new clothes, who will eat in his kingdom forever (Luke 22:29–30).
