*Setting:* The plains of Moab, about forty years after the Exodus, in the last weeks of Moses' life, in the law section of his second sermon (chapters 12–26). Chapters 22–23 dealt with marriage and sexual purity and then with a string of laws about neighbors, debts and vows. Chapter 24 carries on in the same way. It begins with a divorce law that Jesus himself later discussed, and then turns almost entirely to people who are easy to push around: a new bride, a borrower, a kidnapping victim, a day laborer, the sojourner, the fatherless and the widow. Twice Moses gives the reason: "remember that you were a slave in Egypt" (vv18, 22). The chapter shows what it means for a people redeemed by God to treat the weak the way God treated them.

#### Laws Concerning Divorce (vv1–4)
- **What this law does and does not say.** The long "if… and if… and if" of vv1–3 sets up a case, and the command comes only in v4. Moses does not command divorce or set up the grounds for it. He assumes divorce already happened and then forbids one thing: a man who divorced his wife may not remarry her after she has married someone else. Jesus explained that Moses "allowed" divorce "because of your hardness of heart, but from the beginning it was not so" (Matt 19:8).
- **"Because he has found some indecency in her" (v1).** The Hebrew is *'erwat davar*, literally "nakedness of a thing," the same phrase as the "indecent" thing in the camp in 23:14. It points to something shameful, probably sexual, but not adultery itself, which carried the death penalty (22:22). In Jesus' day this phrase was fought over. The school of Shammai read it narrowly as sexual immorality. The school of Hillel read it so broadly that a man could divorce his wife for spoiling a meal. The Pharisees' question to Jesus, "Is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause?" (Matt 19:3), was a question about this verse. Jesus answered by going back behind Moses to creation: "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate" (Matt 19:6).
- **"He writes her a certificate of divorce and puts it in her hand" (v1).** The word is *sefer keritut*, a "document of cutting off," from the verb for cutting. The written certificate protected the woman. It proved she was free to remarry and could not be accused of adultery. It also made divorce a formal act, not something shouted in anger, and in a culture where few could write it often meant going to a scribe, which gave time to think again.
- **"And if she goes and becomes another man's wife" (v2).** The law assumes she might remarry, and does not condemn her for it.
- **"Her former husband… may not take her again to be his wife, after she has been defiled" (v4).** Why? Readers have offered several reasons. It stopped men from treating wives like property to be traded back and forth. It made a man think hard before divorcing, since there was no going back. It may have protected the second marriage from the first husband's interference. "Defiled" is a strong word. Some think it means that in God's eyes, the second marriage, made possible by a divorce God did not approve, was itself a kind of adultery, which fits Jesus' teaching in Matthew 5:32. **"An abomination before the LORD"** and **"sin upon the land"** show how seriously God took marriage.
- **God's own marriage.** Jeremiah quotes this law in God's name: "If a man divorces his wife and she goes from him and becomes another man's wife, will he return to her?… You have played the whore with many lovers; and would you return to me?" (Jer 3:1). By this law Israel could never go back. Then God says, "Return, faithless Israel… for I am merciful" (Jer 3:12). God does what the law would not allow, and takes back his unfaithful bride. Hosea's marriage acted out the same grace (Hos 3:1).
- **Commentators:** Guzik stresses that the law regulates divorce and does not encourage it, and that Jesus read the "indecency" narrowly. He adds that Paul names desertion by an unbelieving spouse as another situation (1 Cor 7:15). JFB says Moses tolerated a practice probably learned in Egypt, and limited it by requiring a written bill, which gave time for reflection, and by forbidding remarriage to the first husband. Henry writes that God's grace can carry a believer through an unhappy marriage: "Divine grace will sanctify this cross, support under it, and teach so to behave." The Tyndale notes call divorce a concession, not a desirable option, pointing to Malachi 2:16.

#### Miscellaneous Laws (vv5–22)
- **"He shall be free at home one year to be happy with his wife whom he has taken" (v5).** A new husband was exempt from war and other public service for a full year. Chapter 20:7 sent home the man who was engaged. This goes further. The Hebrew can be read "to make his wife happy," and the old KJV has "shall cheer up his wife." Either way, a strong marriage was worth more to the nation than one more soldier. Henry: "It is of great consequence that love be kept up between husband and wife."
- **"No one shall take a mill or an upper millstone in pledge" (v6).** Grain was ground by hand every day on a flat lower stone with a smaller upper stone, the "rider," pushed back and forth on top. Taking the upper stone as security for a loan was like taking the family's daily bread. **"That would be taking a life in pledge."** A lender could ask for security, but not for the very means of staying alive. JFB says the law was "founded on the custom of grinding corn every morning for daily consumption."
- **"If a man is found stealing one of his brothers… and sells him, then that thief shall die" (v7).** Kidnapping to sell someone as a slave was a capital crime (Exod 21:16). Joseph's brothers did exactly this to him (Gen 37:28). Paul lists "enslavers," literally "man-stealers," among the lawless (1 Tim 1:10). Henry notes that this theft "could not be settled, as other thefts, by restitution." Later Christian opponents of the Atlantic slave trade, which was built on kidnapping, appealed to this law.
- **"In a case of leprous disease, to be very careful to do according to all that the Levitical priests shall direct you" (v8).** The Hebrew word covers many skin diseases, not only what is today called leprosy, and the priests followed the detailed rules of Leviticus 13–14. **"Remember what the LORD your God did to Miriam"** (v9). Moses' own sister spoke against him and was struck with a skin disease. She was shut outside the camp for seven days, and the whole nation waited for her (Num 12:10–15). If God's law applied to Miriam, it applied to everyone. Her example also warns against speaking against God's chosen servant.
- **"You shall not go into his house to collect his pledge. You shall stand outside" (vv10–11).** A borrower's home was his own. The lender had to wait at the door and let the borrower choose what to bring out. The Tyndale notes say this spared the debtor's dignity.
- **"If he is a poor man, you shall not sleep in his pledge" (v12).** A very poor man might have only his cloak to offer, the outer garment he slept in at night. **"Restore to him the pledge as the sun sets, that he may sleep in his cloak and bless you"** (v13). Exodus 22:26–27 puts God's voice behind the poor man: "if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate." Amos condemned the rich who lay down beside every altar "on garments taken in pledge" (Amos 2:8). **"It shall be righteousness for you before the LORD."** *Tsedaqah*, "righteousness," is the same word used of Abraham's faith "counted… to him as righteousness" (Gen 15:6). God notices a cloak returned at sunset.
- **"You shall not oppress a hired worker who is poor and needy" (v14).** Day laborers had no land and lived from one day's pay to the next. **"Whether he is one of your brothers or one of the sojourners"**: the foreign worker had the same right. **"You shall give him his wages on the same day, before the sun sets"** (v15). Leviticus 19:13 says the same. In Jesus' parable of the vineyard the owner pays everyone "when evening came" (Matt 20:8). **"Lest he cry against you to the LORD."** James 5:4 picks up this exact picture: "the wages of the laborers… which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts."
- **"Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children… Each one shall be put to death for his own sin" (v16).** Human courts were to punish only the guilty person. In other ancient law codes, a man's son or daughter could be put to death for his crime. In the Code of Hammurabi, if a builder's house collapsed and killed the owner's son, the builder's son was to be killed. Israel's law refused that. When King Amaziah executed his father's murderers, he spared their children, and 2 Kings 14:6 quotes this verse as the reason. Ezekiel 18:20 builds a whole sermon on it: "The soul who sins shall die." This does not contradict God's warning that he visits "the iniquity of the fathers on the children" (5:9), which speaks of God's own dealings with generations that go on hating him, not of what a human court may do.
- **"You shall not pervert the justice due to the sojourner or to the fatherless, or take a widow's garment in pledge" (v17).** These three had no one to stand up for them in court. Unlike a poor man (vv12–13), a widow's garment could not be taken at all, even for a day.
- **"But you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt and the LORD your God redeemed you" (v18).** This is the motive. Israel knew what it was to have no rights and no defender, and then to be rescued. They were to treat the helpless the way God treated them.
- **"Forget a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it" (v19).** Three harvests are named: grain, olives and grapes. Olives were knocked from the branches with sticks (**"when you beat your olive trees,"** v20), and the farmer was not to go back over the tree. Grapes were not to be picked clean (v21). Leviticus 19:9–10 adds leaving the corners of the field. What was left was **"for the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow."** This let the poor work for their own food, with dignity. The book of Ruth shows this law at work: a Moabite widow gleaning in Boaz's field, and Boaz telling his men to drop extra stalks for her on purpose (Ruth 2:2–3, 15–16). **"That the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands."** What looks like loss is the path to blessing.
- **Commentators:** Henry on vv14–22: these duties are easy to agree with, but "the difficulty is to attend to them in our daily walk and conversation." He also says the pledge laws teach us "to consult the comfort and subsistence of others, as much as our own advantage." JFB adds only a cross-reference on vv7 and 8–9, says withholding wages overnight could leave a poor family hungry, and that the gleanings let "the hearts of the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow" be gladdened. Guzik reads v5 as teaching that a husband finds his own happiness by giving himself to his wife's, ties v16 to Ezekiel 18, and points to Boaz and Ruth as this chapter's law in action. He notes that Achan's family died with him (Josh 7:24–25), and suggests they likely shared in his sin.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus took up this chapter's divorce law and pointed past it to God's design at creation, and then lived out God's faithfulness to an unfaithful bride. Jeremiah's question, "would you return to me?", gets its answer at the cross, where Christ "loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her" (Eph 5:25–26). He is also the one who sees the poor man's cloak and the widow's need. He told of a laborer's wages paid at sundown, and he paid the wages of sin that were owed by others (Rom 6:23). And the Moabite widow who gleaned in Boaz's field became the great-grandmother of David, in the line of the Redeemer himself (Matt 1:5). The law that said "leave something for the stranger" led, in God's providence, to the Savior of strangers.
