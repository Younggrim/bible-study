*Setting:* Hosea preached to the northern kingdom of Israel, sometimes called Ephraim or Samaria, from around 755 BC until shortly before its fall to Assyria in 722 BC. He was a younger contemporary of Amos, and the only writing prophet who was himself from the north. He began in the last years of Jeroboam II, a time of wealth and military success. After Jeroboam's death the kingdom fell apart: six kings in about twenty-five years, four of them murdered, and the last carried off by Assyria. Where Amos thundered about injustice, Hosea speaks of a broken marriage. God is the faithful husband, Israel the wife who has run after other lovers, especially Baal, the Canaanite storm god credited with rain and harvests. Chapters 1–3 tell that story through Hosea's own family, and the rest of the book (chapters 4–14) applies it in sermons. Chapter 1 gives the marriage and the three children whose names are messages from God, and ends, unexpectedly, with a promise that reverses every name.

#### Hosea's Wife and Children (vv1–11)
- **"The word of the LORD that came to Hosea, the son of Beeri" (v1).** Hosea's name (*Hoshea*) means "salvation" or "the LORD saves." It is the original name of Joshua (Num 13:16) and the name of Israel's last king (2 Kings 17:1), and it shares its root with the name Jesus. Nothing else is known of his father Beeri.
- **"In the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and… Jeroboam" (v1).** These reigns span roughly 790–686 BC. The list is uneven: four kings of Judah, but only one of Israel, though Hosea lived through six more northern kings after Jeroboam. Most scholars think the book was collected and finished in Judah after Samaria fell, so it is dated by Judah's kings. Leaving the later northern kings out may also be a quiet judgment: after Jeroboam they were usurpers, kings God had not chosen (8:4).
- **"When the LORD first spoke through Hosea" (v2).** Literally "the beginning of the LORD's speaking through Hosea." His whole ministry began with this costly command.
- **"Go, take to yourself a wife of whoredom" (v2).** This is one of the hardest commands in Scripture, and Christians have read it in several ways. (1) *It was a vision or a parable*, not a real marriage. Many early and medieval interpreters held this, and so does JFB, which reads it as acted "internally and in vision." Matthew Henry reports that "most commentators think that it was done in vision, or that it is no more than a parable." (2) *It was a real marriage to a known prostitute*, perhaps a woman involved in the sexual rites of Baal worship. (3) *It was a real marriage to a woman who later proved unfaithful*. "A wife of whoredom" would then describe what she became, seen from the end of the story. The Tyndale notes say most interpreters today think the marriage really happened, and the plain, specific detail (her name, her father, the weaning of a child) reads like history. Chapter 3, where Hosea buys her back, makes most sense if it is real. The text does not say exactly what Gomer was when Hosea married her.
- **"Children of whoredom" (v2).** This may mean children born of her unfaithfulness, or children who would grow up in a home marked by it, or simply a people, "children" of an unfaithful nation. Verse 3 says she "bore *him*" the first son; for the next two it says only "she conceived," which some read as a hint that they were not Hosea's. That is possible but not stated.
- **"For the land commits great whoredom by forsaking the LORD" (v2).** This is the reason for the command, and the key to the book. In the Old Testament, idolatry is spiritual adultery (Exod 34:15–16; Jer 3:1–10; Ezek 16). God had bound himself to Israel in a covenant often pictured as a marriage. Israel kept up worship of the LORD at Bethel and Dan while also running to Baal for rain, crops and children. Hosea will live out in his own home what God has been living with Israel.
- **"So he went and took Gomer, the daughter of Diblaim" (v3).** Hosea obeyed without a recorded word of protest. Some have tried to find meaning in the names: "Diblaim" resembles the word for cakes of pressed figs, and Henry takes Gomer to signify "corruption," but these meanings are uncertain and most scholars take them simply as personal names.
- **"Call his name Jezreel" (v4).** Each child is a walking sermon, like Isaiah's sons (Isa 8:18). *Jezreel* means "God sows" or "God scatters," since the same verb is used of sowing seed and of scattering. It was also a place: the broad, fertile valley in the north, and the town where Ahab had a palace. That town was the scene of two infamous bloodbaths: Ahab and Jezebel had Naboth murdered there for his vineyard (1 Kings 21), and Jehu later killed Jezebel and all of Ahab's family there (2 Kings 9–10). To hear the name Jezreel was like hearing the name of a famous massacre.
- **"I will punish the house of Jehu for the blood of Jezreel" (v4).** This raises a real puzzle. God had commissioned Jehu to wipe out Ahab's house (2 Kings 9:6–10) and even commended him for it (2 Kings 10:30), promising his sons the throne for four generations. Jeroboam II was the fourth. So why punish Jehu's house now? The most common answer: Jehu went far beyond his orders, killing Ahaziah king of Judah and his relatives (2 Kings 9:27; 10:12–14) and Ahab's officials and friends, with a brutality that served his own ambition. And he did not turn from Jeroboam I's golden calves (2 Kings 10:29–31). He did God's work in his own spirit, for his own ends. Others suggest "the blood of Jezreel" means the violence that had become the royal way of life since then. The prophecy came true quickly: Jeroboam's son Zechariah reigned six months and was assassinated, ending the dynasty (2 Kings 15:8–12).
- **"I will break the bow of Israel in the Valley of Jezreel" (v5).** The bow stands for military power. The Jezreel Valley was the natural battlefield of the land, where Gideon, Saul and many others fought. In 733 BC the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III took the region (2 Kings 15:29), and eleven years later the kingdom was gone.
- **"Call her name No Mercy" (v6).** In Hebrew, *Lo-ruhamah*, "not pitied" or "not loved." The root *racham* is related to the word for "womb," and describes the tender, protective love of a parent for a child (Ps 103:13; Isa 49:15). God, whose name was proclaimed as "merciful and gracious" (Exod 34:6), is saying that this compassion has been rejected so long that it will be withdrawn. A little girl carrying that name is a terrible picture.
- **"But I will have mercy on the house of Judah" (v7).** The southern kingdom would be spared for now. The rescue would come "by the LORD their God," not by "bow or by sword… or by horses." This was fulfilled in 701 BC, when the Assyrian king Sennacherib besieged Jerusalem and the angel of the LORD struck down his army in a night (2 Kings 19:35), with no battle fought by Judah. Hosea's own king, by contrast, trusted horses and alliances (7:11; 14:3). Judah later followed Israel's path and fell to Babylon in 586 BC, but even then a remnant returned.
- **"When she had weaned No Mercy" (v8).** Children were usually nursed for two or three years. The detail shows time passing. JFB reads it as Israel being cut off step by step, not all at once. Henry sees in the delay a sign of God's patience.
- **"Call his name Not My People" (v9).** In Hebrew, *Lo-ammi*. This is the deepest cut. The covenant formula was "I will be your God, and you shall be my people" (Exod 6:7; Lev 26:12). Now it is undone. The second half is even sharper in Hebrew: "I am not *ehyeh* to you," using the word God used when he revealed his name to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exod 3:14). In effect: "I am not 'I AM' for you." The Tyndale notes make the same point. Israel had chosen to live as though God was not theirs, and God now says so aloud.
- **The chapter break.** In Hebrew Bibles, vv10–11 are the start of chapter 2 (2:1–2), so Hebrew verse numbers in chapter 2 run two ahead of English ones. The English division keeps the three names and their reversal together.
- **"Yet the number of the children of Israel shall be like the sand of the sea" (v10).** With no transition at all, judgment flips to hope. This is the promise God made to Abraham and Jacob (Gen 22:17; 32:12). God's covenant had looked dead in v9, but his promise to the patriarchs still stands underneath it.
- **"In the place where it was said to them, 'You are not my people,' it shall be said… 'Children of the living God'" (v10).** The title "children (sons) of the living God" appears nowhere else in the Old Testament. "Living" contrasts the LORD with the lifeless Baal and the golden calves. Both Paul and Peter quote this promise. Paul uses it (Rom 9:25–26) to show that God has called Gentiles, people who were never "my people," into his family along with Jews. Peter writes to scattered believers, "Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people" (1 Pet 2:10). The New Testament writers saw that if God could take back a people who had become "not my people," he could also bring in those who had never been his.
- **"Judah and… Israel shall be gathered together, and they shall appoint for themselves one head" (v11).** The kingdom had been split for about 170 years, since Solomon's son Rehoboam (1 Kings 12). Hosea foresees reunion under one leader. In 3:5 that leader is "David their king," that is, a king from David's line, the Messiah. Ezekiel gives the same picture with two sticks joined into one (Ezek 37:15–24).
- **"They shall go up from the land" (v11).** This may mean going up from exile in other lands, or "springing up" from the land like a crop, which fits the next line.
- **"Great shall be the day of Jezreel" (v11).** The name of the massacre becomes a name of harvest. *Jezreel*, "God scatters," now means "God sows." The people he scattered he will plant again (2:22–23). All three names are reversed in the next few verses (2:1, 23): Jezreel becomes a sowing, No Mercy becomes "Mercy," and Not My People becomes "My People."
- **Commentators:** On the marriage, the three part ways. JFB holds it was "not externally acted, but internally and in vision," arguing a real marriage would spoil the symbol. Henry reports the vision view as the majority opinion of his day. Guzik insists on a real marriage, following Boice, that if Hosea's redemption of Gomer is unreal, the picture of redemption it illustrates loses its force. Guzik leaves open whether Gomer was unfaithful before or only after the wedding. On Jehu, Henry says he was right to destroy Ahab's house but wrong in motive and because "he kept up the worship of the golden calves." On v7 Henry writes, "The less there is of man in any salvation, and the more of God, the brighter it shines." On v10, Henry: "It is the unspeakable privilege of all believers that they have the living God for their Father," and on v11, "Jesus Christ is the head of the church, the one only head of it." JFB sees Zerubbabel's return as a first, partial fulfilment and Christ as the full one, adding a future restoration of Israel (Rom 11:26).

#### Pointing to Christ
Hosea, whose name is so close to Jesus', married an unfaithful wife on God's orders and carried her shame in his own home. That is a faint picture of what God did in Christ, who took a bride he knew would wander and loved her anyway (Eph 5:25–27). The names Not My People and No Mercy are exactly what humanity deserves. In Christ they are reversed: "Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy" (1 Pet 2:10). The "one head" who gathers the scattered is the Son of David, and the ultimate "day of Jezreel," when God sows his people in the earth, began when Jesus spoke of himself as a grain of wheat that "falls into the earth and dies" and so "bears much fruit" (John 12:24).
