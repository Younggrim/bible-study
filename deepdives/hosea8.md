*Setting:* Hosea is preaching to the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim, with its capital at Samaria) in its last years before the Assyrian conquest of 722 BC, probably during the reign of one of the last kings: Menahem, Pekah or Hoshea. Chapter 7 described a nation burning with plots like a baker's oven, murdering its kings, fluttering between Egypt and Assyria like a silly dove. Chapter 8 sounds the alarm. The enemy is on its way. God lists the reasons in short, sharp charges: kings made without him, a calf made by a craftsman, alliances bought with money, altars that only multiply sin, a law ignored, and a Maker forgotten. At the centre is one of the most famous proverbs in the Bible: "they sow the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind."

#### Israel Will Reap the Whirlwind (vv1–14)
- **"Set the trumpet to your lips!" (v1).** The trumpet is the *shofar*, the ram's horn blown by watchmen to warn of attack (5:8; Amos 3:6). The Hebrew is abrupt, literally "To your mouth, the horn!", like a shout from the city wall. God tells the watchman to sound the alarm.
- **"One like a vulture is over the house of the LORD" (v1).** The bird is *nesher*, which can mean eagle or griffon vulture; ancient writers did not always distinguish them. Both swoop on prey. The picture is the Assyrian army (Deut 28:49: "a nation… swooping down like the eagle"). "The house of the LORD" here probably means God's land and people, as in 9:15, not a temple building. JFB takes it as Israel as God's family. **"They have transgressed my covenant and rebelled against my law"**: "law" is *torah*, God's whole instruction, not only rules.
- **"To me they cry, 'My God, we—Israel—know you'" (v2).** In the crisis they suddenly claim to know God, the very thing Hosea has said they lack (4:1, 6; 5:4). The words are right; the life is not. Jesus warned of people who will say "Lord, Lord" on the last day and hear "I never knew you" (Matt 7:21–23). JFB notes that each speaks individually, "my God," claiming a relationship their conduct denies.
- **"Israel has spurned the good; the enemy shall pursue him" (v3).** "The good" may mean the good life the covenant offered, or God himself. JFB takes it as God, "with all that is good." The verb "spurned" (*zanach*, reject, cast off) appears again in v5 (see below). Because they rejected good, they get the enemy instead.
- **"They made kings, but not through me" (v4).** Each assassin made himself king (7:7), and none asked God. In the north the first king had been God's choice (1 Kings 11:29–39), and Jehu was anointed by a prophet (2 Kings 9:1–6), but none of the last kings had any such call. **"I knew it not"** means "I did not approve," not that God was unaware. Some also hear a deeper charge: the whole northern monarchy began by rejecting David's house (1 Kings 12:16), and Hosea looks for a return to "David their king" (3:5).
- **"With their silver and gold they made idols for their own destruction" (v4).** God's gifts (2:8) turned into idols. The Hebrew can mean "so that it [the silver] may be cut off" or "so that they may be cut off." Either way, what they made will destroy them.
- **"I have spurned your calf, O Samaria" (v5).** The golden calves were set up by Jeroboam I at Bethel and Dan (1 Kings 12:28–30), probably as pedestals on which the invisible LORD was thought to stand, though people soon worshiped the calf itself. "Samaria" here means the kingdom, since the calf was at Bethel. The verb is the same *zanach* as in v3, a deliberate echo: Israel spurned the good; now the calf is spurned. The KJV reads "thy calf… hath cast thee off," taking the calf as subject; the ESV follows a slightly different vowel reading so that God is the one who rejects it. Guzik, citing Leon Wood, mentions a rendering "your calf stinks." The lexicon lists a separate root meaning "stink," but only in Isaiah 19:6, and puts this verse under "reject," so that rendering is unlikely.
- **"How long will they be incapable of innocence?" (v5).** Literally "how long will they not be able to be clean?" It echoes 5:4: their deeds do not let them return. It is the cry of a God who longs for them to change.
- **"A craftsman made it; it is not God" (v6).** Here is the heart of every idol. A human artisan shaped it, so it cannot be God. Isaiah mocks the same thing with a carpenter who burns half a log to cook dinner and bows down to the other half (Isa 44:9–20). **"The calf of Samaria shall be broken to pieces"**: Assyrian armies stripped temples of gold idols and carried them off (10:5–6). The calf that was supposed to protect Israel would itself be hauled away as plunder.
- **"For they sow the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind" (v7).** A farmer who sows wind sows nothing at all, empty effort. But here the harvest is worse than nothing: a storm. Sin is like that. What looks harmless and light when sown comes back multiplied and destructive. Paul says, "whatever one sows, that will he also reap" (Gal 6:7), and Proverbs 22:8, "Whoever sows injustice will reap calamity." Guzik observes that judgment often feels worse than the sin because sin is sown slowly over years while its harvest comes all at once.
- **"The standing grain has no heads; it shall yield no flour" (v7).** In Hebrew there is a rhyme: *tsemach* (sprout, growth) and *qemach* (flour), something like "stalk without a crop, a crop without flour." The stalks grow, but no grain forms. **"If it were to yield, strangers would devour it"**: even if a harvest came, foreigners would take it (7:9). Baal promised harvests. Baal delivered none.
- **"Israel is swallowed up; already they are among the nations as a useless vessel" (v8).** Israel has been absorbed into the nations it imitated (7:8). A "useless vessel" is a pot nobody wants, broken or tossed aside. Jeremiah uses the same picture for King Jehoiachin (Jer 22:28).
- **"They have gone up to Assyria, a wild donkey wandering alone" (v9).** Wild donkeys roamed the desert, stubborn and untamable (Job 39:5–8; Gen 16:12). There may also be a pun: "wild donkey" is *pere*, which sounds like *Ephraim*. The donkey goes off on its own; Israel goes off to Assyria. Jeremiah 2:24 uses a wild donkey in heat as a picture of Israel's craving for other gods. JFB connects this with Menahem's payment to Pul, the Assyrian king (2 Kings 15:19–20).
- **"Ephraim has hired lovers" (v9).** This reverses the usual pattern. A prostitute is normally paid, but Israel pays her lovers. Ezekiel makes the same point about Jerusalem: "you gave your payment… and no payment was given to you" (Ezek 16:33–34). Israel's tribute to Assyria and Egypt is pictured as buying love it can never have.
- **"I will soon gather them up" (v10).** "Gather" is often a word of rescue (Zech 10:8), but here it is the gathering of judgment, as a farmer gathers sheaves for the threshing floor (Joel 3:11; Mic 4:12). **"The king and princes shall soon writhe because of the tribute"** is literally "because of the burden of the king of princes." "King of princes" may be a title of the Assyrian king, who called himself "king of kings." The ESV reads the verb as "writhe" (in pain). The KJV, reading different vowels, has "they shall sorrow a little," and JFB and Guzik take it as a brief grief before the full judgment.
- **"Because Ephraim has multiplied altars for sinning, they have become to him altars for sinning" (v11).** The repetition is deliberate. They built altars supposedly to deal with sin, possibly sin offerings, but because they were built against God's command (Deut 12:5–14), the altars themselves became sin. The more religious activity, the more guilt. Amos 4:4 says it with sarcasm: "Come to Bethel, and transgress."
- **"Were I to write for him my laws by the ten thousands, they would be regarded as a strange thing" (v12).** God had given his law, and could give it ten thousand times over, but Israel would treat it as foreign, something that had nothing to do with them. JFB points out that the verse assumes Hosea's hearers knew a written law, which he takes as evidence the Pentateuch was already known. Most scholars agree Hosea knows traditions now found in the Pentateuch, such as the exodus, the covenant and Jacob's story (12:2–6), though they debate how much of it was written down by then.
- **"They sacrifice meat and eat it, but the LORD does not accept them" (v13).** In many offerings, the worshiper ate part of the meat as a fellowship meal with God (Lev 7:15–18; Deut 12:7). Israel kept the meal and lost God. Their worship had become a barbecue. JFB says their sacrifices served "their own appetites" and not God's honour. This repeats 6:6: God wants steadfast love and knowledge of him, not sacrifices without them.
- **"Now he will remember their iniquity and punish their sins; they shall return to Egypt" (v13).** The covenant curse said God would send them "back in ships to Egypt" (Deut 28:68). "Egypt" here stands for slavery and exile, the undoing of the exodus. Some Israelites did flee to Egypt, but most were taken to Assyria (9:3; 11:5). Hosea uses "Egypt" as the symbol of bondage.
- **"Israel has forgotten his Maker and built palaces" (v14).** "Forgotten" is the charge of 2:13 and 4:6. "Maker" reminds them that God made Israel a nation, as a potter shapes clay (Deut 32:6, 18). The word for "palaces" (*hekalot*) can also mean "temples." Either way, the buildings were monuments to their own greatness and false worship. Amos had seen the same: "the houses of ivory shall perish" (Amos 3:15).
- **"And Judah has multiplied fortified cities" (v14).** Judah's sin was quieter: trust in walls instead of God. Isaiah warned Jerusalem in the same years: "you did not look to him who did it" (Isa 22:8–11). **"I will send a fire upon his cities"** repeats the refrain of Amos 1–2, "I will send a fire." In 701 BC Sennacherib of Assyria captured "all the fortified cities of Judah" except Jerusalem (2 Kings 18:13). His own records boast of taking forty-six walled cities.
- **Commentators:** Guzik, quoting Boice, says that choosing leaders without God's direction "is not only sinful, it is foolish," and compares the cry of v2 with Jesus' "Lord, Lord" warning. JFB sees the eagle as Assyria and the "house of the LORD" as Israel, God's family, and links v9 to Menahem's tribute to Pul. On v12 JFB uses "the great things of my law" to argue Hosea knew a written law, while Guzik, citing Spurgeon, applies the verse to people who treat Scripture as strange today. Henry divides the chapter into sins and matching punishments, and sums up v13: "Their sacrifices should have no reckoning made of them, and their sins should have a reckoning made for them." On v5, Guzik's report of Wood's rendering "your calf stinks" is not supported by the lexicon, which reads "reject."

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel made kings "not through me" and a calf that was "not God." The gospel answers both. Jesus is the king God himself appointed, his anointed Son (Ps 2:6–7; Acts 2:36), and the Word who was God and became flesh (John 1:1, 14). He is the true image of God, not an image made by a craftsman, "the image of the invisible God" (Col 1:15). Israel sowed the wind and reaped the whirlwind. At the cross Jesus reaped a harvest he had not sown, bearing the storm of judgment for sins that were not his (Isa 53:6). And where Israel ate sacrificial meat while God refused their offerings, Jesus offered himself, the one sacrifice God accepted, and gave his people a meal at which he is the host (Luke 22:19–20).
