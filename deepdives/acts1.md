*Setting:* Luke, the physician who traveled with Paul (Col 4:14), writes the second volume of a two-part work, most likely in the early 60s AD. It picks up where his Gospel ended, with the risen Jesus on the Mount of Olives. The disciples have seen the cross, the empty tomb and the risen Lord, but they are still a small band of Galileans in Jerusalem, uncertain what comes next. This chapter covers the forty days, the ascension and the ten days of waiting before Pentecost. It also sets out the plan of the whole book in one sentence (v8).

#### The Promise of the Holy Spirit (vv1–5)
- **"In the first book, O Theophilus" (v1).** The "first book" is the Gospel of Luke, also addressed to Theophilus (Luke 1:3). There he is "most excellent," a title used for high Roman officials (Acts 23:26; 26:25), so he was probably a man of rank and perhaps Luke's patron, who paid for copying and circulating the work. His name means "friend of God," and some have read it as a symbol for any Christian reader, but the title suggests a real person.
- **"All that Jesus began to do and teach" (v1).** The word "began" quietly sets the theme of Acts. The Gospel told what Jesus *began* to do. Acts tells what he keeps doing, now from heaven, through his Spirit and his people. The book could fairly be called the Acts of the risen Lord as much as the Acts of the Apostles.
- **"Through the Holy Spirit" (v2).** Even the commands of the risen Jesus come through the Spirit. Luke has shown Jesus conceived, anointed and led by the Spirit (Luke 1:35; 3:22; 4:1, 18), and now the same Spirit is about to be given to his followers.
- **"Many proofs" (v3).** The Greek *tekmērion* appears only here in the New Testament. The lexicon gives "a sure sign, a positive proof," the word Greek writers used for decisive evidence, not mere hints. Jesus ate with them (Luke 24:42–43), invited them to touch him (Luke 24:39) and appeared to groups, once to more than five hundred at the same time (1 Cor 15:6).
- **"During forty days" (v3).** Only Luke gives the number. Forty is a familiar biblical span of preparation: Moses on Sinai, Israel in the wilderness, Jesus' own temptation. Here the risen Jesus spends it teaching "about the kingdom of God," the same theme as his earthly preaching and the theme of Acts to its last verse (28:31).
- **"Not to depart from Jerusalem, but to wait for the promise of the Father" (v4).** The city that killed Jesus is to be the starting point. The "promise" is the Spirit foretold by the prophets (Joel 2:28; Ezek 36:26–27) and by Jesus himself (Luke 24:49; John 14:16–17). Their first task is not to act but to wait.
- **"John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit" (v5).** Jesus repeats John the Baptist's own words (Luke 3:16). To "baptize" is to plunge or immerse: they will be drenched in the Spirit. "Not many days from now" turns out to be ten (Pentecost was fifty days after Passover).
- **Commentators:** Guzik spends time on Theophilus and the historical reliability of Luke, drawing on William Ramsay's work, and he stresses that the forty days taught nothing secret or new, a warning against later groups claiming hidden teachings from the risen Jesus. JFB calls "began to do and teach" "a very important statement," dividing Christ's work into what he did on earth and what he goes on doing from heaven. Matthew Henry summarizes the section as "the instructions he gave them during the forty days."

#### The Ascension (vv6–11)
- **"Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" (v6).** After forty days of teaching on the kingdom, this is still their question. They expect Israel's national independence and glory to return, now. Every part of the question shows it: "restore," "the kingdom," "to Israel," "at this time."
- **"It is not for you to know times or seasons" (v7).** Jesus does not deny that God has purposes for Israel (compare Rom 11:25–26). He refuses to give a timetable. "Times" (*chronos*) is the span of time, and "seasons" (*kairos*) the fitting moments. The Father has set both.
- **Readings differ.** Many dispensational and premillennial readers note that Jesus corrects only the timing, so a future restoration of Israel is still expected. Many Reformed and amillennial readers see the kingdom being redefined: it is restored as the risen Messiah reigns and the Spirit gathers Israel's remnant and then the nations. Both agree that the disciples' immediate job is witness, not speculation.
- **"You will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you" (v8).** The power is the Spirit's, not political or military. The verb is a promise, not an order: they *will* be witnesses. It is what the Spirit will make them.
- **"My witnesses" (v8).** A *martys* is a witness, someone who tells what they have seen, often in a legal sense. Later Christian usage gave English "martyr," because so many witnesses paid with their lives (22:20; Rev 2:13), but in this verse the word simply means witness.
- **"Jerusalem… Judea and Samaria… the end of the earth" (v8).** This is the outline of Acts: Jerusalem (chs 1–7), Judea and Samaria (chs 8–12), then outward to the Gentile world until Paul reaches Rome (chs 13–28). It echoes Isaiah's servant, "a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth" (Isa 49:6), which Paul quotes in 13:47.
- **"He was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight" (v9).** The ascension is not Jesus vanishing, as he did at Emmaus (Luke 24:31). They watch him go. In the Old Testament a cloud often marks God's presence: on Sinai, over the tabernacle, filling the temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kings 8:10). It also recalls Daniel's vision of one "like a son of man" coming "with the clouds of heaven" to the Ancient of Days (Dan 7:13).
- **"Two men… in white robes" (v10).** Luke describes angels this way at the empty tomb too (Luke 24:4). The same kind of messengers mark both the beginning and the end of the forty days.
- **"Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking into heaven?" (v11).** A gentle rebuke. Gazing is not the task. **"This Jesus… will come in the same way as you saw him go."** The same Jesus, bodily and visibly, with the clouds (Matt 24:30; 1 Thess 4:16–17; Rev 1:7). Zechariah 14:4 pictures the LORD's feet standing on the Mount of Olives on the day of the Lord.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry says, "They thought Christ would restore the kingdom to Israel," "whereas Christ came to set up his own kingdom, and that a kingdom of heaven." On the angels he writes, "Christ's disciples should never stand at a gaze, because they have a sure rule to go by." JFB, by contrast, notes that the disciples' hopes for Israel are "neither rebuked nor contradicted on this point," which is close to Guzik's view that Jesus corrected the timing, not the hope itself. Guzik also stresses that "you will be my witnesses" is a statement of fact, not a command, and that being filled with the Spirit matters more than technique.

#### Matthias Chosen to Replace Judas (vv12–26)
- **"The mount called Olivet… a Sabbath day's journey away" (v12).** The Mount of Olives is the ridge just east of Jerusalem across the Kidron Valley. A Sabbath day's journey was the distance the rabbis allowed on the Sabbath, about 2,000 cubits, a little over half a mile. Luke's Gospel places the ascension near Bethany (Luke 24:50), on the far slope of the same hill.
- **"The upper room, where they were staying" (v13).** A large upstairs room in a private house. It may be the room of the Last Supper (Luke 22:12), and some connect it with the house of Mary, John Mark's mother, where the church later met (12:12), but neither link is certain.
- **The eleven (v13).** The list matches Luke 6:14–16 without Judas Iscariot. Peter and John, who will lead the next chapters together, are now named first as a pair. **"Simon the Zealot"** may once have belonged to the movement that wanted to drive out Rome by force, a striking companion for **Matthew**, a former tax collector for Rome. **"Judas the son of James"** is probably the Thaddaeus of Matthew and Mark.
- **"With one accord… devoting themselves to prayer" (v14).** *Homothymadon* means "with one mind" or "with one accord." Luke uses it ten times in Acts, and it marks the early church's unity (2:46; 4:24; 5:12). "Devoting themselves" (*proskartereō*) means holding fast to something and keeping at it. They pray, and keep praying, through the ten days.
- **"The women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers" (v14).** The women who followed Jesus from Galilee and saw the cross and the tomb are there (Luke 8:2–3; 23:49; 24:10). This is the last time Mary is named in the New Testament: she prays among the disciples, not above them. Jesus' brothers had not believed in him during his ministry (John 7:5). Now they do. The risen Jesus appeared to James (1 Cor 15:7), who later leads the Jerusalem church (15:13), and Jude wrote the letter that bears his name.
- **"About 120" (v15).** The core believers in Jerusalem, though Paul mentions five hundred elsewhere, probably in Galilee. Some note that a Jewish community needed 120 men to set up its own council, but it is not clear Luke intends that.
- **"Peter stood up among the brothers" (v15).** The man who denied Jesus three times now leads. Jesus had prayed for this: "when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers" (Luke 22:32). He leads by opening the Scriptures, not by issuing decrees.
- **"The Scripture had to be fulfilled" (v16).** The "had to" is the same divine necessity Jesus used of his own suffering (Luke 24:26, 44). Judas's betrayal did not catch God off guard. It does not excuse Judas: Peter calls the money "the reward of his wickedness" (v18).
- **"Falling headlong he burst open" (v18).** Matthew says Judas hanged himself and the priests bought a potter's field with the money he threw back (Matt 27:3–10). The two accounts are commonly harmonized this way: the priests bought the field with Judas's money, so in effect it was his purchase, and he hanged himself there, after which the body fell, perhaps when the rope or branch gave way, and burst open. Luke gives the grim result, Matthew the cause.
- **"Akeldama, that is, Field of Blood" (v19).** The name is Aramaic, the everyday language of Jerusalem ("their own language"). Matthew explains the name by the blood money, Luke by Judas's blood. Both can be true. Tradition places the field in the Hinnom Valley south of the city.
- **Two psalms (v20).** "May his camp become desolate" is from Psalm 69:25 and "Let another take his office" from Psalm 109:8. Both are psalms of David about a righteous sufferer betrayed by those close to him. Peter reads them as pointing ahead to the greater Son of David and his betrayer. "Office" is *episkopē*, oversight, the word later used for an overseer's role.
- **The qualifications (vv21–22).** The replacement must have been with them from John's baptism to the ascension, and must be "a witness to his resurrection." An apostle in this sense was first an eyewitness of the risen Jesus. That is why the Twelve could have no successors in that role.
- **Why twelve?** Jesus chose twelve to match the twelve tribes and promised they would sit on thrones judging them (Luke 22:30). Restoring the number before Pentecost shows the renewed Israel being gathered around the Messiah. Notice that when James is killed later (12:2), no one replaces him: the office was lost by Judas's betrayal, not by a faithful apostle's death.
- **Joseph Barsabbas and Matthias (v23).** Neither appears in the Gospels. Both had followed Jesus all along without being among the Twelve, a reminder of how many quiet disciples there were. Matthias is never mentioned again in Scripture, and later traditions about his ministry vary widely.
- **"You, Lord, who know the hearts of all" (v24).** They pray before choosing, and they ask the Lord to *show* whom he has already chosen. "Lord" here is most likely Jesus, who chose the first Twelve.
- **"To go to his own place" (v25).** A restrained phrase for Judas's end. Jesus called him "the son of destruction" (John 17:12).
- **"They cast lots" (v26).** Probably stones marked with the names, shaken in a container until one fell out. Israel's priests did this (Lev 16:8; 1 Chron 24:5), and the proverb says, "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Prov 16:33). This is the last time lots are used in Scripture to find God's will. After Pentecost the church is guided by the Spirit through prayer, prophecy and counsel (13:2; 15:28).
- **Was it right to choose Matthias?** Some have argued that the eleven acted too soon and that Paul was God's real choice for the twelfth place. Luke gives no hint of that. He reports the choice without criticism and later counts "the twelve" (6:2), and Paul himself says he was an apostle "untimely born," separate from the Twelve (1 Cor 15:5, 8).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry notes that Peter "did not sit down, as one that gave laws, or had any supremacy over the rest," and Guzik likewise rejects reading a line of papal succession into Peter's role here. Henry defends the lot as "lawful to be used for determining matters not otherwise determinable" when used with prayer, and observes that Matthias "was not ordained by the imposition of hands." Guzik agrees the lot was a humble reliance on God, though perhaps an imperfect method, and he rejects the view that Matthias was a mistake. JFB says Luke's account of Judas "supplements, but by no means contradicts" Matthew, and calls v14 Mary's last mention in the New Testament, rejecting later legends about her.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter turns on a Jesus who is not absent. He proves he is alive, promises his Spirit, rises in a cloud like the Son of Man in Daniel, and will come back the same way. In between, his people wait, pray and repair the circle a betrayer broke, because the risen King is regathering his Israel. What Jesus "began to do and teach" is not finished. The rest of Acts is him carrying it on.
