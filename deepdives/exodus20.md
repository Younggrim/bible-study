*Setting:* Israel stands at the foot of Mount Sinai, about two months out of Egypt. For three days the people have washed and waited. Now the mountain is wrapped in smoke and fire, the ground is shaking, and a trumpet no human is blowing grows louder and louder (19:16–19). Chapter 19 ended with Moses going down to the people, and now God himself speaks. The Ten Commandments are the heart of the covenant God offered in 19:5–6, the terms of Israel's life as his "treasured possession." They are repeated, with small differences, in Deuteronomy 5:6–21, forty years later. The rest of the chapter shows the people's terror and their plea for a go-between, and then gives the first of the detailed laws that run through chapter 23, the section often called the Book of the Covenant (24:7).

#### The Ten Commandments (vv1–21)
- **"And God spoke all these words" (v1).** Everything else in the law came through Moses. These words alone God spoke directly to the whole nation, out of the fire (Deut 4:12–13; 5:22). Scripture never calls them "the Ten Commandments" in Hebrew. It calls them "the ten words" (34:28; Deut 4:13; 10:4), which is where the old name "Decalogue" comes from (Greek for "ten words"). They were later written by God's own finger on two tablets of stone (31:18).
- **The covenant form.** The Tyndale notes point out how closely this follows the pattern of ancient treaties between a great king and a subject people, known especially from Hittite documents of the second millennium BC: the king names himself, recalls what he has done for them, then states his terms, with blessings and curses to follow. God speaks to Israel as their King, but without any of the pagan religious trappings of the age.
- **"I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (v2).** Before a single command, God says who he is and what he has done. LORD in small capitals stands for *Yahweh*, the covenant name revealed to Moses at the bush (3:14–15). **"Out of the house of slavery."** Literally "house of slaves." The order matters: God did not say "keep these laws and I will rescue you," but "I rescued you; now live like my people." Obedience is the response to grace, not the price of it. Jewish tradition actually counts this verse as the first "word."
- **How to count ten.** The text says there are ten words but does not number them, and traditions divide them differently. Most Protestants and Orthodox count "no other gods" (v3) and "no carved image" (vv4–6) as two, and "covet" (v17) as one. Catholics and Lutherans, following Augustine, take vv3–6 as one command and split v17 into two (house, then wife), following the order in Deuteronomy 5:21. Jewish tradition counts v2 as the first word and vv3–6 as the second. The content is the same in every case.
- **Two tablets.** Many think the first four commands, about God, were on one tablet and the last six, about neighbors, on the other. It is more likely that each tablet held a complete copy, one for each party to the covenant, as was done with ancient treaties. Either way, Jesus summed up the two halves: love God with all your heart, and love your neighbor as yourself (Matt 22:37–40).
- **"You shall have no other gods before me" (v3).** "Before me" is literally "before my face," that is, in my presence, alongside me or besides me. Israel had just come out of a land with dozens of gods, and was heading into a land with dozens more. The command does not argue that other gods do not exist; later Scripture says that plainly (Deut 4:35; Isa 45:5). It simply forbids giving them any place. God will not be one god among many, or even the first among many.
- **"You shall not make for yourself a carved image" (v4).** The first command is about *which* God to worship. The second is about *how*. Israel was not to make an image even of the LORD, as the golden calf would soon try to do (32:4–5). Moses later explains why: "you saw no form on the day that the LORD spoke to you at Horeb out of the midst of the fire" (Deut 4:15–16). An image shrinks God to something in his own creation. **"In heaven above… in the earth beneath… in the water under the earth."** Egypt worshiped sun and stars, cattle, crocodiles, frogs and the Nile itself. Nothing in creation may stand in for the Creator (Rom 1:23).
- **Is all religious art forbidden?** The same God soon commands golden cherubim over the ark and woven into the curtains (25:18; 26:1), and almonds, flowers and pomegranates on the lampstand and robes. The next words make the point clear: **"You shall not bow down to them or serve them" (v5).** The command forbids images made to be worshiped. Christians have disagreed about how far this reaches: Orthodox and Catholic traditions honor icons and images (while denying that they worship them), and the Reformed tradition has been more restrictive. All agree that worship belongs to God alone.
- **"For I the LORD your God am a jealous God" (v5).** The word is *qanna*, used only of God in the Old Testament. Human jealousy is often petty, but this is the right jealousy of a husband who will not share his wife with another man. The prophets use exactly that picture for Israel's idolatry (Hos 2; Ezek 16). God's jealousy is the fierce side of his love. Later he says, "the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God" (34:14).
- **"Visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate me" (v5).** This does not mean God punishes innocent children for their parents' sins. The law itself says, "Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be put to death because of their fathers" (Deut 24:16; compare Ezek 18:20). It describes what happens when sin runs through a family line, "of those who hate me," as children repeat what they learned. Three or four generations is roughly how many might live in one household at once. **"But showing steadfast love to thousands" (v6).** The contrast is the point. "Steadfast love" is *chesed*, God's loyal covenant love. The ESV footnote offers "to the thousandth generation" (compare Deut 7:9). Judgment is limited to a few generations; mercy runs on for a thousand.
- **"You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain" (v7).** To "take" the name is literally to "lift up" or "carry" it. "In vain" (*shav*) means empty, worthless, false. This is about more than swearing. It includes oaths in God's name that one does not mean to keep (Lev 19:12), using God's name to dress up a lie, and treating his name lightly. In a sense Israel itself carried God's name before the world, and a people who bore his name but lived like pagans took it in vain. Jesus taught his disciples to pray first, "hallowed be your name" (Matt 6:9). **"The LORD will not hold him guiltless."** Others may shrug at it, but God takes his name seriously.
- **"Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy" (v8).** "Remember" suggests the Sabbath was not entirely new. God had already taught it with the manna, which did not fall on the seventh day (16:23–30), and it goes back to creation (Gen 2:2–3). "Sabbath" (*shabbat*) comes from a verb meaning to stop or cease. Deuteronomy's version says "Observe" (Deut 5:12). To keep it holy is to set it apart for God.
- **"Six days you shall labor… but the seventh day is a Sabbath to the LORD your God" (vv9–10).** Work is commanded too. The command shapes the whole week, not just one day. **"You, or your son, or your daughter, your male servant, or your female servant, or your livestock, or the sojourner."** This was remarkable in the ancient world. The rest belonged to slaves, foreigners and even animals, not just the free and powerful. A people who had just come out of slavery, where Pharaoh had refused them any rest (5:4–9), were to give rest to everyone in their household.
- **"For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth" (v11).** Here the reason is creation: Israel copies God's own pattern of work and rest. In Deuteronomy the reason is redemption: "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (Deut 5:15). Both are true. The Sabbath points back to God as Maker and as Rescuer.
- **Christians and the Sabbath.** Faithful believers have disagreed. Some keep the seventh day (Saturday) still. Many in the Reformed tradition see the Lord's Day (Sunday) as the Christian Sabbath, moved to the day of the resurrection (Acts 20:7; Rev 1:10). Others, reading Colossians 2:16–17 and Hebrews 4:9–10, see the Sabbath as a shadow fulfilled in the rest Christ gives, while still honoring the wisdom of a weekly day of rest and worship. Jesus himself said, "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27), and called himself "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28).
- **"Honor your father and your mother" (v12).** "Honor" is *kabed*, from the root for "heavy" or "weighty": to give weight, to treat as important. It is the word used for glorifying God. It applies to adult children as well as young ones: Jesus rebuked those who used religious loopholes to avoid supporting their aged parents (Mark 7:9–13). **"That your days may be long in the land."** Paul calls this "the first commandment with a promise" (Eph 6:2–3). A society where families hold together and the old are honored is a society that lasts. This command stands at the hinge between duties to God and duties to neighbor. Parents are God's first authority in a child's life.
- **"You shall not murder" (v13).** The verb is *ratsach*. The lexicon gives "murder, slay," usually with intent, and it is never used for killing in war. The law itself requires capital punishment for some crimes (21:12) and allows killing in self-defense in some cases (22:2), so the older rendering "Thou shalt not kill" is too broad. Interestingly, the same verb is used for the accidental "manslayer" who flees to a city of refuge (Num 35:11), so it covers all wrongful taking of a human life. The reason goes back to creation: man is made "in the image of God" (Gen 9:6). Jesus took the command to the heart: anger and contempt are murder's root (Matt 5:21–22).
- **"You shall not commit adultery" (v14).** Adultery is sex with someone else's spouse, the sharpest form of breaking faith. The command guards marriage, the first human relationship God made (Gen 2:24). In the ancient world a husband's affairs were often overlooked. The law here makes no such allowance. Jesus again goes to the heart: "everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matt 5:28).
- **"You shall not steal" (v15).** The command assumes that people have a right to what is theirs. Jewish tradition sometimes read it as forbidding kidnapping above all, stealing a person (21:16), but its plain sense covers any taking of what belongs to another. Paul gives the positive side: "Let the thief no longer steal, but rather let him labor… so that he may have something to share with anyone in need" (Eph 4:28).
- **"You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (v16).** The words come from the courtroom. A false witness could get an innocent man executed, as happened to Naboth (1 Kgs 21:10–13) and was attempted with Jesus (Mark 14:56–57). The law required at least two witnesses for a death sentence (Deut 17:6) and gave a false witness the very penalty he meant for the other person (Deut 19:16–19). The principle reaches further, to slander, gossip and any lie that harms someone's name.
- **"You shall not covet" (v17).** The verb *chamad* means to desire or take pleasure in. It can be used in a good sense: the trees in Eden were "pleasant" (*nechmad*, Gen 2:9). Here it means a grasping desire for what belongs to someone else. Achan's confession uses the same word: "I coveted them and took them" (Josh 7:21). This command is different from the others because it reaches inside. No human court could enforce it. It was this command that showed Paul his own sin: "I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet'" (Rom 7:7). And it circles back to the first: covetousness "is idolatry" (Col 3:5).
- **"Your neighbor's house… wife… male servant… female servant… ox… donkey" (v17).** "House" here probably means the whole household, which the rest of the list spells out. Deuteronomy 5:21 puts the wife first and uses a second verb for the other things. The list is a picture of everything a man in that world might own or envy.
- **"When all the people saw the thunder and the flashes of lightning" (v18).** Literally "saw the voices," the same word *qolot* as 19:16. The ESV follows the sense. **"They stood far off."** The people pull back from the boundary.
- **"You speak to us, and we will listen; but do not let God speak to us, lest we die" (v19).** Moses later says God approved this request: "They are right in all that they have spoken" (Deut 5:28). It is the right instinct of sinners before a holy God, and it leads straight to the need for a mediator. God promises in the same chapter of Deuteronomy to raise up "a prophet like me from among your brothers" (Deut 18:15–18), and the New Testament sees Jesus as that prophet (Acts 3:22).
- **"Do not fear, for God has come to test you, that the fear of him may be before you, that you may not sin" (v20).** This is a play on words. "Do not fear" means do not be terrified that you will be destroyed. "The fear of him" means a lasting reverence and awe that keeps them from sin. The first fear drives people away from God. The second keeps them close to him and away from evil. "Test" here means to prove what is in them, as God tested Israel with manna (16:4).
- **"Moses drew near to the thick darkness where God was" (v21).** God's presence is "thick darkness" (*arafel*), as Solomon later says: "The LORD has said that he would dwell in thick darkness" (1 Kgs 8:12). The darkness is not evil but the hiddenness of God's glory, too bright to look at. The people stand far off; Moses draws near. The contrast sets up the rest of the book: the tabernacle will be God's way of drawing near to a people who cannot yet draw near to him.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that God "never spake at any time so as he spake the TEN COMMANDMENTS," and calls the first command one that concerns "the object of worship, JEHOVAH, and him only," and the second "the worship we are to render." On the last: "The tenth commandment strikes at the root." On v12 he adds that careful observers have seen "a peculiar blessing in temporal things on obedient" children. JFB reads "before me" as "in My presence, beside, or except Me" and argues that "Remember" shows the Sabbath "was already known, and recognized as a season of sacred rest." On v4 JFB says making images was not sinful in itself; the sin was in making them "in order to bow." Guzik makes several of the same points: grace before law, images made for worship rather than art, and the difference between the verb for murder and general killing. He prefers "zealous" for *qanna* and reads "visiting iniquity" as the consequence on those who go on hating God, not punishment of innocent heirs, which matches the Tyndale notes. Guzik says "covet" literally means "to pant after," but the lexicon gives "desire, take pleasure in" for *chamad*. The panting picture belongs to other Hebrew words, so it is better not to press it here. Guzik also notes how quickly the people, who trembled here, would make the golden calf.

#### Laws About Altars (vv22–26)
- **"You have seen for yourselves that I have talked with you from heaven" (v22).** This begins the Book of the Covenant (20:22–23:33), the detailed case laws that apply the Ten Commandments to daily life. It starts where the Ten Words did, with worship. "From heaven" and yet on the mountain: God is above all creation and also truly present.
- **"You shall not make gods of silver to be with me, nor… gods of gold" (v23).** The first and second commands, restated. "With me" means alongside me, as partners. The warning is pointed, because the next time gold is mentioned in the camp, Aaron will make it into a calf (32:2–4).
- **"An altar of earth you shall make for me" (v24).** A simple heap of soil or mud brick, not an elaborate shrine. **"Burnt offerings and… peace offerings."** A burnt offering was burned up entirely, a gift wholly given to God. A peace offering (or fellowship offering) was shared, part burned and part eaten by the worshipers in a meal before God. Leviticus 1 and 3 give the details.
- **"In every place where I cause my name to be remembered I will come to you and bless you" (v24).** Israel was not to choose holy places for itself, as the Canaanites did on every high hill. God would choose where he wanted to be worshiped, and he would come to meet them there. Before the temple, God was worshiped at several places: Shiloh, Shechem, Gilgal, Mount Carmel. Later he chose one central place (Deut 12:5), finally Jerusalem.
- **"If you make me an altar of stone, you shall not build it of hewn stones" (v25).** Altars of stone were to be built from natural, uncut fieldstones. Joshua obeyed this on Mount Ebal (Josh 8:30–31), and Elijah rebuilt such an altar on Carmel (1 Kgs 18:31–32). **"If you wield your tool on it you profane it."** Why? The text does not say. Many suggest that human craftsmanship would draw attention to the builder, or invite carving images, or treat the altar as a human achievement. The bronze altar of the tabernacle (27:1–8) was a different, portable kind and did not break this rule.
- **"You shall not go up by steps to my altar, that your nakedness be not exposed on it" (v26).** Men in that time wore loose robes or kilts, so climbing steps could expose them to those below. Canaanite worship often involved nakedness and sexual rites. Israel's worship was to be modest and holy. Later God provided linen undergarments for the priests (28:42–43), and ramps were used instead of steps. Some commentators take this as a principle: worship must never draw attention to the human body or the human performer.
- **Commentators:** JFB takes the earthen altar as meant for "special or temporary occasions," says carved stone was forbidden because figures and ornaments "might lead to superstition," and explains the rule about steps by the priests' "loose, flowing garments." Henry introduces these verses simply: "The laws in these verses relate to God's worship," and he notes that knowing the law "shows our need of repentance." Guzik draws the lesson that the simplest altar keeps glory on God rather than on the builder, and points out that a law with sacrifices built into it already admits that people will fail to keep it.

#### Pointing to Christ
The Ten Words show what God is like and what a life lived in his image looks like: loyal to him alone, truthful, faithful, generous, content. But the chapter also shows a people who cannot bear to hear God speak, standing far off while one man goes into the darkness for them. The law was good, but it could not change the heart; it could only show what the heart lacked (Rom 7:7–12; Gal 3:24). Jesus did not come to abolish the law but to fulfill it (Matt 5:17). He is the only one who ever loved God with all his heart and his neighbor as himself. He is the prophet like Moses, and more than Moses, for "the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (John 1:17). And at the end of the chapter, before any temple or priesthood, God already provides an altar where sinners can bring a sacrifice and he will "come to you and bless you." Every altar of earth points to the cross, where God came down and blessed his people through the one perfect sacrifice.
