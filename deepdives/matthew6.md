*Setting:* The middle of the Sermon on the Mount, on a hillside in Galilee early in Jesus' ministry. In chapter 5 Jesus said his disciples' righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20), and showed what that means in anger, lust, divorce, oaths, revenge and love for enemies. Chapter 5 was about what righteousness looks like. Chapter 6 turns to why and for whom: the motive behind religious practice and the master of the heart. The first half takes three pillars of Jewish devotion, giving, prayer and fasting, and contrasts doing them for show with doing them for the Father. At its center is the prayer Jesus taught. The second half turns to money, possessions and worry, and ends with the call to seek God's kingdom first.

#### Giving to the Needy (vv1–4)
- **"Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them" (v1).** This verse is the heading for vv1–18. "Righteousness" here means religious duties. Some manuscripts read "alms," but the best read "righteousness," which covers all three examples that follow. It is not that good deeds must never be seen. Jesus has just said "let your light shine before others" (5:16). The issue is "in order to be seen." The question is whose applause is wanted.
- **"Then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven" (v1).** Jesus is not shy about rewards. God is a Father who delights to reward his children (Heb 11:6). But a reward collected from people cannot also be collected from God.
- **"When you give to the needy" (v2).** "When," not "if." Giving to the poor was expected of every Jew (Deut 15:7–11), and rabbis ranked it with prayer and fasting as the core of piety. The word for "giving to the needy" (*eleēmosynē*) comes from the word for mercy and gives English "alms."
- **"Sound no trumpet before you, as the hypocrites do" (v2).** There is no clear record of people literally blowing trumpets when they gave. Some suggest the trumpets blown at public fasts when gifts were collected, or the trumpet-shaped offering boxes in the temple. Most likely it is a figure of speech, as in "blowing one's own horn." "Hypocrites" (*hypokritēs*) originally meant stage actors who wore masks. Religion becomes a performance for an audience.
- **"They have received their reward" (v2).** The verb *apechō* was used in business documents of the time to mean "paid in full," as on a receipt. Papyri from Egypt use it this way. The hypocrites got what they wanted, the praise of people, and the account is closed. Jesus repeats this sentence three times (vv2, 5, 16).
- **"Do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing" (v3).** A vivid exaggeration: giving so quietly that even the giver's other hand does not notice. It means not keeping score, not even admiring oneself.
- **"Your Father who sees in secret will reward you" (v4).** Some later manuscripts add "openly," but the oldest do not. The reward comes from the one who sees what no one else sees. Notice the repeated "your Father." The whole chapter is about living before a Father, not an audience.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that a hypocrite was originally an actor, and cites Barclay that *apechō* means "paid in full," which the use of the word in commercial receipts supports. JFB prefers the reading "righteousness" in v1, since copyists could easily have changed it to "alms," and calls the trumpet "figuratively for blazoning it." Henry: "When we take least notice of our good deeds ourselves, God takes most notice of them."

#### The Lord's Prayer (vv5–15)
- **"They love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners" (v5).** Devout Jews prayed at set times, morning and afternoon, often at 9 a.m. and 3 p.m. (Acts 3:1). Standing was a normal posture for prayer. The problem is not public prayer but timing it so the street is crowded.
- **"Go into your room and shut the door" (v6).** The word (*tameion*) means an inner storeroom, often the only room in a house with a door that shut. It echoes Isaiah 26:20 ("enter your chambers, and shut your doors"). Jesus himself prayed alone (14:23; Mark 1:35). He does not forbid praying with others (18:19–20). He says the heart of prayer is between a child and the Father who sees in secret.
- **"Do not heap up empty phrases as the Gentiles do" (v7).** The verb (*battalogeō*) appears nowhere else in Greek before this. It probably imitates the sound of babbling, like "blah-blah." Pagan prayers often piled up the names and titles of a god to make sure the right one was addressed, and repeated formulas to wear the god down. The prophets of Baal cried out from morning until noon (1 Kings 18:26), and the Ephesian crowd chanted for two hours (Acts 19:34). Jesus does not condemn long or repeated prayer as such. He prayed the same words three times in Gethsemane (26:44). He condemns treating prayer as a technique to manipulate God.
- **"Your Father knows what you need before you ask him" (v8).** Then why pray? Not to inform God, but to depend on him, as children who know where good things come from (7:11).
- **"Pray then like this" (v9).** The prayer that follows is a pattern, not only a formula. Luke gives a shorter form, taught at another time when a disciple asked, "Lord, teach us to pray" (Luke 11:1–4). Christians have prayed these words together since the earliest days. The *Didache*, a church manual from about AD 100, tells believers to pray it three times a day.
- **"Our Father in heaven" (v9).** "Our," not "my": it is a family prayer. Jews spoke of God as Father of the nation (Isa 63:16; 64:8), but rarely addressed him in prayer as "Father." Jesus did so constantly, using the Aramaic *Abba* (Mark 14:36), and he gives his disciples the same privilege (Rom 8:15; Gal 4:6). "In heaven" keeps the intimacy reverent. This Father is the King of all.
- **"Hallowed be your name" (v9).** "Hallowed" means treated as holy. God's "name" means God himself as he has made himself known. The prophets said God's name was profaned when his people lived badly before the nations, and promised he would act to make it holy (Ezek 36:22–23). The first request is not for us but for God's glory. A familiar Jewish synagogue prayer, the *Kaddish*, begins in a very similar way: "Magnified and sanctified be his great name… may he establish his kingdom."
- **"Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (v10).** In heaven God's will is done perfectly and gladly. The prayer asks for the same on earth. The kingdom has come near in Jesus (4:17), but it has not yet come in full. "On earth as it is in heaven" probably applies to all three of the first requests. Jesus will pray "your will be done" in Gethsemane with blood-like sweat (26:42). This is not a passive prayer. It submits the one praying to God's will first.
- **"Give us this day our daily bread" (v11).** The turn to our needs comes only after God's name, kingdom and will. The Greek word for "daily" (*epiousios*) appears nowhere else in surviving Greek outside this prayer and its parallel in Luke. It may mean "necessary for existence," "for today," or "for the coming day," that is, tomorrow's bread given today. All three readings come to much the same thing: enough for now. The manna in the wilderness was given one day at a time (Exod 16:4). Some early Christians also heard a reference to the bread of the Lord's Supper or Christ the bread of life (John 6:35). That may be a fair echo, but the plain meaning is ordinary food.
- **"Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (v12).** In Aramaic, the language Jesus spoke, the word *hoba* meant both "debt" and "sin." Sin is something owed to God that cannot be repaid. Luke's version has "forgive us our sins" (Luke 11:4). "As we also have forgiven" is in the past tense. Those who ask forgiveness are people who have already let others off. This does not mean forgiving others earns God's forgiveness. It means a forgiven heart forgives. Jesus' parable of the unforgiving servant (18:21–35) explains it.
- **"Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil" (v13).** "Temptation" (*peirasmos*) means testing or trial. God tempts no one to sin (Jas 1:13), but he does allow tests, as he led Jesus into the wilderness (4:1). The request means: do not let us be overwhelmed by trial, keep us from falling. Jesus told the sleeping disciples, "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (26:41). "From evil" can also be translated "from the evil one," since the Greek has the article (*tou ponērou*). The ESV puts "evil one" in a footnote. Either way, Jesus has just met the tempter face to face.
- **"For yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory, forever. Amen."** These familiar closing words are not in the oldest Greek manuscripts of Matthew, so the ESV leaves them out of the text. They appear in the *Didache* in a shorter form and in many later manuscripts. They were probably added early for use in worship, drawing on David's prayer: "Yours, O LORD, is the greatness and the power and the glory" (1 Chron 29:11). They are true and fitting, and many churches still pray them, but they were probably not part of Jesus' original words.
- **"If you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (vv14–15).** Jesus singles out one petition for comment. Of all the requests in the prayer, forgiveness is the one people most easily say without meaning. A person who refuses to forgive shows that he has not grasped how much he himself has been forgiven. "Trespasses" (*paraptōma*) means a false step, stepping over a line.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that the word for "room" meant a storeroom, where treasures were kept, and that *battalogeō* may imitate the sound of babbling. He insists "daily bread" means real bread, and notes that most modern scholars treat the doxology as a later addition. JFB says "Babble not" would be a better rendering, and that repetition itself is not condemned. JFB favors "the bread of subsistence" for *epiousios*, and judges the doxology "no part of the original text." JFB adds that our forgiving others is not the ground of God's forgiveness but a sign of a forgiving heart. Henry: "You may as soon find a living man that does not breathe, as a living Christian that does not pray," and the Lord's Prayer "has much in a little." Henry also says, "Those who desire to find mercy with God, must show mercy to their brethren."

#### Fasting (vv16–18)
- **"When you fast" (v16).** Again, "when." The law required only one fast a year, on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29–31). Pharisees fasted twice a week, usually Mondays and Thursdays (Luke 18:12), which happened to be market days, when towns were busiest. Jesus expects his disciples to fast, especially after he is gone (9:15).
- **"Do not look gloomy like the hypocrites, for they disfigure their faces" (v16).** "Disfigure" (*aphanizō*) is literally "make invisible." There is a wordplay in Greek: they make their faces unseen so that they may be seen (*phanōsin*). They went unwashed, perhaps with ashes on their heads (Isa 58:5), so that everyone would notice their piety.
- **"Anoint your head and wash your face" (v17).** Olive oil was used daily to groom hair and skin in the dry climate. Jesus says look normal. Isaiah had condemned fasting that was all show and no justice: "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness… to share your bread with the hungry?" (Isa 58:6–7).
- **"Your Father who sees in secret will reward you" (v18).** The same refrain as vv4 and 6. Three practices, one principle: live for an audience of one.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "disfigure" literally means "make unseen," and pictures ashes sprinkled on the head. Guzik says the oil was ordinary daily grooming, not a sign of celebration. Henry calls fasting "the humbling of the soul," and adds, "God sees in secret, and will reward openly."

#### Lay Up Treasures in Heaven (vv19–24)
- **"Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy" (v19).** Wealth in that world was often kept as fine clothing, which moths ate, and as grain or metal goods, which rotted or corroded. "Rust" (*brōsis*) literally means "eating," and may refer to rot, vermin or corrosion. Houses were made of mud brick, and thieves literally "break in" by digging through the wall (*diorussō*, "dig through").
- **"Lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven" (v20).** Jesus does not tell his disciples to stop caring about treasure. He tells them to invest it where it lasts. Jewish teaching already spoke of good deeds, especially generosity to the poor, as treasure stored with God. Jesus told the rich young man: "sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven" (19:21; Luke 12:33).
- **"For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (v21).** The heart follows the treasure. What a person invests in, they come to love. This is the key to the passage.
- **"The eye is the lamp of the body" (v22).** Ancient people often thought of the eye as letting light in, like a window, or even giving off light. A healthy eye lets a person see and live in the light.
- **"If your eye is healthy… if your eye is bad" (vv22–23).** "Healthy" is *haplous*, "single," "simple," "undivided," and it can also mean "generous" (the related noun means generosity in Rom 12:8; 2 Cor 8:2). A "bad eye" (literally "evil eye") was a Hebrew idiom for stinginess or envy: "Is your eye bad because I am good?" (20:15; Deut 15:9; Prov 28:22). In this context about money, Jesus means both: an eye fixed single-mindedly on God, and a generous eye. A grasping, divided outlook darkens the whole life. "If then the light in you is darkness, how great is the darkness!"
- **"No one can serve two masters" (v24).** "Serve" means serve as a slave. A slave could not belong fully to two owners. A person can work two jobs, but cannot give his whole allegiance to two lords.
- **"You cannot serve God and money" (v24).** "Money" is *mamōnas*, from the Aramaic *mamona*, "wealth" or "property," which the KJV kept as "mammon." It may come from a root meaning "that in which one trusts." Jesus personifies it as a rival god. The choice is not between God and poverty but between two masters. Paul calls greed idolatry (Col 3:5).
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees a double meaning in *haplous*, both single-mindedness and generosity, which the lexicon and the "evil eye" idiom support. JFB reads the "single eye" as a "single-minded purpose" toward God, and gives the likely sense of mammon as "what one trusts in." Henry: "A man may do some service to two masters, but he can devote himself to the service of no more than one," and "Christ counsels to make our best things the joys and glories of the other world."

#### Do Not Be Anxious (vv25–34)
- **"Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life" (v25).** "Therefore" links this to the two masters. Worry is what serving money looks like when money is scarce. "Be anxious" (*merimnaō*) means to be pulled in different directions, divided by care. It appears six times in vv25–34. The KJV's "take no thought" meant this in 1611, but now sounds as if Jesus forbids planning. He does not. Proverbs praises the ant that prepares in summer (Prov 6:6–8). He forbids worry.
- **"Look at the birds of the air: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns" (v26).** Birds work for their food, but they do not store up or fret. God feeds them. The argument runs from lesser to greater, a common rabbinic method: "Are you not of more value than they?" (see 10:29–31).
- **"Which of you by being anxious can add a single hour to his span of life?" (v27).** Literally "add one cubit (*pēchys*) to his *hēlikia*." The word can mean height ("stature," KJV) or age, life span. A cubit was about eighteen inches. Since no one worries about growing taller, most translations take it as length of life, with "cubit" used figuratively for a small step. Either way, worry adds nothing.
- **"Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow: they neither toil nor spin" (v28).** The "lilies" were probably the wildflowers that cover Galilee's hills in spring, such as red anemones, which would fit the next verse's royal robes. Toiling and spinning were the work of making cloth, often women's work, just as sowing and reaping were men's work in v26.
- **"The grass of the field, which today is alive and tomorrow is thrown into the oven" (v30).** Wood was scarce, so dried grass and weeds were used to fire clay ovens. Life is short (Isa 40:6–8), yet God clothes even this.
- **"O you of little faith" (v30).** *Oligopistos*, "little-faith," is one of Matthew's words for the disciples (8:26; 14:31; 16:8). It is a gentle rebuke. They have faith, but too little. Worry is a failure to trust the Father's care.
- **"For the Gentiles seek after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them all" (v32).** People who do not know God as Father naturally pursue food and clothing as their highest goal. Disciples have a Father who already knows. Jesus does not say these needs are unimportant. He says God knows them.
- **"But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (v33).** This is the heart of the chapter. "Seek" is ongoing: keep on seeking. "First" means priority, not merely first in time. "His righteousness" is the life of obedience described in the sermon (5:6, 20). "All these things" are food, drink and clothing. Jesus does not promise wealth. He promises the Father's provision of what is needed. This is the "daily bread" of v11, given to those whose first prayer is "your kingdom come."
- **"Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself" (v34).** Tomorrow will bring its own concerns, and God's grace for them will come with it. Worrying about tomorrow does not lessen tomorrow's trouble. It only adds it to today's. "Sufficient for the day is its own trouble" is a plain, almost proverbial ending. Lamentations says God's mercies "are new every morning" (Lam 3:22–23).
- **Commentators:** JFB renders "take no thought" as "Be not solicitous," distinguishing anxious care from reasonable forethought. He takes the cubit as a step added to life's journey rather than to height, and calls "Sufficient unto the day" an admirable practical maxim: worrying ahead only doubles the load. Henry says "Thoughtfulness for our souls is the best cure of thoughtfulness for the world," and urges believers to "Seek first the kingdom of God, and make religion your business." Guzik stresses that a ruling passion can only be replaced by a greater one, which is why Jesus does not just say "stop worrying" but "seek first the kingdom."

#### Pointing to Christ
Jesus lived what he taught here. He prayed alone in the hills before dawn. He gave himself without seeking human praise, and when crowds wanted to make him king, he withdrew. He fasted forty days and told no one but his Father. He had nowhere to lay his head (8:20), yet he never worried, because he trusted his Father completely. In Gethsemane he prayed the prayer he gave his disciples: "your will be done." And at the cross he paid the debt that could not be repaid, so that those who come to the Father through him can pray "forgive us our debts," and know they are heard. Because of him, disciples can call God "our Father."
