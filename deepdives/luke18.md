*Setting:* About AD 30, the last stretch of the journey to Jerusalem, heading south through the Jordan valley toward Jericho with Passover pilgrims. Chapter 17 ended with Jesus warning that the Son of Man's day will come suddenly, after a long wait in which disciples will long for him and not see him. Chapter 18 opens with how to live through that wait: keep praying. Two parables about prayer follow, then two scenes that show who receives the kingdom (little children) and who finds it hardest (the rich). Jesus foretells his death for the last time before Jerusalem, and the chapter ends near Jericho, about eighteen miles from the city, with a blind beggar calling him the Son of David.

#### The Parable of the Persistent Widow (vv1–8)
- **"That they ought always to pray and not lose heart" (v1).** Luke states the point before the story, which is rare. "Lose heart" means to give up, grow weary, or go slack. The setting is the long wait of 17:22–37. "Always" does not mean praying every waking minute but never giving up on prayer.
- **"A judge who neither feared God nor respected man" (v2).** A good judge was to fear God and deal fairly with people (2 Chr 19:6–7). This one had neither restraint. He was not afraid of God's judgment or of public opinion. Local judges in that world could often be swayed by bribes or influence.
- **"A widow... kept coming to him" (v3).** In Scripture the widow is the picture of helplessness: no husband to argue for her, often no money, no standing. God repeatedly calls himself the defender of widows (Exod 22:22–23; Deut 10:18; Ps 68:5). "Kept coming" means again and again. She has only one weapon, persistence.
- **"Give me justice against my adversary" (v3).** She is not asking for a favor or for revenge, but for a fair ruling in a case she should win, perhaps a dispute over her husband's estate.
- **"So that she will not beat me down" (v5).** The verb *hypōpiazō* literally means "to strike under the eye," to give a black eye, a boxing term. Paul uses it of disciplining his own body (1 Cor 9:27). The judge may be joking, or he may really be afraid of a public scene, but either way he gives in to make her go away.
- **"Hear what the unrighteous judge says" (v6).** This is a "how much more" argument. If a corrupt judge who cares for no one gives in to a stranger's persistence, how much more will God, who is just and loves his people, answer them.
- **"Will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night?" (v7).** The contrast is complete. The widow was a nobody to the judge. God's people are "his elect," chosen and loved. She had to wear him down. God is not worn down. He is waiting for the right time.
- **"Will he delay long over them?" (v7).** This line is hard to translate. It can mean "Will he keep putting them off?" or "Is he patient with them?" The idea of God's patience appears in 2 Peter 3:9, where his delay in judgment is mercy giving people time to repent.
- **"He will give justice to them speedily" (v8).** "Speedily" can mean "soon" or "suddenly." From the human side the wait can feel long (Rev 6:10, "How long, O Lord?"). When God acts, he will act quickly and decisively.
- **"When the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?" (v8).** In Greek it is "*the* faith," the kind of persevering trust the widow showed. The question turns back on the listeners. The issue is not whether God will be faithful, but whether his people will still be praying when he comes.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the parable first of the church in her "widowed, desolate, oppressed, defenseless condition" while her Lord is away, but adds that its application to prayer in general is "so precious that one cannot allow it to disappear." Henry: "All God's people are praying people," and earnest prayer, unlike the widow's, does not irritate God but pleases him. Guzik notes the boxing image behind the judge's complaint in v5 and argues that delays in answered prayer are usually not God being reluctant but God doing a work in the one who prays. The lexicon confirms the black-eye meaning.

#### The Pharisee and the Tax Collector (vv9–14)
- **"Some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and treated others with contempt" (v9).** Again the point comes first. Trusting in oneself and despising others go together. "Treated with contempt" is the word used of Herod's soldiers mocking Jesus (23:11).
- **"Two men went up into the temple to pray" (v10).** The temple stood on a hill, so people "went up" to it and "went down" home. Set times of public prayer came with the morning and afternoon sacrifices, when worshipers gathered in the courts. The two men may have been there at the same service. A Pharisee was the model of devotion. A tax collector was a collaborator and a cheat.
- **"Standing by himself, prayed thus" (v11).** The Greek can mean he stood apart from others, to avoid contact with the unclean, or that he "prayed to himself," and both fit. Standing was the normal posture for prayer (Mark 11:25).
- **"God, I thank you that I am not like other men" (v11).** He begins correctly, with thanks, but everything after is about himself. "I" appears again and again. He measures himself against the worst people he can think of: swindlers, cheats, adulterers, "or even like this tax collector." A later Jewish prayer thanks God for not making the speaker one of those who sit on street corners, which shows this attitude was real, not a caricature.
- **"I fast twice a week; I give tithes of all that I get" (v12).** The law required fasting only on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29–31). Devout Pharisees fasted Mondays and Thursdays. The law required tithes of crops and livestock. He tithes everything he acquires, even what the law did not require, perhaps including things bought from others in case they had not been tithed. He is going well beyond the law. He asks God for nothing because he does not think he needs anything.
- **"The tax collector, standing far off" (v13).** Probably at the edge of the court, as far from the holy place as he could stand. "Would not even lift up his eyes to heaven" was the normal posture for prayer, but he feels too ashamed (Ezra 9:6). "Beat his breast," and the verb shows he kept doing it, was a gesture of grief, used again of the crowds leaving the cross (23:48).
- **"God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (v13).** In Greek it is "the sinner," as if he were the only one. The verb is *hilaskomai*, which the lexicon gives here as "be propitious, be merciful." Its only other New Testament use, in Hebrews 2:17, means "to make propitiation," to deal with sin by sacrifice. Some see a hint that he is praying with the sacrifice of the temple service in view, asking God to accept that atonement for him. It is a reasonable reading, but the words themselves simply ask for mercy.
- **"This man went down to his house justified, rather than the other" (v14).** "Justified" means declared right with God. It is the language Paul will build his whole letter to the Romans on (Rom 3:24–28; 4:5). The man with nothing to offer goes home right with God. The man with a long list goes home as he came. This would have shocked the first hearers.
- **"Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled" (v14).** Jesus said the same at the Pharisee's dinner (14:11). It runs through Scripture from Hannah's song to Mary's (1 Sam 2:7–8; Luke 1:52) and is repeated by James and Peter (Jas 4:6; 1 Pet 5:5).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the Pharisee was probably "free from gross and scandalous sins," and "all this was very well and commendable," yet "he was full of himself and his own goodness." On the tax collector: "His prayer was short, but to the purpose," and "the self-condemned, and not the self-righteous, are justified before God." JFB says the tax collector stood far off "as unworthy to draw near; but that was the way to get near" (Ps 34:18). On *hilaskomai*, JFB says an allusion to sacrifice is possible "though not likely." Guzik, by contrast, makes more of the link to atonement and Hebrews 2:17. The lexicon supports the link in vocabulary but gives "be merciful" as the meaning here, which favors JFB's caution.

#### Let the Children Come to Me (vv15–17)
- **"They were bringing even infants to him" (v15).** Luke alone uses the word *brephos*, "babies," where Matthew and Mark say "children" (Matt 19:13; Mark 10:13). Parents brought children to respected rabbis for a blessing. "Even infants" shows Luke's point: these could do nothing for themselves at all.
- **"The disciples... rebuked them" (v15).** Children had little status in that culture, and the disciples probably thought babies were a waste of the Teacher's time on the way to important business in Jerusalem. Mark says Jesus was "indignant" (Mark 10:14).
- **"Let the children come to me, and do not hinder them" (v16).** Jesus calls them to himself. The kingdom belongs "to such," to children and those like them.
- **"Whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child shall not enter it" (v17).** Placed right after the tax collector's prayer, the point is clear. A baby brings nothing and earns nothing. The kingdom can only be received as a gift. The proud Pharisee and the rich ruler about to appear both try to come another way.
- **Readings differ on baptism.** JFB, like many in the Reformed and other paedobaptist traditions, sees in Jesus' blessing of infants support for bringing children into the covenant community through baptism. Baptist and many evangelical readers note that the passage says nothing about baptism and stresses childlike trust. Both agree that Jesus welcomes children and that no one should keep them from him.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the disciples thought "infants were not capable of receiving anything from Him," and that this mistake is what displeased Jesus. Henry: "None are too little, too young, to be brought to Christ," and we must receive his kingdom "as children, not by purchase." Guzik says receiving the kingdom like a child means taking a blessing without trying to earn it.

#### The Rich Ruler (vv18–30)
- **"A ruler asked him" (v18).** "Ruler" probably means a synagogue leader or member of a local council. Matthew says he was young (Matt 19:20), and all three Gospels say he was rich. Mark says he ran up and knelt (Mark 10:17).
- **"Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" (v18).** A lawyer asked the same question in 10:25. "What must I do" assumes eternal life is earned by doing the right thing. Coming right after "receive the kingdom like a child," the contrast is sharp.
- **"Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (v19).** Jesus is not denying he is good or that he is God. He makes the man stop and think. Calling a teacher "good" was rare and flattering. If only God is good, then either the man is using the word too lightly, or he is closer to the truth about Jesus than he knows.
- **"You know the commandments" (v20).** Jesus lists commandments from the second half of the Ten Commandments, those about how people treat each other: adultery, murder, theft, false witness and honoring parents (Exod 20:12–16). He leaves out "You shall not covet," the one most likely to catch a rich man.
- **"All these I have kept from my youth" (v21).** He is probably sincere. He has kept them outwardly. But Jesus taught that the commandments reach the heart (Matt 5:21–30).
- **"One thing you still lack" (v22).** Jesus goes straight to the first commandment, "You shall have no other gods before me" (Exod 20:3). The man's wealth is his god. "Sell all that you have and distribute to the poor" is not a rule for every believer. Zacchaeus gives half in the next chapter (19:8) and is told salvation has come to his house. It is the specific surgery this man needs. The call is in the last words: "Come, follow me." Mark adds that Jesus looked at him and "loved him" (Mark 10:21).
- **"He became very sad, for he was extremely rich" (v23).** Matthew and Mark say he went away. Luke leaves him standing there, sad, perhaps listening to what Jesus says next.
- **"Easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle" (v25).** The camel was the largest animal in Palestine, and the needle's eye the smallest opening. The picture is deliberately absurd, an impossibility. The popular idea of a small gate in Jerusalem called "the needle's eye," through which a camel could squeeze on its knees, has no ancient evidence and blunts Jesus' point. A few late manuscripts read *kamilos*, "rope," for *kamēlos*, "camel," which is also a later attempt to soften it.
- **"Then who can be saved?" (v26).** Many Jews saw wealth as a sign of God's favor (Deut 28:1–14). If the rich, apparently blessed by God, cannot enter, who can?
- **"What is impossible with man is possible with God" (v27).** This is the answer to the whole chapter. No one gets in by effort, rich or poor. Salvation is God's work. The very next chapter shows it happening to a rich man in Jericho (19:1–10).
- **"See, we have left our homes and followed you" (v28).** Peter speaks for the group, and Matthew adds, "What then will we have?" (Matt 19:27). It is an honest question.
- **"Many times more in this time, and in the age to come eternal life" (vv29–30).** Mark adds "with persecutions" (Mark 10:30). Jesus promises a new family of brothers, sisters and homes in the church in this life, and eternal life after it. No one gives up more than God gives back.
- **Commentators:** JFB argues that Jesus' question in v19 can only mean he wants "to raise the youth's ideas of Himself," and that it gives no support to those who deny Christ's deity. Henry: "Many have a great deal in them very commendable, yet perish for lack of some one thing." JFB on v22: since riches were his idol, Jesus "lays His great authoritative grasp at once upon it," and gives no general command about wealth except that we must "sit loose to" it. Guzik rejects the needle-gate theory as softening a deliberately humorous impossibility and reads the man's wealth as an idol that broke the first table of the law. On Peter, Henry remarks that "men are apt to speak too much of what they have left and lost." JFB is kinder, seeing Peter speaking "in the simplicity of his heart."

#### Jesus Foretells His Death a Third Time (vv31–34)
- **"Taking the twelve" (v31).** He draws them aside from the crowds of pilgrims. "We are going up to Jerusalem" is literal, since Jerusalem is about 2,500 feet above sea level and Jericho about 800 feet below. The road climbs over 3,000 feet in eighteen miles.
- **"Everything that is written about the Son of Man by the prophets will be accomplished" (v31).** Only Luke records this line here. It anticipates the risen Jesus explaining "in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (24:25–27, 44–46). The suffering servant of Isaiah 52:13–53:12 stands behind it.
- **"Delivered over to the Gentiles... mocked... spit upon... flogging" (vv32–33).** This is the most detailed of the predictions (9:22, 44; 17:25), and the first to name the Gentiles, meaning the Romans. Isaiah 50:6 foretold the servant's back given to those who strike and his face not hidden "from disgrace and spitting." Each detail happens in chapters 22–23.
- **"On the third day he will rise" (v33).** The prediction never ends with death.
- **"But they understood none of these things" (v34).** Luke says it three ways: they understood nothing, it was hidden from them, they did not grasp it. They expected a Messiah who would reign, not one who would be killed. Only after the resurrection would their eyes be opened (24:31, 45).
- **Commentators:** JFB says v31 shows "how Christ Himself read, and would have us to read, the Old Testament." He also notes that Luke stresses the disciples' failure to understand so strongly perhaps to strengthen their later testimony. Henry: "People run into mistakes, because they read their Bibles by halves, and are only for the smooth things." Guzik suggests God hid the meaning because the disciples could not yet bear it.

#### Jesus Heals a Blind Beggar (vv35–43)
- **"As he drew near to Jericho" (v35).** Jericho was an oasis city in the Jordan valley, one of the oldest cities on earth, with Herod's winter palace nearby and wealthy residents. It was the last stop for pilgrims before the climb to Jerusalem, so a beggar could count on crowds.
- **Three accounts.** Mark names him Bartimaeus and places it as Jesus left Jericho (Mark 10:46). Matthew has two blind men, also leaving (Matt 20:29–30). Luke places it as Jesus approached. Common suggestions are that there were two Jerichos, the old city on its mound and Herod's new city about a mile away, so Jesus was leaving one and approaching the other, or that the man called out as Jesus arrived and was healed as he left. Matthew may mention two where Mark and Luke focus on the one who was better known. The differences are honest witnesses telling the same event in their own ways.
- **"Jesus of Nazareth is passing by" (v37).** The crowd gives him the ordinary name. The blind man uses another.
- **"Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (v38).** "Son of David" was a title for the Messiah, the king God promised from David's line (2 Sam 7:12–16; Isa 11:1). It is the first time anyone uses it of Jesus in Luke, just before he enters Jerusalem as king (19:38). The blind man sees what others miss.
- **"Those who were in front rebuked him" (v39).** The same verb used of the disciples rebuking the parents (v15). Once again, the people closest to Jesus try to keep the needy away. Once again, Jesus overrules them.
- **"Jesus stopped" (v40).** On the most important journey of his life, with the cross ahead of him, he stops for one blind beggar.
- **"What do you want me to do for you?" (v41).** It seems obvious, but the question invites the man to say his need and his faith out loud. He answers with "Lord." The ruler called Jesus "Good Teacher" and went away sad. The beggar calls him "Lord" and receives what he asks.
- **"Your faith has made you well" (v42).** The same words spoken to the Samaritan leper (17:19). The verb *sōzō* means both "heal" and "save."
- **"He recovered his sight and followed him, glorifying God" (v43).** He does exactly what the rich ruler would not do: he follows. He follows Jesus up the road to Jerusalem. "All the people... gave praise to God." The whole scene points ahead to the crowds' praise at the triumphal entry.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the three accounts' differences and concludes they show there "could have been no collusion among the authors," since collaborators would have removed these "spots on the sun." Henry calls the man "not only blind, but poor, the fitter emblem of the world of mankind which Christ came to heal and save." Guzik sees saving faith in the beggar: he seeks Jesus, knows who he is, knows his own need and calls him Lord.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter is full of people who bring nothing: a widow with no power, a tax collector with no record, babies with no words, a blind beggar with no sight. They are the ones who receive. Between them stands a rich ruler with everything except the one thing he needed, and in the middle of the chapter, Jesus going up to Jerusalem to be mocked, spat on and killed. The tax collector asked God to be merciful, and the cross is how God answers that prayer. What is impossible with man, God accomplishes there.
