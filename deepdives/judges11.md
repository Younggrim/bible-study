*Setting:* The Ammonites have crushed the Israelite tribes east of the Jordan for eighteen years (10:8). Israel has put away its foreign gods, and the LORD "became impatient over the misery of Israel" (10:16). The two armies are camped in Gilead, and Gilead's leaders have promised to make whoever leads the fight "head over all the inhabitants of Gilead" (10:18). They did not ask God whom to choose. Chapter 11 gives the answer they find: an outcast son of a prostitute who turns out to be a brave fighter and a sharp negotiator, and who wins a great victory, then loses his only child to a vow he never should have made. Gilead is the hill country east of the Jordan, between the Arnon River in the south and the Yarmuk in the north, cut through the middle by the Jabbok.

#### Jephthah Delivers Israel (vv1–28)
- **"A mighty warrior, but he was the son of a prostitute" (v1).** "Mighty warrior" is the same phrase the angel used of Gideon (6:12). Jephthah has real ability and a stain he cannot wash off. His name means "he opens," and it will matter later in the chapter.
- **"You shall not have an inheritance in our father's house" (v2).** His half-brothers drive him out to keep the family land for themselves. The law did not give a prostitute's son an assured place in the family, so they may have been within their rights, but it was hard and selfish. Jephthah's story starts like Abimelech's (another son of "another woman," 8:31), but runs the other way: Abimelech killed his brothers, while Jephthah's brothers threw him out.
- **"The land of Tob" (v3).** A region north and east of Gilead, toward Syria. Its men later fight for Ammon against David (2 Sam 10:6). **"Worthless fellows collected around Jephthah"** means empty men, men with nothing, the same phrase used of Abimelech's hired gang (9:4). The difference is that Jephthah did not buy them. Like David in his exile (1 Sam 22:2), he became leader of a band of outsiders, probably living by raiding or by protecting settlements for pay.
- **"Did you not hate me and drive me out…? Why have you come to me now when you are in distress?" (v7).** The elders who once let him be thrown out now need him. Jephthah's question is fair, and it echoes God's own words to Israel only a few verses back: "Go and cry out to the gods whom you have chosen" (10:14). Both God and Jephthah were rejected and then begged for help.
- **Hard bargaining (vv6–11).** The elders first offer only to make him "leader" (*qatsin*, a military commander). He pushes back, and they raise the offer to "head" over all Gilead, the full civil ruler they promised in 10:18. Jephthah makes sure: "If… the LORD gives them over to me, I will be your head" (v9). Notice he speaks of the LORD giving the victory, not of himself winning it.
- **"Jephthah spoke all his words before the LORD at Mizpah" (v11).** The agreement is sealed with an oath before God at the shrine at Mizpah in Gilead. The Tyndale notes compare it to a formal covenant between a ruler and a people.
- **Diplomacy first (vv12–13).** Before he fights, Jephthah sends messengers, as the law directed (Deut 20:10). The Ammonite king's claim: when Israel came out of Egypt, it stole his land "from the Arnon to the Jabbok and to the Jordan." Give it back peacefully.
- **Jephthah's history lesson (vv14–22).** His reply is the longest speech of a judge in the book, and it closely follows Numbers 20–21. Israel asked Edom and Moab for passage and was refused, so it went the long way around them and never touched their land (vv17–18; Num 20:14–21; 21:13). It asked Sihon the Amorite king for passage. Sihon attacked instead, and the LORD gave his land to Israel (vv19–22; Num 21:21–25). Point one: the land was never Ammon's. It was the Amorites', and Sihon had earlier taken part of it from Moab (Num 21:26). God had told Israel not to touch Ammon's land at all (Deut 2:19, 37), and Israel obeyed.
- **"Will you not possess what Chemosh your god gives you to possess?" (v24).** Point two: each people holds what its god gives it, and the LORD gave this land to Israel. Chemosh was actually the god of Moab (1 Kgs 11:33), while Ammon's god was Milcom or Molech. Jephthah may be treating the claim as really Moab's, since the disputed land had been Moabite before Sihon, or Ammon may have honored Chemosh as well. He is arguing on the king's own terms, not saying Chemosh is real.
- **"Are you any better than Balak…?" (v25).** Point three: Balak, king of Moab in Moses' day, the one with the strongest claim, never went to war over this land. He hired Balaam to curse Israel instead (Num 22–24), and that failed.
- **"300 years" (v26).** Point four: Israel has held these towns for three centuries, and no one objected until now. The number is probably a round figure, but it is one of the clues scholars use in dating the judges period. Those who date the exodus to about 1446 BC find it fits well. Those who prefer a later date read it as an overstatement in a speech.
- **"The LORD, the Judge, decide this day" (v27).** In a book named for Israel's judges, this is the only time the noun is used as a title for God. The real Judge of Judges is the LORD. The king does not listen (v28).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "Men ought not to be blamed for their parentage, so long as they by their personal merits roll away any reproach." He sees Jephthah's willingness to forgive the elders as a reflection of God's forgiving Israel: "God had forgiven Israel, therefore Jephthah will forgive." On the embassy, "Those who possess the most courageous faith, will be the most disposed for peace." JFB says the name Jephthah means "opener," calls his first reply to the elders a "haughty and cold reception," and praises his care to have a binding agreement. JFB lays out Jephthah's case as three arguments: conquest from the Amorites, 300 years of possession, and God's grant. Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's view that the "worthless fellows" were poor men without work, not bandits, and says Jephthah saw the dispute as first of all a contest between the LORD and false gods. The Tyndale notes point out that the elders tried to give him less than they had promised and that he held them to their word.

#### Jephthah's Tragic Vow (vv29–40)
- **"Then the Spirit of the LORD was upon Jephthah" (v29).** God's Spirit came on him to lead Israel, as with Othniel (3:10) and Gideon (6:34). He rallies men from Gilead and Manasseh. This is God's answer to the "decide this day" of v27.
- **The vow (vv30–31).** Then Jephthah adds something God had not asked for: "If you will give the Ammonites into my hand, then whatever comes out from the doors of my house to meet me… shall be the LORD's, and I will offer it up for a burnt offering." Vows were common and could be good (Gen 28:20–22; 1 Sam 1:11), but this one tries to buy a victory God had already signaled by his Spirit. The ESV footnote says "whatever" can also be "whoever." Houses in Israel often kept animals in the ground floor, so an animal could have come out. But the phrase "comes out… to meet me" is the language of a person, and a man returning from war knew who usually ran out to greet him.
- **"The LORD gave them into his hand" (vv32–33).** The text credits the victory to the LORD, not to the vow. The Tyndale notes are clear that there is no link between the two. Jephthah strikes "twenty cities" from Aroer to Abel-keramim ("meadow of vineyards"), a long sweep through Ammon's land.
- **"His daughter came out to meet him with tambourines and with dances" (v34).** Women met victorious armies with music and dancing, as Miriam did at the sea (Exod 15:20) and the women did for Saul and David (1 Sam 18:6). And then the narrator slows to let the blow land: "She was his only child; besides her he had neither son nor daughter." The phrase recalls Abraham's "only son" Isaac (Gen 22:2). But in that story God stopped the knife and provided a ram. Here no voice from heaven comes.
- **"Alas, my daughter! You have brought me very low" (v35).** Jephthah tears his clothes in grief, but he speaks as if she has done this to him. "I have opened my mouth to the LORD, and I cannot take back my vow." The man whose name means "he opens" opened his mouth rashly. The law took vows very seriously (Num 30:2; Deut 23:21–23; Eccl 5:4–6). But it also provided a way to redeem a person vowed to God with money (Lev 27:1–8), and it called child sacrifice an abomination the LORD hates (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31; 18:10). Jephthah, who knew Numbers 21 well enough to argue it with a king, did not apply the law where it mattered most.
- **"Do to me according to what has gone out of your mouth" (v36).** The daughter, unnamed, shows more faith and courage than anyone else in the scene. She thinks of the LORD's victory before herself.
- **"Weep for my virginity" (vv37–38).** She asks for two months in the hills with her friends to mourn that she will never marry or have children. In Israel to die childless, with no one to carry on one's name, was a deep sorrow, and for Jephthah it meant his line would end with her.
- **"Who did with her according to his vow" (v39).** The narrator says it quietly and does not describe it. What did Jephthah do? Faithful Christians have read it two ways.
- **View one: he offered her as a sacrifice.** This is the plain reading of "I will offer it up for a burnt offering" and "did with her according to his vow." It was the view of the Jewish historian Josephus and most early church fathers, and it is held by JFB, the Tyndale notes and most modern scholars. On this reading the story shows how far Israel had sunk, so that even a Spirit-empowered judge did what the Canaanites did.
- **View two: he dedicated her to lifelong service and she never married.** Some medieval Jewish scholars and many later Christian writers take this view. They argue the Hebrew "and" in v31 can mean "or" (it will be the LORD's, *or* I will offer it), that women served at the tabernacle door (Exod 38:8; 1 Sam 2:22), that the emphasis on her virginity, not her death, suggests a life without marriage, and that Hebrews 11:32 names Jephthah among the faithful.
- **Weighing them.** Most Hebrew scholars think the "or" reading strains the grammar, and the sorrow of the scene (torn clothes, lament, a yearly mourning) fits a death more naturally. Either way, the narrator does not approve of the vow, and God never asked for it.
- **"The daughters of Israel went year by year to lament the daughter of Jephthah" (v40).** The verb is rare. In 5:11 it means to "repeat" or recount the LORD's deeds, so it may mean the young women went to retell her story. No other record of this custom survives. She has no name in the text, but Israel's daughters would not let her be forgotten.
- **Commentators:** On the vow, Henry warns, "Our vows to God should not be as a purchase of the favour we desire," and "What we have solemnly vowed to God, we must perform, if it be possible and lawful." He thinks a sacrifice "would have been an abomination to the Lord," so he supposes she was kept unmarried and apart from her family, though he admits it is "hard to say." Guzik leans the same way, favoring service at the tabernacle, citing Leviticus 27 and Hebrews 11:32, and he adds that being filled with the Spirit does not keep a person from foolish choices. JFB takes the opposite view: the vow "evidently points not to an animal," and "there is but too much reason to conclude" that Jephthah sacrificed her from "a pious but unenlightened conscience." The Tyndale notes agree with JFB and say there is no evidence she was merely kept unmarried.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jephthah's story is full of echoes of the gospel turned upside down. A rejected son, driven out by his brothers, becomes their deliverer, which is the shape of Joseph's story and of Jesus' (Ps 118:22). But Jephthah tried to bargain with God for a victory God was already giving, and a father's only child paid for his rash words. In the gospel the order is reversed. No one has to bargain, because God gives the victory freely. And it is God the Father who gives his "only Son" (John 3:16), not to a careless vow but by a plan of love, and the Son goes willingly. On Moriah God provided a ram in Isaac's place. At Jephthah's house there was no substitute. At the cross the Substitute himself was offered, once for all, so that no child of God is ever required to die in payment for a debt.
