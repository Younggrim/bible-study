*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to The Dove on Far-off Terebinths. A Miktam of David, when the Philistines seized him in Gath." The title takes us back to an early, humiliating moment in David's flight from Saul, told in 1 Samuel 21:10–15. After leaving Nob (the background of Psalm 52), David ran to the last place anyone would look for him: Gath, one of the five Philistine cities, the hometown of Goliath, whose sword David was carrying. He hoped to hide among Israel's enemies. He was recognized at once. The servants of Achish, king of Gath, said, "Is not this David the king of the land? Did they not sing to one another of him in dances, 'Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands'?" And David "took these words to heart and was much afraid" (21:11–12). To survive, he pretended to be insane, scratching on the doors of the city gate and letting spit run down his beard, until Achish said, "Do I lack madmen?" and let him go (21:13–15). Psalm 34 is also tied to this escape and is its thanksgiving; Psalm 56 is the prayer from inside the danger. The phrase "seized him" suggests he was held for a time, which 1 Samuel does not spell out. The psalm has a refrain of trust (vv4, 10–11) that divides it into two movements, each going from fear to confidence, as the Tyndale notes observe.

#### The Title
- **"The Dove on Far-off Terebinths" (title).** Probably the name of a tune. The Hebrew is *yonat 'elem rechoqim*. With one set of vowels it means "the silent dove of far-off places" (so JFB, and the King James transliteration *Jonath-elem-rechokim*). With another it means "the dove of the distant terebinths" (oaks), as the ESV has it. Either way, it is a lovely fit. The previous psalm sighed, "Oh, that I had wings like a dove!" (55:6). Here David is the dove: far from home, silent, in a foreign land. JFB suggests the title may be a veiled picture of David himself as an exiled, meek dove.
- **"A Miktam" (title).** The ESV footnote calls it "probably a musical or liturgical term." Six psalms carry this title (16, 56–60). Its meaning is uncertain. Older interpreters tied it to the word for gold (*ketem*), a "golden" psalm, which Guzik favors. The Greek Old Testament rendered it "for an inscription on a stone pillar," which may mean a psalm worth engraving and keeping. All of the Miktam psalms come from danger and end in confidence.

#### When I Am Afraid (vv1–4)
- **"Be gracious to me, O God, for man tramples on me" (v1).** The same opening as 51:1 and 57:1, "Be gracious to me." "Man" here is *'enosh*, a word that stresses human frailty, mortal man. "Tramples" (*sha'aph*) can also mean "pants after," "snaps at," like an animal chasing its prey (JFB: "pants as a raging beast"). David feels hunted.
- **"All day long an attacker oppresses me" (v1).** "All day long" comes three times in the first five verses (vv1, 2, 5). Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the relentless pressure. There is no break.
- **"My enemies trample on me all day long, for many attack me proudly" (v2).** "Enemies" is again "those who watch me," as in 54:5 (JFB: "watchers"). "Proudly" (*marom*) normally means "on high." The King James took it as a name for God, "O thou most High." Most modern translations, and JFB, take it with the enemies: they attack from a height, arrogantly. Clarke, cited by Guzik, offers another view, that it refers to a high place.
- **"When I am afraid, I put my trust in you" (v3).** One of the most honest lines in the Bible. David does not say, "I am never afraid." He was afraid. 1 Samuel says so plainly: he was "much afraid of Achish the king of Gath" (21:12). Literally, "in the day I fear, I will trust in you." The verb "trust" (*batach*) means to lean on, to rely on. Fear and faith exist at the same moment. Faith does not wait for fear to leave; it acts while afraid. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, makes the point that fear and faith may both be in the mind at once. Henry: "There are some times which are, in a special manner, times of fear with God's people," and trust in God "will fix the heart and keep it in peace."
- **"In God, whose word I praise" (v4).** David's trust rests on something: God's word. JFB offers two readings: praising with God's help, or boasting in God about his word. Either way, God's word is the focus. Boice, cited by Guzik, suggests David may have been thinking of the specific promise that he would be king (1 Sam 16:1, 13). God had spoken. David could not be killed in Gath if God's word was true.
- **"In God I trust; I shall not be afraid" (v4).** Notice the progress from v3. "When I am afraid, I trust" becomes "I trust; I shall not be afraid." Trust starts in fear and ends by driving fear out. Morgan, cited by Guzik, traces this step.
- **"What can flesh do to me?" (v4).** "Flesh" (*basar*) is the second word for humanity in the psalm. It stands for human beings as weak and mortal, in contrast to God (Isa 31:3: "The Egyptians are man, and not God, and their horses are flesh, and not spirit"). JFB: "intimating frailty." Of course flesh could do a great deal to David. It could kill him. But it could not undo God's word or touch his soul. Henry: it "can do nothing but by divine permission."
- **Commentators:** Henry, Guzik and JFB all stress that David's faith is anchored in God's word. Guzik quotes Spurgeon: the open mouths of sinners raging against us should open our mouths in prayer. JFB reads v3's "in" as "unto": God is the one "to whom he turns in trouble."

#### You Have Kept Count of My Tossings (vv5–11)
- **"All day long they injure my cause" (v5).** The ESV footnote gives the other reading: "they twist my words." The verb means to hurt or to labor painfully. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, says it describes hard, wearying work. They work hard at distorting everything David says. In Gath, his very fame, the song about his ten thousands, was turned against him.
- **"All their thoughts are against me for evil" (v5).** They plan nothing else.
- **"They stir up strife, they lurk; they watch my steps" (v6).** "Steps" is literally "heels" (*'aqev*), the word from 49:5 and Jacob's name. They follow on his heels, waiting for a slip. "Lurk" means hide in ambush.
- **"As they have waited for my life" (v6).** They wait the way a hunter waits for game.
- **"For their crime will they escape?" (v7).** The Hebrew is very compressed, literally "on account of iniquity, escape for them?" It can be read as a question ("shall they escape by iniquity?"), as the ESV does, or as JFB's alternative, "Their escape is by iniquity." Either way, David asks God not to let wickedness get away with it.
- **"In wrath cast down the peoples, O God!" (v7).** "Peoples" widens the prayer to the nations. David is among the Philistines, and his enemies at home and abroad are many. He asks the God of all the earth to act (compare 47:2–3).
- **"You have kept count of my tossings" (v8).** The tone suddenly turns tender. The ESV footnote gives "wanderings." David was a wanderer, a fugitive moving from place to place, Nob to Gath to Adullam to the wilderness. God has numbered every step. "Kept count" means recorded, like a ledger.
- **"Put my tears in your bottle" (v8).** The Tyndale notes point out a wordplay: "wanderings" is *nod*, and "bottle" is *no'd*, a skin bottle, a leather flask for water, milk or wine (Josh 9:4; 1 Sam 16:20). The ancient Hebrew reader would hear the echo: God, you have counted my *nod*; now put my tears in your *no'd*. Every tear David shed in flight was precious enough to be kept. Some older commentators, like Clarke and JFB, thought of the small glass "tear bottles" found in Roman tombs. Spurgeon doubted David had any such custom in mind, and most scholars now think those little bottles held perfume, not tears. The picture works without it. God does not let a single tear fall unnoticed.
- **"Are they not in your book?" (v8).** God keeps a record. The Bible speaks of God's book in several ways: the book of life (Exod 32:32; Rev 20:12), the book of days (139:16), and a "book of remembrance" for those who fear the LORD (Mal 3:16). Here it is the book of David's sorrows. Nothing he has suffered is forgotten.
- **"Then my enemies will turn back in the day when I call" (v9).** "In the day when I call" matches "in the day I fear" (v3). His day of fear becomes his day of calling, and that becomes the day his enemies turn back.
- **"This I know, that God is for me" (v9).** The footnote gives "because God is for me." This is the center of the psalm. David does not know how he will get out of Gath. He knows one thing: God is on his side (JFB: "on my side"). Paul builds his great climax on the same thought: "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31). Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes the echo.
- **"In God, whose word I praise, in the LORD, whose word I praise" (v10).** The refrain returns, expanded. This time David adds God's covenant name, "the LORD" (*YHWH*), as if to say: not just God in general, but the God who made promises to Israel and to me. JFB gives no separate note on vv10–11.
- **"In God I trust; I shall not be afraid. What can man do to me?" (v11).** "Man" is now *'adam*, the third word for humanity in the psalm. The Tyndale notes observe that all three (*'enosh*, *basar*, *'adam*) stress mortality and limits. Mere humans against the living God. Psalm 118:6 says the same thing, and Hebrews quotes it: "So we can confidently say, 'The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?'" (Heb 13:6).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God has a bottle and a book for his people's tears, both those for their sins and those for their afflictions," and "What was sown a tear will come up a pearl." Guzik reads the bottle as God's tender, detailed awareness of pain, and passes on Trapp's story that Luther took the same courage before emperor and pope. JFB says God is "mindful of his exile," and reads "God is for me" as God being "on my side."

#### You Have Delivered My Soul from Death (vv12–13)
- **"I must perform my vows to you, O God" (v12).** Literally "upon me, O God, are your vows," the vows I made to you are binding on me. In trouble David had made promises. Now he must keep them (compare 50:14; 66:13–14). Henry: "our occasional vows under convictions, under corrections, by these we are bound to live to God."
- **"I will render thank offerings to you" (v12).** "Thank offerings" is *todot*, plural of *todah*, the thank offering of Leviticus 7:12 and Psalm 50:14. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes it can mean real sacrifices or songs of thanks. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, points out that David vows thanks before he is out of danger.
- **"For you have delivered my soul from death" (v13).** Spoken as though already done (compare 52:9; 54:7). Some think the last verses were added after the escape from Gath. Either way, the psalm ends in rescue.
- **"Yes, my feet from falling" (v13).** JFB: "as from a precipice." In Gath David had been one stumble away from death. God kept his feet.
- **"That I may walk before God in the light of life" (v13).** The purpose of rescue is not just to stay alive but to live in God's presence. To "walk before God" is what God asked of Abraham: "walk before me, and be blameless" (Gen 17:1). JFB: "in His favor during life." "The light of life" (or "of the living") is the opposite of the darkness of death and Sheol (49:19). The Tyndale notes say God's goodness and salvation replace that darkness. Psalm 36:9 says, "in your light do we see light."
- **Commentators:** Guzik, citing Spurgeon, says the psalm moves from the enemy's jaws into God's presence, and with Boice hears Jesus' words in John 8:12. JFB reads v13 as a question implying yes, based on past experience: "Will you not deliver my feet from falling?"

#### Pointing to Christ
David in Gath was the anointed king, hunted, alone, his words twisted, surrounded by enemies who watched his every step. Jesus lived that life more fully. His enemies "watched him" to find a charge against him (Mark 3:2; Luke 20:20), twisted his words at his trial (Matt 26:59–61), and waited for his life. He too was afraid in the garden, "greatly distressed and troubled" (Mark 14:33), and he trusted his Father anyway. He offered up "prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death" (Heb 5:7), and those tears were not lost. Because of him, believers can say with David, "God is for me" (Rom 8:31), and know that their tears are counted too, until the day when "God will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Rev 7:17). And the one who was delivered from death says to his followers, "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life" (John 8:12).
