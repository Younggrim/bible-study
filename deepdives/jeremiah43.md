*Setting:* Late 587 or 586 BC, a few months after Jerusalem fell. After the murder of the governor Gedaliah (ch 41), the remnant of Judah gathered near Bethlehem, afraid of Babylon's revenge and already leaning toward Egypt. They asked Jeremiah to pray and swore to obey whatever the LORD said (42:5–6). After ten days the answer came: stay in the land, and God will build and plant you; go to Egypt, and the sword and famine you fear will follow you there (ch 42). This chapter tells their reply. It is the last time Jeremiah appears in the land of Israel. From here on he speaks in Egypt, an old man taken where he did not want to go, still giving God's word to people who will not hear it.

#### Jeremiah Taken to Egypt (vv1–13)
- **"All these words of the LORD their God, with which the LORD their God had sent him to them" (v1).** The narrator says it twice: the LORD *their* God sent Jeremiah. Whatever they claim next, the book wants no doubt about whose word this was.
- **"Azariah the son of Hoshaiah and Johanan the son of Kareah and all the insolent men" (v2).** "Insolent" (*zedim*) means proud, arrogant, presumptuous. It comes from the same root as the word the law used for the man who acts "presumptuously" against God's word (Deut 17:12–13; 18:22). Azariah is probably the "Jezaniah" of 42:1. He is named first here, and some think he led the push for Egypt.
- **"You are telling a lie" (v2).** The word is *sheqer*, "falsehood," the word Jeremiah used again and again for the false prophets (14:14; 23:25–26; 28:15). Now it is thrown back at him. **"The LORD our God did not send you."** Ten days earlier they called him "the LORD our God" and promised to obey him whether his word was good or bad (42:6). The promise lasted until the answer was one they did not like.
- **"But Baruch the son of Neriah has set you against us" (v3).** Baruch was Jeremiah's scribe and friend (ch 36). The Tyndale notes say no one knows why he was blamed. He may have been known for good relations with the Babylonians, or as the younger man he may have seemed the more likely schemer. Perhaps it was easier to blame the assistant than to say God's prophet was lying. The charge makes little sense. Jeremiah had spent forty years refusing to soften God's word for kings. He was not about to take orders from his secretary.
- **"So Johanan… and all the people did not obey the voice of the LORD, to remain in the land of Judah" (v4).** "Obey the voice of the LORD" was the very phrase of their oath (42:6). The narrator uses it to show them breaking it.
- **"All the remnant of Judah who had returned to live in the land of Judah from all the nations" (v5).** These were the refugees who had come home from Moab, Ammon and Edom when Gedaliah was appointed (40:11–12). They came home to rebuild, and now they are taken away again. **"The men, the women, the children, the princesses" (v6).** The "princesses" (literally "the king's daughters") were probably women of Zedekiah's household (41:10). **"Also Jeremiah the prophet and Baruch the son of Neriah."** Jeremiah did not choose this. Babylon had offered him a comfortable life, and he had chosen to stay in the land (40:4–6). Now his own people force him to leave it. Being taken by force, he is not guilty of disobeying the warning of chapter 42.
- **"For they did not obey the voice of the LORD" (v7).** The third time in four verses. The flight to Egypt is told as disobedience from start to finish.
- **"And they arrived at Tahpanhes" (v7).** Tahpanhes was a frontier fortress on Egypt's northeast border, in the eastern Nile delta, the first major town on the road from Palestine. The Greeks called it Daphnae, and its site is Tell Defenneh. Jeremiah had named it decades earlier, in his first sermons: "the men of Memphis and Tahpanhes have shaved the crown of your head" (2:16). Pharaoh Psammetichus I had stationed Greek mercenaries there in the 600s BC. So Judah's exodus runs backward. The nation that once walked out of Egypt in triumph walks back in, in defeat and fear.
- **"Then the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah in Tahpanhes" (v8).** They have left the land, but they cannot leave God's word behind. The prophet they called a liar is still with them, and God still speaks through him.
- **"Take in your hands large stones and hide them in the mortar in the pavement that is at the entrance to Pharaoh's palace in Tahpanhes" (v9).** This is another of Jeremiah's acted signs, like the ruined loincloth (ch 13), the smashed jar (ch 19) and the yoke (ch 27). The words for "mortar" and "pavement" are rare, and translators differ. The KJV has "the clay in the brickkiln." The idea seems to be a brick terrace or platform at the entrance of an Egyptian government building, since Pharaoh did not normally live on the frontier. "Pharaoh's palace" probably means a royal residence or official building. **"In the sight of the men of Judah."** The sign is for the refugees, not the Egyptians.
- **Petrie's discovery.** In 1883–86 the archaeologist Flinders Petrie excavated Tell Defenneh. He found a large fortress-palace that local people called *Qasr Bint el-Yahudi*, "the palace of the Jew's daughter," and in front of it a broad brick pavement. Petrie believed this was the very platform of v9. The name is striking, given the "king's daughters" of v6, though no one can prove the connection, and many scholars are cautious about the identification.
- **"Behold, I will send and take Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant" (v10).** God has called Nebuchadnezzar "my servant" before (25:9; 27:6). The pagan king does not know it, but he is doing God's work. **"I will set his throne above these stones that I have hidden, and he will spread his royal canopy over them."** The stones Jeremiah hid will become the base of the conqueror's throne. The word for "royal canopy" (*shaphrir*) occurs only here, and its exact meaning is uncertain. Most think it was a carpet or a decorated canopy over the throne where the king sat in judgment. The place the refugees chose for safety will be the place the conqueror sits.
- **"Giving over to the pestilence those who are doomed to the pestilence, to captivity those who are doomed to captivity, and to the sword those who are doomed to the sword" (v11).** This echoes Jeremiah's earlier word on Jerusalem almost exactly (15:2). The judgment the remnant fled from has followed them, just as God warned (42:16).
- **"I shall kindle a fire in the temples of the gods of Egypt" (v12).** The Hebrew reads "I," and the Greek and other versions read "he." Either way God is behind it, and v13 makes Nebuchadnezzar the one who does the burning. In the ancient world a conqueror's god was thought to defeat the gods of the conquered. Here the LORD himself sets fire to Egypt's temples. The refugees have fled to a land whose gods cannot even protect their own houses.
- **"He shall clean the land of Egypt as a shepherd cleans his cloak of vermin" (v12).** The verb (*'atah*) usually means "wrap oneself." The BDB lexicon reads it that way here: Nebuchadnezzar will "wrap himself in the land of Egypt" as easily as a shepherd throws on his cloak. The KJV says "array himself with the land of Egypt, as a shepherd putteth on his garment." Many modern scholars, following a related word, take it to mean "pick clean" or "delouse," which the ESV follows: he will pick Egypt clean as a shepherd picks lice from his cloak. Both pictures make the same point. Conquering Egypt will be easy. **"And he shall go away from there in peace."** He will leave without a fight.
- **"He shall break the obelisks of Heliopolis" (v13).** The Hebrew is "the pillars of Beth-shemesh," "house of the sun." It means On, the Egyptian city the Greeks called Heliopolis, "city of the sun," near modern Cairo, the great center of sun worship. Joseph had married the daughter of a priest of On (Gen 41:45). Its temple was famous for its obelisks, tall stone pillars carved with inscriptions to the sun god. One still stands on the site, and others were carried off in later centuries and now stand in Rome, London and New York.
- **Was it fulfilled?** A broken Babylonian tablet in the British Museum records a campaign by Nebuchadnezzar against Egypt in his 37th year, about 568–567 BC, against Pharaoh Amasis. The text is damaged, and it does not describe a full conquest. Most scholars think it was a punishing raid rather than a lasting occupation. Josephus later wrote that Nebuchadnezzar invaded Egypt about five years after Jerusalem fell and took the Jews there captive to Babylon, but he may be drawing on this very chapter. Ezekiel, prophesying in Babylon, says God gave Egypt to Nebuchadnezzar as wages for his army (Ezek 29:17–20). The prophecy came true in substance, though the details of the campaign are not known.
- **What became of Jeremiah?** Scripture does not say. Chapter 44 is his last recorded message. A Jewish and Christian tradition, recorded in the early *Lives of the Prophets* and repeated by Tertullian and Jerome, holds that the Jews in Egypt stoned him to death at Tahpanhes. Another Jewish tradition says he was taken on to Babylon. Neither can be confirmed. Hebrews 11:37 mentions prophets who "were stoned," and some readers have thought of Jeremiah there.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry traces the whole revolt to pride: "The proud unhumbled heart of man is one of the most daring enemies God has on this side hell." On Baruch he adds, "the best services are no fences against malice and slander." On the move to Egypt: "It is the folly of men that they know not when they are well off." JFB agrees that "Pride is the parent of disobedience," and suggests Baruch was blamed because he was younger and was known to stand well with the Babylonians. Guzik points to commentators who note that a prophet who would not bend for a king would hardly bend for his secretary, and stresses that Jeremiah was not disobedient in going, since he was taken against his will. On v10 Henry says God calls Nebuchadnezzar "his servant, because herein he executed God's will," and both Henry and JFB observe that God "sometimes makes one wicked man, or wicked nation, a scourge and plague to another" (Henry's wording). On v12 JFB and the older English versions read the shepherd image as putting on a garment, quick and easy. Guzik passes on both that reading and the newer "pick clean" one. JFB adds that if even the temples are not spared, private houses will not be either.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah ran back into Egypt to save its life and found that God's word had come with it. The chapter turns the exodus backward. Centuries later another family went down to Egypt to escape a murderous king, and Matthew sees in that journey the story of Israel itself, God's Son going down to Egypt and being called out again: "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Matt 2:15; Hos 11:1). Where the remnant fled into Egypt in unbelief, Jesus went there as a child and came back out in perfect obedience. He relived Israel's story and got it right, so that the exodus the remnant abandoned is accomplished for his people in him (Luke 9:31, where his "departure" is literally his "exodus").
