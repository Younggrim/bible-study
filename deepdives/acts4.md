*Setting:* Jerusalem, about AD 30, the same afternoon as chapter 3. A man lame for forty years has just walked into the temple, and Peter is preaching to the crowd in Solomon's Portico about the Jesus they crucified. The temple leaders, who had Jesus executed only weeks before, now face his followers in their own courts. This is the church's first brush with persecution. Luke shows how the apostles answer the authorities, how the church prays when threatened, and what life together looked like in those early days.

#### Peter and John Before the Council (vv1–22)
- **"The priests and the captain of the temple and the Sadducees" (v1).** The captain of the temple was the senior priest in charge of the temple police, second only to the high priest. The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy who ran the temple. They accepted only the five books of Moses as binding and denied the resurrection, angels and spirits (23:8). Working with Rome kept them in power, so crowds and talk of a Messiah alarmed them.
- **"Proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead" (v2).** This hit the Sadducees twice. It taught the resurrection they denied, and it proved it with a man they had handed over to death. If Jesus rose, their theology and their verdict were both wrong.
- **"Until the next day, for it was already evening" (v3).** The prayer service had been at 3 p.m. (3:1), and by now it was too late for a hearing. Jewish law did not allow capital cases to be tried at night, though the council had bent that rule for Jesus.
- **"The number of the men came to about five thousand" (v4).** "Men" here (*andres*) means males, so the whole number was larger. Arrest did not stop the growth. It may have helped it.
- **The council (vv5–6).** This is the Sanhedrin, the seventy-one-member ruling council of the Jewish people under Rome: chief priests, elders from leading families, and scribes, the experts in the law, many of them Pharisees. **Annas** had been high priest AD 6–15 and was deposed by the Romans, but many Jews still regarded him as the true high priest, and five of his sons held the office after him. **Caiaphas**, his son-in-law, was the serving high priest, AD 18–36 (John 18:13). **John** may be Jonathan son of Annas, high priest in AD 36–37; one early manuscript tradition reads "Jonathan" here. **Alexander** is unknown. These are the very men who tried Jesus.
- **"By what power or by what name did you do this?" (v7).** They do not deny that something happened. They want to know whose authority is behind it. They had asked Jesus the same question (Luke 20:2).
- **"Then Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit" (v8).** Jesus had promised exactly this: when brought before rulers, "the Holy Spirit will teach you in that very hour what you ought to say" (Luke 12:11–12). The Greek tense suggests a fresh filling for this moment.
- **"A good deed done to a crippled man" (v9).** A touch of irony: they are on trial for an act of kindness.
- **"By the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom you crucified, whom God raised" (v10).** Peter answers their question exactly. The name is Jesus'. Then he turns the hearing around. "You crucified" and "God raised" sit side by side: God has reversed their verdict.
- **"The stone that was rejected by you, the builders" (v11).** Psalm 118:22. Jesus applied it to himself in the temple, speaking to these same leaders (Luke 20:17). Peter adds two words to the psalm: "by you." The builders are Israel's leaders, and the cornerstone they threw out is now the stone the whole building rests on. Peter uses the image again in 1 Peter 2:4–8.
- **"There is no other name under heaven… by which we must be saved" (v12).** "Saved" is the same word used of the man's healing in v9 ("healed"): the healing of his body points to a deeper salvation. The claim is exclusive and clear. Not one way among many, but the only name given by God. Peter says it to the highest religious court in Israel.
- **"Uneducated, common men" (v13).** *Agrammatos* means "unlettered." In papyri of the time it was the word written beside the mark of someone who could not sign their own name, but here it most likely means untrained in the rabbinic schools, not illiterate. *Idiōtēs* means a layman, a private person with no official standing. Fishermen from Galilee, arguing Scripture like trained teachers. **"Boldness"** is *parrēsia*, from "all" and "speech": plain, open, unafraid speaking.
- **"They recognized that they had been with Jesus" (v13).** Jesus too had amazed people who asked, "How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied?" (John 7:15). The likeness was unmistakable. They spoke like their teacher.
- **"Seeing the man who was healed standing beside them" (v14).** The best argument in the room is a man on his own two feet.
- **"What shall we do with these men?" (v16).** The private debate. Luke may have learned what was said from someone present, perhaps a member who later believed (6:7 says many priests did), or from Saul of Tarsus, who was close to the council at this time.
- **"We cannot deny it" (v16).** They admit the miracle and still refuse its meaning. Their only aim is to stop it spreading.
- **"Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge" (v19).** The apostles respect the council's authority by asking them to judge, but they put God above it. Peter states the same principle in 5:29: "We must obey God rather than men." The New Testament teaches respect for authorities (Rom 13:1–7; 1 Pet 2:13–17), but where human authority forbids what God commands, God comes first.
- **"We cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard" (v20).** Eyewitnesses cannot unsee. Compare Jeremiah: "there is in my heart as it were a burning fire" (Jer 20:9) and John: "that which we have seen and heard we proclaim also to you" (1 John 1:3).
- **"Finding no way to punish them, because of the people" (v21).** The council feared the crowd, as it had with Jesus (Luke 22:2). **"More than forty years old" (v22).** A man lame for over forty years could not be faking it.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the Sadducees' alarm plainly: "the resurrection of Christ, if a fact, effectually overthrew the Sadducean doctrine." On v13 JFB says the apostles were "uninstructed in the learning of the Jewish schools," which matches the lexicon's "unlettered" in the sense of untrained. On v20 JFB admires the "wonderful union" of respectful appeal and calm resolve. Matthew Henry: "Miserable is their case, to whom the glory of Christ's kingdom is a grief." Guzik notes that the trial did more good than harm, bringing more believers and bolder apostles, and calls the exclusive claim of v12 offensive to modern pluralism but plainly biblical.

#### The Believers Pray for Boldness (vv23–31)
- **"They went to their friends" (v23).** Literally "to their own." The first thing they do after being threatened is go to the church.
- **"They lifted their voices together to God" (v24).** The voice is singular in Greek, "they lifted voice with one accord," which may mean one person led while all joined in, or that they prayed a familiar form together. Either way the prayer was united (*homothymadon*, as in 1:14).
- **"Sovereign Lord" (v24).** *Despotēs*, the master of a household with full authority over his slaves. The word gives English "despot," but here it means total, rightful rule. Simeon used it in the temple (Luke 2:29). The church calls themselves his "servants" (v29, *douloi*, slaves).
- **"Who made the heaven and the earth and the sea" (v24).** The prayer starts with who God is, not with the threat. It echoes Exodus 20:11, Psalm 146:6 and the prayers of Hezekiah and Nehemiah when they faced enemies (Isa 37:16; Neh 9:6). The council is large, but God made everything.
- **Psalm 2 (vv25–26).** "Why did the Gentiles rage… against the Lord and against his Anointed." "Anointed" is Christ, Messiah. The psalm pictures kings and nations rising against God's King, and God laughing from heaven (Ps 2:4).
- **"Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel" (v27).** The church reads the psalm line by line: Herod Antipas is the king, Pilate the ruler, the Roman soldiers the Gentiles, and Israel's leaders and crowd the peoples. Luke alone records that Herod also questioned Jesus (Luke 23:6–12). Everyone united against one man.
- **"To do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined" (v28).** The worst act in history happened inside God's plan. That does not make it less evil (2:23). It means God's enemies could only do what he had already decided to turn to salvation. And if that is true of the cross, it is true of the council's threats now.
- **"Look upon their threats and grant to your servants to continue to speak your word with all boldness" (v29).** They do not ask God to remove the danger or punish their enemies. They ask for courage to keep doing the very thing they were forbidden to do.
- **"While you stretch out your hand to heal" (v30).** "Your hand" planned the cross (v28), and now they ask that hand to heal. The healing at the gate started this, and they ask for more.
- **"The place… was shaken" (v31).** In the Old Testament the earth shakes when God shows himself (Exod 19:18; Isa 6:4). It is God's visible "yes." **"They were all filled with the Holy Spirit"** again. The Spirit given at Pentecost fills them afresh for new need. **"And continued to speak the word of God with boldness."** The prayer is answered almost word for word (v29).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry: "They do not pray, Lord let us go away from our work, now that it is become dangerous," and "The place was shaken, that their faith might be established and unshaken." Guzik also stresses that they asked for boldness, not safety, knowing it would lead to more conflict. He reads "voice" as one person praying with all agreeing, which is possible but not required by the Greek. He notes *despotēs* as the word for a master whose authority no one questions, which the lexicon supports ("absolute ownership," as one older lexicon puts it). JFB calls the shaking a "glorious token of the commotion which the Gospel was to make."

#### They Had Everything in Common (vv32–37)
- **"Of one heart and soul" (v32).** A Hebrew way of saying complete unity of mind and will (compare Deut 6:5; 1 Chron 12:38). Greek writers had a proverb that "friends have all things in common." Luke shows the church living it.
- **"No one said that any of the things that belonged to him was his own" (v32).** They still owned things. The point is how they held them: as available to others.
- **"With great power the apostles were giving their testimony to the resurrection" (v33).** Luke sets this in the middle of the sharing. Preaching and generosity go together. **"Great grace was upon them all"** may mean God's favor on them, or the people's goodwill toward them (2:47). Both were true.
- **"There was not a needy person among them" (v34).** This deliberately echoes Deuteronomy 15:4 in the Greek Old Testament: "there will be no poor among you." God's ideal for Israel was coming true in the renewed Israel.
- **"Laid it at the apostles' feet" (v35).** A gesture of handing over authority over the gift. The apostles managed the fund, at least until it became too much (6:1–4).
- **"Joseph, who was also called by the apostles Barnabas" (v36).** Luke explains the name as "son of encouragement." The exact Aramaic behind it is debated (perhaps *bar nebuah*, "son of prophecy"), but "son of" in Semitic idiom means "someone marked by," so the apostles named him for what he was like. And it fits: he vouches for Saul when no one else trusts him (9:27), encourages the new Gentile church in Antioch (11:23), and gives John Mark a second chance (15:37–39).
- **"A Levite, a native of Cyprus" (v36).** Levites had no tribal land inheritance in Israel (Num 18:20–24), but by this time that rule was not kept strictly, and he may have owned the field in Cyprus or near Jerusalem. Cyprus had a large Jewish community. Barnabas later takes the gospel back there (13:4).
- **"Sold a field… and laid it at the apostles' feet" (v37).** Luke gives one good example by name, setting up the contrast with the bad example that follows (5:1–11).
- **Commentators:** The Tyndale notes, Guzik and Henry all stress that the sharing was voluntary and met real needs, and Guzik contrasts *koinōnia*, which gives, with forced collectivism, which takes. Guzik also notes that generosity like this was later abused, and Paul had to give rules for who should receive support (1 Tim 5:3–16). JFB notes that a Levite, though his tribe had no inheritance, could acquire land himself, and calls Barnabas "an eminent example of that spirit of generous sacrifice." Matthew Henry suggests that Barnabas, "as one designed to be a preacher of the gospel, he disentangled himself from the affairs of this life."

#### Pointing to Christ
The builders threw away the stone, and God made it the cornerstone. The council that condemned Jesus now finds his name doing what they cannot stop, through men who are plainly his because they talk like him. The church prays Psalm 2 and sees that every enemy of God's Anointed only carried out God's plan to save. "There is no other name": the one name that heals a lame man and saves a sinner is the same name that holds a frightened church together, makes it bold, and opens its hands.
