*Setting:* Isaiah 40–55 speaks to Judeans who would be exiles in Babylon in the 500s BC, after Jerusalem fell in 586. Chapter 41 put the nations and their idols on trial and named Israel as God's "servant" (41:8). It ended with the idols declared "nothing" and the courtroom silent: "there is no one to give counsel" (41:28–29). Chapter 42 answers that silence with "Behold my servant." This is the first of four passages scholars call the Servant Songs (42:1–9; 49:1–13; 50:4–11; 52:13–53:12). In them the servant is sometimes the nation and sometimes one person who does what the nation could not. The chapter moves from that gentle Servant, to a hymn for the whole earth, to God going out like a warrior, and ends with Israel itself as a servant who is blind and deaf.

#### The LORD's Chosen Servant (vv1–9)
- **"Behold my servant, whom I uphold" (v1).** "Behold" answers the "Behold" of 41:29, where the idols were "all a delusion." God turns the court's eyes from empty idols to his servant. "Servant" (*'eved*) was an honor title for those who served a king closely, given to Moses, Joshua and David. "Uphold" (*tamak*) means to grasp or hold firmly. God does not just send this servant out. He keeps hold of him.
- **"My chosen, in whom my soul delights" (v1).** At Jesus' baptism the voice from heaven said, "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" (Matt 3:17). Those words join Psalm 2:7 ("You are my Son") to this verse. At the very start of his ministry Jesus is named both the royal Son and the Servant.
- **"I have put my Spirit upon him" (v1).** In the Old Testament the Spirit came on judges, kings and prophets for a task. Isaiah has already promised a king from Jesse on whom "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest" (11:2), and later the servant says, "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me" (61:1). Jesus read that passage in Nazareth and said, "Today this Scripture has been fulfilled" (Luke 4:18–21). The Tyndale notes point out that the Spirit marks this servant as a king like David or a prophet like Moses.
- **"He will bring forth justice to the nations" (v1).** "Justice" (*mishpat*) is a key word, three times in four verses (vv1, 3, 4). It means more than court verdicts. It is God's right order for life, what kings were supposed to establish (9:7; Ps 72:1–4). Israel's kings failed to bring it to Israel. This servant brings it to the nations.
- **Who is the servant?** In 41:8 the servant was the nation, and in 42:19 it is again. But this servant has a mission *to* Israel and the nations (v6; 49:5–6), so he cannot simply be Israel. Many Jewish interpreters read the songs of the nation or of a faithful remnant, though the ancient Aramaic paraphrase (the Targum) reads 42:1 as "Behold my servant, the Messiah." Matthew quotes vv1–4 and says plainly that Jesus fulfilled them (Matt 12:15–21). Christians have long seen a narrowing: Israel was called to be God's servant, failed, and then one Israelite took up the calling for them.
- **"He will not cry aloud or lift up his voice" (v2).** Three ways of saying the same thing: no shouting, no raising his voice, no making it heard in the street. This is not a ruler who wins by noise, threats or self-promotion. Matthew quotes this exactly when Jesus heals the crowds and then "ordered them not to make him known" (Matt 12:16–19).
- **"A bruised reed he will not break" (v3).** Reeds grew thick along the Nile and the Jordan. A bruised one was bent and cracked, good for nothing, and the normal thing was to snap it off and throw it away. Egypt is called a "broken reed" in 36:6. The word "bruised" (*ratsuts*) means crushed.
- **"A faintly burning wick he will not quench" (v3).** A lamp's wick was made of flax. When the oil ran low it smoked and sputtered, and the natural thing was to pinch it out. The servant does the opposite. He handles the weak and nearly spent with care. That describes Jesus with lepers, a woman caught in adultery, Peter after his denials, and Thomas in his doubt.
- **"He will not grow faint or be discouraged" (v4).** Here is a play on words that English hides. "Grow faint" (*yikheh*) is the same Hebrew root as "faintly burning" in v3, and "be discouraged" (*yaruts*) is the same root as "bruised." The servant is gentle with smoldering wicks and bruised reeds, but he himself will not smolder out or be crushed until his work is done. Both roots are confirmed in the Hebrew lexicon.
- **"The coastlands wait for his law" (v4).** "Coastlands" are the far shores of the Mediterranean, the ends of the known world (41:1). "Law" is *torah*, God's instruction. The nations are pictured as longing for what only he can give. Matthew follows the Greek Old Testament here: "in his name the Gentiles will hope" (Matt 12:21).
- **"Thus says God, the LORD, who created the heavens" (v5).** Before God speaks to the servant directly, he states his credentials: Creator of the heavens, the earth, and the breath in every person. The God who gave breath to all can make good on a promise to all.
- **"I have called you in righteousness; I will take you by the hand and keep you" (v6).** Now God speaks *to* the servant. "In righteousness" means for a right purpose and in faithfulness to his promises. Holding someone's hand is the same tender picture as 41:13, where God holds Israel's hand.
- **"I will give you as a covenant for the people, a light for the nations" (v6).** The servant does not just bring a covenant. He *is* the covenant, the place where God and people meet. "The people" is probably Israel (49:8), with "the nations" in the next line. Jesus took the cup and called it "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20). Old Simeon held the baby Jesus and called him "a light for revelation to the Gentiles" (Luke 2:32), echoing this verse and 49:6.
- **"To open the eyes that are blind, to bring out the prisoners" (v7).** The exiles were literal captives, but Isaiah's language reaches further, to spiritual blindness and the prison of sin (61:1). Jesus opened blind eyes and said, "I am the light of the world" (John 9:5). Paul was sent "to open their eyes, so that they may turn from darkness to light" (Acts 26:18).
- **"I am the LORD; that is my name; my glory I give to no other" (v8).** "The LORD" is God's personal name, Yahweh (Exod 3:14–15). He will not share his glory with idols. That makes Jesus' prayer striking: "glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had with you before the world existed" (John 17:5). The God who shares his glory with no creature shares it with his Son.
- **"The former things have come to pass, and new things I now declare" (v9).** "Former things" are earlier prophecies that have already come true. "Before they spring forth" uses a word for plants sprouting: God names the shoot before there is any sign of it. That is the test the idols failed in 41:22–23.
- **Commentators:** Henry begins, "We are sure that these verses are to be understood of Christ, for the evangelist tells us expressly." All three take the servant as the Messiah. JFB adds that in its highest ideal Israel is God's servant, but that ideal is realized only in Christ, its representative. Guzik and Henry both mention a reading of "uphold" as "on whom I lean," the Father trusting the Son with his whole purpose. That is a devotional picture rather than what *tamak* means, which is to grasp or support. JFB ties the "bruised reed" to the servant who is himself bruised in 53:5, so that he can feel for the bruised. Guzik notes the wordplay in vv3–4 and draws a pointed application: the test of a real servant is whether he bends over the bruised and smoldering, rather than looking for work worthy of his talents.

#### Sing to the LORD a New Song (vv10–17)
- **"Sing to the LORD a new song" (v10).** The phrase comes from the Psalms (Ps 33:3; 96:1; 98:1). A new act of God calls for a new song, one never needed before. In Revelation the redeemed sing "a new song" to the Lamb who ransomed people "from every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:9).
- **From sea to desert (vv10–11).** The call goes out to sailors, sea creatures, the far coastlands, the desert, and the mountains. It covers every edge of the world Israel knew. **Kedar** was a nomadic Arab people descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13), living in black tents in the Syrian-Arabian desert. **Sela** means "rock." It was an Edomite stronghold, often linked with the rock city later called Petra (2 Kgs 14:7). These were Israel's old neighbors and sometimes enemies, and here they are invited to sing.
- **"Let them give glory to the LORD" (v12).** This is the opposite of v8. The glory that idols cannot have is given freely by the nations to the true God.
- **"The LORD goes out like a mighty man, like a man of war" (v13).** The picture changes suddenly from the quiet servant to a warrior raising his battle cry. It echoes the song at the Red Sea: "The LORD is a man of war" (Exod 15:3). The same God who will not shout in the street (v2) will shout on the battlefield (v13). Gentleness toward the weak and fierceness toward evil belong to one character.
- **"For a long time I have held my peace… now I will cry out like a woman in labor" (v14).** To the exiles God seemed silent, and Psalm 50:21 and Habakkuk 1:2 voice the same complaint. God says the silence was restraint, not absence. The picture is a woman in labor who has held her breath through long pains and now cries out. It is a startling image for God, but it fits: something new is about to be born, and it comes with effort and cries.
- **"I will lay waste mountains and hills… turn the rivers into islands" (v15).** "Islands" here probably means dry land. This reverses the blessing of 41:18, where God opened rivers in the desert. For his enemies the land dries up. For his people it blooms.
- **"I will lead the blind in a way that they do not know" (v16).** The blind here are God's people, who cannot see the road home. He promises to turn darkness to light and rough places to level ground (40:4). **"These are the things I do, and I do not forsake them."** It is one of the Bible's quiet, solid promises (Deut 31:6; Heb 13:5).
- **"They are turned back and utterly put to shame, who trust in carved idols" (v17).** The section ends where the trial in chapter 41 ended. Those who said to a metal image, "You are our gods," will find no one answers.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this "a New-Testament song." The old songs were sung mostly in Jerusalem's temple, and in exile Israel hung up its harps and could not sing (Ps 137), but now the song goes to the whole earth. JFB reads the mountains of v11 as those of Paran and Sela as Petra, though both are guesses about exact places. Guzik contrasts v2 and v13 as the two comings of Christ, quiet the first time and with a shout the second (1 Thess 4:16). JFB similarly sees the servant's gentle work now and his judgment at the end joined in one prophecy, as in 61:1–2, where Jesus stopped reading before "the day of vengeance."

#### Israel's Failure to Hear and See (vv18–25)
- **"Hear, you deaf, and look, you blind" (v18).** God has just promised to lead the blind (v16). Now he turns to a different kind of blindness, the kind that refuses to see. This goes back to Isaiah's call: "keep on seeing, but do not perceive" (6:9–10).
- **"Who is blind but my servant, or deaf as my messenger whom I send?" (v19).** The servant is now Israel, the nation, and the contrast with vv1–9 is deliberate. The servant of vv1–7 opens blind eyes. The servant of v19 is blind himself. Israel was meant to be a messenger to the nations, but a blind guide cannot show anyone the way. Jesus says the same of the Pharisees: "blind guides" (Matt 15:14).
- **"Blind as my dedicated one" (v19).** The Hebrew is *meshullam*, related to *shalom*, peace or wholeness. Its exact sense is debated: "the one at peace with me," "the one in covenant with me," or "the perfect one" (KJV). Whichever it is, it names Israel's privileged standing, and makes the blindness worse.
- **"He sees many things, but does not observe them" (v20).** Israel had seen the plagues of Egypt, the sea parted, manna, the walls of Jericho, and the angel's defeat of Sennacherib (ch 37). Seeing is not the same as taking it to heart (Deut 29:2–4).
- **"The LORD was pleased, for his righteousness' sake, to magnify his law and make it glorious" (v21).** Israel's failure does not defeat God's purpose. He gave his *torah* to show his righteousness, and he will see it honored. Paul makes a similar point: "Does their faithlessness nullify the faithfulness of God? By no means!" (Rom 3:3–4). Christians have also seen here the servant of v1, who perfectly kept the law that Israel broke (Matt 5:17).
- **"A people plundered and looted… with none to say, 'Restore!'" (v22).** This is the exile from the inside: trapped in holes, hidden in prisons, and no one to speak up for them. The cry nobody makes, "Restore!", is exactly what the servant comes to do (49:6, "to restore the preserved of Israel").
- **"Who gave up Jacob to the looter… Was it not the LORD, against whom we have sinned?" (v24).** This is the hard truth at the center of the exile. Babylon was only the tool. The LORD himself handed them over because they would not walk in his ways. Notice Isaiah's "we." The prophet includes himself among the sinners, as he did at his call: "I am a man of unclean lips" (6:5).
- **"It set him on fire all around, but he did not understand" (v25).** The tragedy is not only the judgment but the failure to learn from it. The fire burned, and still Israel did not take it to heart. Discipline is meant to turn people back (Heb 12:11). Chapter 43 opens with the answer: "But now… Fear not, for I have redeemed you."
- **Commentators:** Henry quotes the Latin proverb *corruptio optimi pessima*, "what is best becomes, when corrupted, the worst": nobody is as blind as God's own servant who refuses to see. He also notes that "multitudes are ruined for want of observing that which they cannot but see." JFB, following Horsley, also reads v19 as pointing to Christ, who endured insult as though blind and deaf (Ps 38:13–14). Most interpreters, including the Tyndale notes, see only Israel here, and that is the plainer reading of v24. Guzik stresses that healing starts with admitting blindness, and links Jesus' words to the Pharisees: "now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains" (John 9:41). He asks whether the fire of God's discipline is burning away sin or only making a person harder.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter sets two servants side by side. One is blind, deaf and plundered, and it is Israel, and in it every one of us. The other is chosen, Spirit-filled and gentle, and he does not give up until justice is established. Matthew watched Jesus heal crowds, refuse to promote himself, and bear with the bruised and smoldering, and he saw this chapter come true (Matt 12:18–21). The Servant does not snap off what is cracked. He came to be the covenant, the light, the opener of blind eyes, and in the end he was bruised himself so that the bruised could be made whole (53:5).
