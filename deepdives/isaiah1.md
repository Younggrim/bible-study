*Setting:* Jerusalem, roughly 740–700 BC. Isaiah, whose name means "the LORD is salvation," prophesied for about half a century under four kings of Judah, from the year Uzziah died (6:1) through the reign of Hezekiah. He lived in the capital, had access to the royal court, and was a contemporary of Micah, Hosea and Amos. His book is the longest of the prophets and, after the Psalms, the Old Testament book the New Testament quotes most. Most scholars think its later chapters (especially 40–66) come from disciples writing during or after the exile. Many conservative scholars hold that Isaiah wrote the whole book, noting that John quotes from both halves and names Isaiah as the author of both (John 12:38–41). Jewish tradition holds that Isaiah was martyred under King Manasseh, sawn in two, and Hebrews 11:37 may allude to this. Chapter 1 serves as the overture to the whole book. The devastation it describes in vv7–8, with the countryside burned and Jerusalem left alone, fits Sennacherib's invasion of 701 BC (2 Kgs 18–19), but it has been placed first because it sums up everything that follows: rebellion, empty religion, the offer of cleansing, purifying judgment, and a restored "faithful city."

#### The Wickedness of Judah (vv1–20)
- **"The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz" (v1).** "Vision" (*chazon*) is the word for revelation God gives a prophet, here used as a title for the whole book (compare Obad 1; Nah 1:1). Amoz is not the prophet Amos; the names are spelled differently in Hebrew. **"Concerning Judah and Jerusalem"**: Isaiah speaks to many nations (chs 13–23), but his focus is always God's people.
- **"In the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah" (v1).** About sixty years that included Judah's last age of prosperity under Uzziah (2 Chr 26), the faithless Ahaz (2 Chr 28), the fall of the northern kingdom (722 BC), and Hezekiah's reform and rescue (2 Chr 29–32).
- **"Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth" (v2).** These are almost the opening words of Moses' great song, "Give ear, O heavens… and let the earth hear" (Deut 32:1). Moses called heaven and earth as witnesses to the covenant (Deut 30:19; 31:28). Now they are summoned to court to hear how it has been broken. Micah opens his third round of messages the same way (Mic 6:1–2).
- **"Children have I reared and brought up, but they have rebelled against me" (v2).** God's complaint is a father's grief. He raised them (Exod 4:22, "Israel is my firstborn son"), and they turned on him. "Rebelled" (*pasha'*) is political language, a vassal breaking faith with his king. The law said a stubborn, rebellious son should be brought before the elders (Deut 21:18–21). Now God himself brings the case.
- **"The ox knows its owner, and the donkey its master's crib, but Israel does not know" (v3).** The dullest animals on the farm know who feeds them and where their manger is. God's people do not. "Know" (*yada'*) means more than awareness. It is relationship and loyalty, the way a wife knows her husband (Hos 2:20). Guzik's blunt summary is that Judah was dumber than the dumbest animals. This verse is why medieval and later Christian nativity scenes so often include an ox and a donkey beside the manger. The Gospels never mention them, but early Christian writers saw in Jesus' birth in a manger the moment when God's own did not know him (John 1:11).
- **"Ah, sinful nation, a people laden with iniquity" (v4).** "Ah" is *hoy*, the cry of grief at a funeral. The four descriptions grow worse: a nation, a people, offspring, children. JFB notes the rising intensity. They are "laden" with sin like an overloaded donkey. **"They have despised the Holy One of Israel."** This is Isaiah's signature title for God, used about twenty-five times in his book and only a handful of times in the rest of the Old Testament. It comes from his vision in the temple, when the seraphim cried "Holy, holy, holy" (6:3). The holy God is *Israel's* God, and that makes their contempt for him all the more shocking. **"They are utterly estranged"** is literally "they have gone backward."
- **"Why will you still be struck down? Why will you continue to rebel?" (v5).** God has already disciplined them, and they still refuse to learn. The picture is of a man beaten again and again who keeps provoking the next blow.
- **"The whole head is sick, and the whole heart faint. From the sole of the foot even to the head, there is no soundness in it" (vv5–6).** The nation is like a body covered in injuries. "Bruises and sores and raw wounds" are the marks of a beating, not disease. **"They are not pressed out or bound up or softened with oil."** This is ancient first aid: squeezing out a wound, bandaging it, and soothing it with olive oil, as the Good Samaritan did (Luke 10:34). No one has treated Judah's wounds, because the people will not admit they are hurt.
- **"Your country lies desolate; your cities are burned with fire" (v7).** In 701 BC Sennacherib of Assyria swept through Judah. His own annals boast that he captured forty-six walled cities and countless villages, and shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." **"Foreigners devour your land… in your very presence."** The people watched, helpless, as foreign soldiers ate their crops. This is exactly the curse the covenant warned of (Deut 28:33, 51).
- **"The daughter of Zion is left like a booth in a vineyard, like a lodge in a cucumber field" (v8).** At harvest time, farmers built flimsy shelters of branches in the fields so a watchman could guard the crops at night. After the harvest these huts were left standing, sagging and alone in the stripped fields. That is Jerusalem: the one thing left standing in a ruined land. **"Like a besieged city."** JFB prefers "preserved city," which the Hebrew also allows. It was both: besieged, but spared.
- **"If the LORD of hosts had not left us a few survivors, we should have been like Sodom, and become like Gomorrah" (v9).** "LORD of hosts" means the LORD of armies, heavenly and earthly. Only God's mercy kept a remnant alive. Sodom and Gomorrah were wiped out entirely (Gen 19). Paul quotes this verse, from the Greek version, to show that God has always kept a remnant by grace: "If the Lord of hosts had not left us offspring" (Rom 9:29).
- **"Hear the word of the LORD, you rulers of Sodom! Give ear to the teaching of our God, you people of Gomorrah!" (v10).** A sudden, stinging turn. In v9 Judah was *almost* like Sodom. Now Isaiah says they *are* Sodom. Ezekiel later spells out Sodom's sin: "pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy" (Ezek 16:49). "Teaching" is *torah*, God's instruction.
- **"What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? says the LORD" (v11).** The temple was busy. Offerings were plentiful. Religion was booming. And God says he has "had enough" of it, literally "I am full," like a man who cannot eat another bite. **"I do not delight in the blood of bulls, or of lambs, or of goats."** These were the very sacrifices God commanded in Leviticus. He is not rejecting sacrifice itself but sacrifice offered by people who have no intention of changing (1 Sam 15:22; Ps 51:16–17). Henry: "Many that will readily part with their sacrifices will not be persuaded to part with their sins."
- **"Who has required of you this trampling of my courts?" (v12).** The worshippers came "to appear before me," as the law commanded at the festivals (Exod 23:17). But God hears only the sound of feet tramping across his courtyard, like cattle in a pen.
- **"Bring no more vain offerings; incense is an abomination to me" (v13).** "Vain" means empty, worthless. Incense was meant to rise as a pleasing aroma, a picture of prayer (Ps 141:2). Offered with hypocrisy, it stinks. **"Incense is an abomination,"** the word normally used for idolatry. **"New moon and Sabbath and the calling of convocations—I cannot endure iniquity and solemn assembly."** The new moon began each month with offerings (Num 28:11). Sabbaths and holy assemblies were God's own gifts. What God cannot stand is the two things together: sin and worship side by side.
- **"Your new moons and your appointed feasts my soul hates; they have become a burden to me; I am weary of bearing them" (v14).** God speaks as though the festivals were a heavy load he must carry. Isaiah later turns this around: "you have burdened me with your sins; you have wearied me with your iniquities" (43:24). God bears the weight of his people, but he will not bear their hypocrisy forever.
- **"When you spread out your hands, I will hide my eyes from you; even though you make many prayers, I will not listen" (v15).** The usual posture for prayer was standing with hands raised and palms open toward heaven (1 Kgs 8:22). God looks at those open palms and sees **"your hands are full of blood."** "Blood" is plural in Hebrew, bloodshed and bloodguilt, the blood of the poor and innocent crushed by injustice. Henry: "Hypocrisy in religion is of all things most abominable to the God of heaven."
- **"Wash yourselves; make yourselves clean; remove the evil of your deeds from before my eyes" (v16).** The washing is moral, not ritual. Nine commands follow in quick succession across vv16–17: wash, be clean, remove, cease, learn, seek, correct, bring justice, plead. Repentance is not a feeling but a turnaround in behaviour.
- **"Cease to do evil, learn to do good" (vv16–17).** Doing good has to be *learned*. Years of injustice had trained them in the wrong direction.
- **"Seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause" (v17).** "Correct oppression" can also be translated "reprove the oppressor" or "set right the oppressed." The fatherless and widows had no man to defend them in court, so they were easy prey. God names himself their defender (Deut 10:18; Ps 68:5), and he expects his people to be the same. James echoes this: "Religion that is pure and undefiled before God… is this: to visit orphans and widows in their affliction" (Jas 1:27).
- **"Come now, let us reason together, says the LORD" (v18).** The verb (*yakach*) is a legal word, meaning "decide, argue a case, prove who is right." BDB lists meanings like "decide, judge" and "argue before." It is not a friendly chat but an invitation to settle the case in court. The plaintiff, who has every right to condemn, offers the defendant a way out.
- **"Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they are red like crimson, they shall become like wool" (v18).** Scarlet (*shani*) and crimson (*tola'*) were colourfast dyes made from crushed insects, deep red and almost impossible to wash out. The red recalls the blood on their hands (v15). "Crimson" is the same word as "worm." It is the root used in Psalm 22:6, "I am a worm and not a man," a psalm Jesus prayed on the cross. Snow and wool are the whitest things an Israelite knew (Ps 51:7). Some scholars read the sentence as a question, "If your sins are like scarlet, can they become white as snow?" The context of promise and choice in vv19–20 favours the ESV's reading, and so do all three commentators. JFB says the Hebrew suggests sin "double-dyed," and connects the scarlet with Rahab's cord and the robe put on Christ (Matt 27:28).
- **"If you are willing and obedient, you shall eat the good of the land; but if you refuse and rebel, you shall be eaten by the sword" (vv19–20).** The same verb, *'akal*, "eat," appears in both halves: eat the good of the land, or be eaten by the sword. It is the choice Moses set before Israel, "life and good, death and evil" (Deut 30:15–20). **"For the mouth of the LORD has spoken."** Henry: "Those that will not be governed by God's sceptre will certainly and justly be devoured by his sword."
- **Commentators:** Henry says that God is never tired of the prayers of the upright, but soon tires of the costly sacrifices of the wicked. On v16 he says God "will make us white by a full remission." JFB says the sacrifices are rejected not as sacrifices but because they are offered without obedience, and he reads them as shadows pointing to Christ's one sacrifice. He links "know" in v3 with recognising the Owner. Guzik argues that following God is the most reasonable thing a person can do. He insists that only the work of Christ can remove sin's stain, not good works, promises, suffering or time, and he quotes Spurgeon's appeal to respond now, since tomorrow may be too late. He notes Bultema's account of Isaiah's martyrdom and cites John 12 for single authorship.

#### The Unfaithful City (vv21–31)
- **"How the faithful city has become a whore, she who was full of justice!" (v21).** "How" (*'eikhah*) is the opening cry of a funeral lament, the same word that begins the book of Lamentations over fallen Jerusalem (Lam 1:1). Jerusalem was once "faithful" (*ne'emanah*), a loyal wife. Under David, justice and righteousness had lived there (2 Sam 8:15). Now she sells herself. Prostitution is the prophets' image for unfaithfulness to God (Hos 1–3; Ezek 16).
- **"Righteousness lodged in her, but now murderers" (v21).** Righteousness used to stay overnight in Jerusalem as a welcome guest. Now murderers have moved in. In a society where judges take bribes and widows lose their homes, people die.
- **"Your silver has become dross, your best wine mixed with water" (v22).** Dross is the scum skimmed off molten metal, worthless slag. Watered wine is a merchant's cheat. Both look like the real thing but are not. Bultema, quoted by Guzik, puts it memorably: Jesus turned water into wine, but sinners turn wine into water.
- **"Your princes are rebels and companions of thieves" (v23).** There is a sharp pun in Hebrew: *sarayikh sorerim*, "your princes are rebels." Those meant to govern are the worst rebels of all. **"Everyone loves a bribe and runs after gifts."** Compare Micah 3:11 and 7:3, describing the same city at the same time. **"They do not bring justice to the fatherless, and the widow's cause does not come to them."** The cases of the poor never even reach court, because there is no bribe to be had.
- **"Therefore the Lord declares, the LORD of hosts, the Mighty One of Israel" (v24).** Three great titles stack up, as if God were signing his verdict with his full name. "Mighty One of Israel" is found only here. Elsewhere the title is "Mighty One of Jacob," a name going back to Jacob's own blessing (Gen 49:24). It usually describes God as protector.
- **"Ah, I will get relief from my enemies and avenge myself on my foes" (v24).** The terrible twist is that God's "enemies" are his own people. "Get relief" (*nicham*) means to ease himself, to comfort himself, as one finally free of a heavy burden (compare v14). JFB calls this God speaking in human terms of patience that has at last run out.
- **"I will turn my hand against you and will smelt away your dross as with lye and remove all your alloy" (v25).** Now the purpose of the judgment becomes clear. It is not destruction but refining. "Lye" (*bor*) is an alkali or potash used as a flux in smelting, to help separate impurities. "Alloy" (*bedil*) is tin or lead, the base metal mixed into silver. God puts Jerusalem into the furnace, not to destroy the silver but to burn away the dross (Mal 3:2–3; 1 Pet 1:7). JFB says the hand is turned in grace as well as wrath, and that the dross includes sinful people as well as sins.
- **"I will restore your judges as at the first, and your counselors as at the beginning" (v26).** "As at the first" means as in the days of David and Solomon, when the city was "full of justice" (v21). The corrupt princes of v23 will be replaced by honest judges.
- **"Afterward you shall be called the city of righteousness, the faithful city" (v26).** The lament of v21 is answered. The city that "has become a whore" will again be "the faithful city." The same word frames the whole passage, faithful city at the start (v21) and faithful city at the end (v26). Zechariah later uses the same title for the restored Jerusalem (Zech 8:3).
- **"Zion shall be redeemed by justice, and those in her who repent, by righteousness" (v27).** "Redeemed" (*padah*) means ransomed, bought free. God does not save Zion by setting justice aside. He saves her *through* it. Guzik makes the point plainly: God never redeems at the expense of his justice. **"Those in her who repent"** is literally "her returners" (*shaveha*). The word *shuv* means both "repent" and "return," and Isaiah will use both senses. Those who turn back to God will return home.
- **"But rebels and sinners shall be broken together, and those who forsake the LORD shall be consumed" (v28).** The refining fire separates. Those who repent are redeemed; those who persist are destroyed. JFB notes that "destruction" is literally "breaking to shivers."
- **"For they shall be ashamed of the oaks that you desired; and you shall blush for the gardens that you have chosen" (v29).** The oaks were probably terebinths, large shade trees used as sacred groves in Canaanite fertility worship, and the gardens were sacred enclosures for the same rites (Isa 65:3; 66:17; Hos 4:13). People believed these trees and gardens were sources of life and fertility.
- **"For you shall be like an oak whose leaf withers, and like a garden without water" (v30).** A fitting judgment: those who worship trees will become like dying trees. JFB: people take on the character of what they worship (compare Ps 115:8). This is the opposite of the righteous man, "like a tree planted by streams of water… its leaf does not wither" (Ps 1:3), and of the watered garden Isaiah later promises to the faithful (58:11).
- **"The strong shall become tinder, and his work a spark, and both of them shall burn together, with none to quench them" (v31).** "Tinder" is flax fibre, which catches fire instantly. The "strong" man who thought himself secure becomes kindling, and his own deeds become the spark that lights it. Sin carries the seeds of its own destruction. **"With none to quench them."**
- **The book's bookends.** Isaiah ends almost exactly as it begins. The last verse of the book speaks of those "who have rebelled against me" (the same word as 1:2), whose "worm shall not die" and whose "fire shall not be quenched" (66:24). Jesus quotes that last line in his warning about hell (Mark 9:48). In between come all of Isaiah's promises of a new heavens and new earth (65:17; 66:22), echoing the heavens and earth summoned in 1:2.
- **Commentators:** JFB says national corruption starts with the rulers and spreads downward. He notes that Bishop William Bedell, a seventeenth-century Anglican, took v25 as his motto, and that the restoration was partly fulfilled after Babylon and fully in the Messiah. He reads the "strong" man as both the fuel and the spark of his own destruction. Guzik explains the "harlot" as spiritual adultery and the terebinths as Canaanite fertility shrines. He sees in the refining a God who purifies rather than simply destroys. He notes that "LORD of hosts" often appears when God is about to treat his people as enemies. The Tyndale notes read the refining fire as the exile, which would remove the wicked and purify those who returned.

#### Pointing to Christ
"Come now, let us reason together… though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow." Isaiah does not say how that can happen. He will say later. The "worm" word for crimson belongs to the sufferer of Psalm 22, and Isaiah's own fourth Servant Song shows the one who was "wounded for our transgressions" and by whose stripes we are healed (53:5). The bruised and untreated body of Judah (1:5–6) is matched by the Servant's body, "marred, beyond human semblance" (52:14), so that his wounds might bind up theirs. The faithful city that God promised to restore is fulfilled in the new Jerusalem, the bride made "without spot" (Eph 5:27; Rev 21:2). And the blood that stained their hands is washed away by blood of another kind: those in Revelation "have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb" (Rev 7:14).
