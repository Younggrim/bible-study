*Setting:* Galilee, around AD 28, still early in Jesus' public ministry. Chapter 2 began a run of conflict stories in which religious leaders objected to Jesus forgiving sins, eating with sinners, not fasting and letting his disciples pick grain on the Sabbath. Chapter 3 brings that run to a head with a second Sabbath clash, after which his enemies start plotting his death. Then the chapter widens out: huge crowds from every direction, the choosing of the twelve, and two groups who think something is badly wrong with Jesus, his own family and the scribes from Jerusalem. Mark sandwiches the scribes' charge between two scenes with his family, a technique he uses often, so that each story helps explain the other. The chapter ends with Jesus naming his true family.

#### A Man with a Withered Hand (vv1–6)
- **"Again he entered the synagogue" (v1).** Probably the synagogue in Capernaum (1:21). "A withered hand" means a hand that was shriveled or paralyzed. Luke, the doctor, says it was his right hand (Luke 6:6), his working hand. One later tradition imagined him as a stonemason begging to be healed so he would not have to beg for bread. Whether true or not, it fits: a working man in that world without a usable right hand was close to ruin.
- **"They watched Jesus, to see whether he would heal him on the Sabbath, so that they might accuse him" (v2).** The verb means to watch closely, to spy. They expect him to heal. They have no doubt about his power. They only want grounds for a charge. The rabbis allowed medical help on the Sabbath only when life was in danger. A withered hand could wait until tomorrow.
- **"Come here" (v3).** Literally "Rise into the middle." Jesus does not heal quietly. He puts the man and the question in the center of the room.
- **"Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good or to do harm, to save life or to kill?" (v4).** Jesus sharpens the question beyond "Can one heal on the Sabbath?" Refusing to do good when one can is itself a kind of harm. And there is a sharp irony: on this Sabbath Jesus is about to save a life, while his opponents are about to plot taking one (v6). Jewish history offered a clear example. When Gentile troops attacked faithful Jews on the Sabbath during the Maccabean revolt, the Jewish leaders decided they could fight back on the Sabbath to save their lives (1 Macc 2:39–41). If they could kill to save life on the Sabbath, surely Jesus could heal.
- **"But they were silent" (v4).** They have no answer, and they will not admit it.
- **"He looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart" (v5).** Only Mark mentions Jesus' anger here, one of the clearest glimpses of his emotions in the Gospels. Matthew and Luke leave it out. The anger is the look; the grief runs deeper. "Grieved" is a present participle, an ongoing sorrow. "Hardness" (*pōrōsis*) is the word for a callus, or for stone, a heart that has lost its feeling. Jesus' anger is not wounded pride. It is anger at what their hearts are doing to a suffering man and to God's good gift of the Sabbath. It is possible to be angry and not sin (Eph 4:26), and here is what that looks like.
- **"Stretch out your hand" (v5).** The command asks the man to do the very thing he cannot do. As he obeys, the power comes. Jesus did no "work" by the rabbis' rules. He touched nothing and gave no medicine. He only spoke, and that left his accusers nothing to charge him with except the healing itself.
- **"The Pharisees went out and immediately held counsel with the Herodians against him, how to destroy him" (v6).** The Herodians were supporters of Herod Antipas, ruler of Galilee, and his family's rule under Rome. The Pharisees saw Herod's family as half-pagan compromisers. Normally these two groups had little to do with each other. Their shared hatred of Jesus brings them together, and they will team up again in Jerusalem (12:13). Early in Mark, the cross already casts its shadow. On a Sabbath, the day of rest and life, they plan a death.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry treats the command "Stretch forth thine hand" as a picture of grace: God commands what the soul cannot do, and gives the power as it obeys. He notes that Christ "looked round about on them with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts," angry at the sin but grieving over the sinners. Guzik makes the same point, that God enables what he commands, and observes that the critics' expectation that Jesus would heal shows they already knew his power. He calls the alliance of Pharisees and Herodians an irony: old enemies united against the one who did good. JFB, in its notes on Matthew 12, calls the Herodians a "political rather than religious party," whom the Pharisees despised and yet joined against Christ.

#### A Great Crowd Follows Jesus (vv7–12)
- **"Jesus withdrew with his disciples to the sea" (v7).** Away from the synagogue and its plotting, to the open shore.
- **"From Galilee and Judea and Jerusalem and Idumea and from beyond the Jordan and from around Tyre and Sidon" (vv7–8).** A map of the whole region. Judea and Jerusalem lay to the south, about eighty miles away. Idumea was the old land of Edom far to the south, settled by Edomites after the exile and forcibly converted to Judaism a century before Christ; Herod the Great's family came from there. "Beyond the Jordan" means Perea to the east. Tyre and Sidon were Gentile port cities on the Mediterranean coast to the northwest. People came from every direction, Jews and Gentiles alike. John the Baptist drew Judea and Jerusalem (1:5). Jesus draws far more.
- **"Have a boat ready for him because of the crowd, lest they crush him" (v9).** The boat probably belonged to Peter and Andrew or to James and John. It let Jesus push off a little from shore and teach without being trampled, as he does in 4:1.
- **"All who had diseases pressed around him to touch him" (v10).** Literally "fell upon him." The word for "diseases" (*mastix*) means "whips" or "scourges," a vivid picture of illness as a lash. Many came for what he could do for their bodies, which was real compassion on his part, but not yet faith in who he was.
- **"You are the Son of God" (v11).** The unclean spirits "fell down before him," bowing in fear, not worship. They know exactly who he is, while the crowds and even the disciples are still working it out. Mark's readers have known since 1:1.
- **"He strictly ordered them not to make him known" (v12).** As in 1:25 and 1:34. Jesus would not accept testimony from demons, and he would not let his identity be announced before the time and in terms the crowds would misunderstand. The title would be proclaimed rightly by a Roman centurion beneath the cross (15:39).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "Wise men, as much as they can, decline a crowd," and on the demons' confession he writes, "It is sad that this great truth should be denied by any of the children of men," when even demons were forced to admit it. Guzik warns that following Jesus for what he does rather than who he is will not last, and follows Lane that naming someone was thought to give power over them, which made the demons' outburst an attempt at control that failed. JFB refers the section to its notes on Matthew 12.

#### The Twelve Apostles (vv13–19)
- **"He went up on the mountain" (v13).** Luke adds that Jesus spent the whole night there in prayer before choosing (Luke 6:12). Moses went up the mountain before God formed Israel as a nation at Sinai (Exod 19). Jesus goes up a mountain to form the core of a renewed Israel.
- **"Called to him those whom he desired, and they came" (v13).** The initiative is his. "You did not choose me, but I chose you" (John 15:16).
- **"He appointed twelve" (v14).** Literally "he made twelve." The number is deliberate. Israel had twelve tribes, descended from the twelve sons of Jacob. Jesus is gathering a new people of God around himself, and later he says the twelve will sit on thrones "judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matt 19:28). "Whom he also named apostles" is in many early manuscripts and the ESV includes it. An apostle (*apostolos*) is "one sent," an envoy who carries the authority of the one who sends him.
- **"So that they might be with him and he might send them out to preach and have authority to cast out demons" (vv14–15).** Two purposes, in a set order. First, to be with him. Then, to be sent. The apostles' ministry would grow out of time spent with Jesus. Their work would be his work: preaching the kingdom, and power over the demonic realm. He sends them out in pairs in chapter 6.
- **"Simon (to whom he gave the name Peter)" (v16).** "Peter" is the Greek *Petros*, from *petra*, "rock," translating the Aramaic *Kepha*, which is why Paul often calls him Cephas. Giving someone a new name in Scripture marks a new identity and calling (Abram to Abraham, Jacob to Israel). Peter is always first in every list of the twelve.
- **"James… and John… Boanerges, that is, Sons of Thunder" (v17).** An Aramaic nickname that Mark translates for his Roman readers. It fits their temper: they wanted to call down fire on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54), and they angled for the best seats in the kingdom (10:35–37). Yet John became known as the apostle of love, and James was the first of the twelve to die for Christ (Acts 12:2). Jesus names them as they are and makes them more.
- **"Andrew, and Philip, and Bartholomew, and Matthew, and Thomas, and James the son of Alphaeus" (v18).** Four lists of the twelve appear in the New Testament (Matt 10:2–4; Mark 3:16–19; Luke 6:14–16; Acts 1:13). They always fall into three groups of four, with Peter first, Philip fifth and James son of Alphaeus ninth. Bartholomew means "son of Tolmai," a family name, and many think he is the Nathanael of John 1:45–49, since John never mentions Bartholomew and the others never mention Nathanael. Matthew is Levi the tax collector (2:14). Thomas means "twin" in Aramaic (John 11:16).
- **"Thaddaeus" (v18).** Luke and Acts list "Judas the son of James" in the same place, and most think they are the same man, perhaps with Thaddaeus as a nickname that kept him from being confused with the other Judas (John 14:22).
- **"Simon the Zealot" (v18).** The Greek is *Kananaios*, from the Aramaic for "zealous." It does not mean "from Canaan," as the KJV's "Canaanite" suggests. Luke translates it as "the Zealot" (Luke 6:15). Zealots were Jewish nationalists who believed God would help them throw off Roman rule, if need be by violence. Whether there was an organized Zealot party this early is debated, but Simon was clearly a man of fierce national zeal. Jesus chose both Matthew, who had worked for the Roman system, and Simon, who would have wanted to tear it down. Only Jesus could hold such a group together.
- **"Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him" (v19).** Every list puts him last and marks him as the betrayer. "Iscariot" probably comes from the Hebrew *ish Kerioth*, "man of Kerioth," a town in southern Judea (Josh 15:25). If so, he was likely the only one of the twelve who was not a Galilean. Other explanations have been offered, including a link to the Latin *sicarius*, "dagger man," but they are less likely. Why would Jesus choose him? The Gospels do not fully explain it. Jesus knew from the beginning (John 6:64, 70), and Scripture was fulfilled (John 13:18). Judas walked with Jesus, heard his teaching and saw his miracles, and still betrayed him. Being near Jesus is not the same as belonging to him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Christ's ministers must be much with him," and he sees in Boanerges "the zeal and fervency of their spirits." Guzik also puts being with Jesus first and notes the range of backgrounds: fishermen, a tax collector, a zealot and a traitor. He follows Geldenhuys that Judas came from Kerioth. On why Jesus chose Judas, he says Jesus knew how it would end and still chose, and that God's wise purposes were at work. The Tyndale notes add that a government official and an anti-government activist side by side shows Jesus' power to overcome natural hostility. JFB refers this section to its notes on Luke 6.

#### Blasphemy Against the Holy Spirit (vv20–30)
- **"He went home, and the crowd gathered again, so that they could not even eat" (v20).** Jesus and his disciples are so pressed by need that they cannot find time to eat. Mark mentions this again in 6:31.
- **"When his family heard it, they went out to seize him, for they were saying, 'He is out of his mind'" (v21).** "His family" is literally "those from beside him," his relatives, as v31 makes clear. They probably came from Nazareth, about twenty miles away. "Seize" is the word used for arresting someone (6:17; 14:1). "Out of his mind" means beside himself, mentally unbalanced. They may have been worried about his health and safety, or embarrassed. Either way, they misunderstand him. John says that "not even his brothers believed in him" (John 7:5). Yet after the resurrection, his brother James met the risen Jesus (1 Cor 15:7) and became a leader of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13).
- **"The scribes who came down from Jerusalem" (v22).** Jerusalem was the center of religious authority. These men were likely an official delegation sent to look into this Galilean teacher. "Came down" is because Jerusalem sits high in the hills.
- **"He is possessed by Beelzebul" (v22).** Beelzebul was a name for Satan. It probably comes from the Canaanite god Baal-zebul, "Baal the prince" or "lord of the house." The Old Testament mentions Baal-zebub, "lord of flies," the god of Ekron (2 Kgs 1:2), which many think was a Jewish mocking twist on the original name. The scribes cannot deny the exorcisms, so they explain them: Jesus is in league with the prince of demons. Jewish writings from later centuries say Jesus "practiced sorcery," the same charge in another form.
- **"How can Satan cast out Satan?" (v23).** Jesus answers "in parables," his first use of the word in Mark. A kingdom divided against itself cannot stand. A household divided cannot stand. If Satan were fighting himself, his rule would be "coming to an end" (v26). The charge makes no sense.
- **"No one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man" (v27).** Here is the real explanation. Satan is the strong man, and the "goods" are the people he holds captive. John had said that one "mightier than I" was coming (1:7), the same root word as "strong." Jesus is the stronger one. He bound Satan in the wilderness (1:12–13), and every exorcism is a raid on Satan's house. Isaiah had asked, "Can the prey be taken from the mighty?" and God answered, "the captives of the mighty shall be taken" (Isa 49:24–25). The final defeat came at the cross (Col 2:15; Heb 2:14).
- **"All sins will be forgiven the children of man, and whatever blasphemies they utter" (v28).** "Truly, I say to you" is Jesus' solemn formula, *amēn*, used at the start of a saying to stress its weight, a usage unique to him. The first half of the saying is a breathtaking promise: every kind of sin and blasphemy can be forgiven. Peter denied him, Paul persecuted his church, and both were forgiven.
- **"But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness, but is guilty of an eternal sin" (v29).** The KJV's "in danger of eternal damnation" follows later manuscripts. The older ones, which the ESV follows, read "eternal sin." What is this sin? Mark explains in v30: "for they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit.'" The scribes saw the Spirit's work in plain view, the setting free of people held by demons, and called it Satan's work. That is calling good evil and light darkness (Isa 5:20). It is not a single careless word, but a settled, willful rejection of the Spirit's clear testimony to Jesus. It is unforgivable not because God's grace is too small, but because the person refuses the only one through whom forgiveness comes. Jesus' warning implies these scribes were in danger of it, not that they had already crossed the line. Christians who worry they have committed this sin show by that very worry that they have not. A heart hardened in this way does not care.
- **Commentators:** Henry gives both readings of the family's motive, concern or embarrassment, and explains the unpardonable sin as rejecting the last means of conviction God offers. On v27 he says Christ binds the strong man by superior power. JFB, in its notes on Matthew 12, says the sin cannot be unpardonable because of the nature of sin, since all other sin is forgiven, and calls it to "hate the light the clearer it became." It describes the Pharisees as "bordering upon, and in spirit committing" it. Guzik calls it a settled rejection of the Spirit's witness to Jesus, and cites Ironside that the warning is meant to alert, not to torment anxious seekers. He lists the reasons Jesus' family might have thought him unbalanced: he left his trade, drew huge crowds, faced threats and neglected his meals.

#### Jesus' Mother and Brothers (vv31–35)
- **"His mother and his brothers came, and standing outside" (v31).** This picks up the story from v21. They have arrived to take him home. "Outside" is a quiet but pointed word. They stand outside while the crowd sits around him inside.
- **"Your mother and your brothers are outside, seeking you" (v32).** Some manuscripts add "and your sisters," which the ESV notes. Mark later names four brothers, James, Joses, Judas and Simon, and mentions sisters (6:3). Christians have understood these brothers in different ways. Most Protestants read them as children born to Mary and Joseph after Jesus (Matt 1:25). An early tradition, held by Eastern Orthodox Christians, sees them as Joseph's children from a previous marriage. Roman Catholic teaching, following Jerome, sees them as cousins, holding to Mary's lifelong virginity. The Greek word *adelphos* normally means brother but can be used more broadly for a relative.
- **"Who are my mother and my brothers?" (v33).** The question would have shocked his hearers. In that culture family loyalty was close to sacred, and honoring parents was one of the Ten Commandments. Jesus is not rejecting his family. He later provides for his mother from the cross (John 19:26–27). He is redefining what family means in the kingdom.
- **"Looking about at those who sat around him, he said, 'Here are my mother and my brothers!'" (v34).** Mark loves to note where Jesus looks (3:5; 10:21, 23). Here it is a look of warmth at the people sitting at his feet to listen.
- **"Whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother" (v35).** Note the word "sister." Women were among his listeners and his followers, and they belong to his family fully. Not birth, not race, not religious standing, but doing God's will makes someone kin to Jesus. The first will of God is to believe in the one he sent (John 6:29). Jesus later promises those who leave family for him "a hundredfold… brothers and sisters and mothers and children" (10:29–30). The church is meant to be that family.
- **Commentators:** Henry says those who do God's will are those "on whom Christ here puts a superlative honour," a closer bond than any natural kinship. JFB notes that "brethren" here could be rendered "relatives," and says that those who do the Father's will are joined to Christ more closely than blood relations. Guzik argues that the brothers were Mary's own children, against the doctrine of her perpetual virginity, and notes that the saying, by naming "sister," explicitly includes women. The Tyndale notes set out the main views on the brothers fairly, as above.

#### Pointing to Christ
This chapter shows how differently people respond to Jesus. A man with a withered hand obeys and is healed. Religious leaders plot his death. Crowds press in for healing. Demons fall down in terror. Twelve ordinary men are called to be with him. His own family thinks he has lost his mind. Scholars from the capital say he works for Satan. In the middle of it all, Jesus calmly reveals who he is: the one who does good on the Sabbath, the one who binds the strong man and frees his captives, the one in whom all sins can be forgiven. And he opens his family to anyone who will sit at his feet and do the will of God. The question the chapter leaves is the one every reader must answer: outside, or inside?
