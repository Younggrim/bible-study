*Setting:* Jacob has lived in Paddan-aram, the region around Haran in upper Mesopotamia (southeastern Turkey today), for twenty years (v38). He came with nothing but a staff (32:10), fleeing the brother he had cheated (27:41–45). At Bethel God promised to be with him and bring him back (28:15), and Jacob vowed that if God did, the LORD would be his God (28:20–22). Since then Laban has tricked him into marrying Leah before Rachel (29:25), eleven sons and a daughter have been born (29:31–30:24), and in chapter 30 Jacob's flocks of speckled and spotted animals multiplied while Laban's shrank. Dates are approximate, somewhere in the early second millennium BC. The deceiver has been deceived, but he has also been blessed. Chapter 31 is the break with Laban: a secret flight, a hot pursuit, a stolen set of household gods, a fierce argument, and a treaty that sets a border between Jacob's family and his Aramean relatives. The ESV gives the whole chapter one heading.

#### Jacob Flees from Laban (vv1–55)
- **"Jacob has taken all that was our father's" (v1).** Laban's sons see only their shrinking inheritance. The word the ESV renders "wealth" is *kabod*, usually "glory" or "honor." The lexicon lists this verse under its first sense, "abundance, riches," but the choice of word is telling: Jacob's prosperity looks like weight and standing. The sons' charge is half true at best. Jacob gained the animals under an agreement Laban accepted (30:31–34).
- **"Laban did not regard him with favor as before" (v2).** Literally Laban's "face" was not toward him "as yesterday and the day before," a Hebrew idiom for how things used to be. Envy in the sons and coldness in the father show that the household has turned against Jacob.
- **"Return to the land of your fathers… and I will be with you" (v3).** The decision to leave does not rest on hurt feelings. God speaks. "I will be with you" repeats the Bethel promise (28:15) and the word God gave Isaac (26:3). The Tyndale notes observe how much this return looks forward to Israel's later return from Egypt: God protects his people through dreams, defeats foreign gods, and sets boundaries between nations.
- **"Called Rachel and Leah into the field" (v4).** Jacob meets his wives where no one in Laban's house can overhear. The order of names is striking: Rachel first, the beloved wife, though Leah was the elder. Jacob needs both women willing to leave their father, so he lays out his case.
- **"Changed my wages ten times" (v7).** "Ten times" is probably a round number meaning "over and over" (compare Num 14:22, where Israel tested God "these ten times"). Jacob says it again in v41. His point is that every time Laban rewrote the deal to favor himself, God made the flock bear the very kind of animal Jacob had been promised (v8).
- **"God has taken away the livestock of your father and given them to me" (v9).** Chapter 30 described Jacob's peeled branches and selective breeding (30:37–43), which looked like folk science and cleverness. Here Jacob gives the credit to God. The Hebrew verb "taken away" is *natsal*, which most often means "deliver" or "rescue." The lexicon puts this verse under its sense "take away, snatch away," as a shepherd snatches prey from a lion's mouth (1 Sam 17:35). The same verb returns in Jacob's prayer "Please deliver me" (32:11).
- **The dream of the goats (vv10–12).** Jacob now reveals a dream from the breeding season: the angel of God showed him that the mating goats were all striped, spotted and mottled, "for I have seen all that Laban is doing to you." The point is not that the branches worked, but that God was watching and acting. The reader is left to weigh the branches of chapter 30 against this dream. Most interpreters conclude that Jacob's methods did no harm but that God, not technique, made the difference.
- **"I am the God of Bethel" (v13).** The "angel of God" in v11 speaks here as God himself. This angel who speaks in God's own name appears often in Genesis (16:10–13; 22:11–16), and many Christians have seen in him an appearance of the Son before his incarnation. God reminds Jacob of the pillar he anointed and the vow he made (28:18–22). Twenty years later the vow is still open, and it is time to keep it.
- **"He has sold us, and he has indeed devoured our money" (v15).** Rachel and Leah answer as one, perhaps the only time they agree on anything. In that world a bride price was often meant to benefit the bride in part, a kind of security. Laban took fourteen years of Jacob's labor and kept the value for himself. His daughters feel like "foreigners" in their own father's house. Their answer is complete: "whatever God has said to you, do" (v16).
- **Camels, flocks and the road to Canaan (vv17–18).** Jacob gathers everything "he had acquired in Paddan-aram." The narrator stresses that it was his own. The goal is not just escape but "to his father Isaac," back to the family and the land of the promise.
- **"Rachel stole her father's household gods" (v19).** The word is *teraphim*. The lexicon calls them "a kind of idol, object of reverence, and means of divination." They could be small enough to hide under a saddle (v34), though in 1 Samuel 19:13 an image large enough to pass for a man in bed is called by the same name. Later Scripture condemns them (1 Sam 15:23; 2 Kgs 23:24). Why Rachel took them is never said. The main suggestions are that she still revered them (compare 35:2–4, where Jacob's household must bury its foreign gods), that she wanted protection on the journey, that she wanted to keep her father from divining their route, or that she was taking some revenge for her lost inheritance. In the mid-twentieth century many scholars pointed to tablets from Nuzi in Mesopotamia and argued that holding the household gods gave a claim to the family estate. The Tyndale notes allow that this may be part of it, but many scholars now think the Nuzi parallel was pressed too far. The text simply records the theft and lets it set up the tense scene to come.
- **Two kinds of stealing (vv19–20).** The same verb, *ganab*, "steal," appears twice in a row. Rachel "stole" the gods, and Jacob "stole the heart of" Laban, as the ESV footnote gives the Hebrew for "tricked." The lexicon confirms that "to steal the heart" means "to deceive." Husband and wife are both taking something from Laban by stealth. Jacob, who stole a blessing (27:35–36), is still a man who works in the dark.
- **"Laban the Aramean" (v20).** The narrator names Laban this way here and in v24, underlining that this is now a quarrel between two peoples, not just two men. Israel would later confess, "A wandering Aramean was my father" (Deut 26:5), speaking of Jacob, who lived long years in Aram.
- **"Crossed the Euphrates… toward the hill country of Gilead" (v21).** The ESV footnote notes the Hebrew says simply "the River," the usual biblical name for the Euphrates. Gilead is the hilly, wooded land east of the Jordan, in northern Jordan today, some 300 miles or more from Haran. Jacob "set his face" toward home, an expression of firm resolve.
- **Pursuit and a warning dream (vv22–24).** Laban hears on the third day and covers the distance in seven, which shows how slowly a caravan with flocks and children moved. On the night before he catches up, God comes to him in a dream: "Be careful not to say anything to Jacob, either good or bad." This idiom means "do nothing at all to change the situation." Laban may not coax Jacob back with kind words or force him back with threats. God, who has been silent toward Laban for twenty years, now steps in to protect Jacob.
- **"Like captives of the sword" (v26).** Laban plays the wronged father. He would have sent them off "with mirth and songs, with tambourine and lyre" (v27), and he was denied a farewell kiss (v28). Nothing in the last twenty years suggests he would have let them go so happily. Then the threat slips out: "It is in my power to do you harm" (v29). The ESV footnote says "you" is plural here, so the threat covers Jacob's whole company. But Laban has to admit that "the God of your father" warned him. His power is real but bound.
- **"Why did you steal my gods?" (v30).** This is the charge Laban really cares about. Henry's comment is sharp: "Foolish man! to call those his gods that could be stolen!" Isaiah would later mock gods that have to be carried and cannot save themselves (Isa 46:1–2, 7).
- **"Anyone with whom you find your gods shall not live" (v32).** Jacob answers the first charge honestly: he left in secret "because I was afraid" (v31). Then, sure of his household, he speaks a rash sentence of death. The narrator adds at once, "Jacob did not know that Rachel had stolen them." The reader holds his breath. Rachel later dies giving birth on the road to Bethlehem (35:16–19). Some older interpreters, Henry among them, connect that death to this oath. The text does not draw that link.
- **The search (vv33–35).** Laban goes tent by tent: Jacob's, Leah's, the two servants', and finally Rachel's. He "felt all about," the same verb Isaac used when he felt Jacob's disguised arms (27:12, 22). The deceiver of the blind father is now the one hidden in plain sight. Rachel sits on the gods in the camel's saddle and pleads "the way of women," her monthly period. Later law treated anything a menstruating woman sat on as unclean (Lev 15:20). The story is quietly mocking: Laban's gods are hidden under a camel saddle, and his daughter is sitting on them.
- **"What is my offense? What is my sin?" (v36).** Twenty years of resentment pour out. These words come from the language of a lawsuit. Jacob asks for the kinsmen on both sides to judge (v37). Now Laban is the one in the dock.
- **A shepherd's record (vv38–40).** Jacob lists his faithfulness. No miscarriages in the flocks, which suggests skilled care. He never ate Laban's rams. Animals torn by beasts he replaced himself. Ancient custom did not require this. The Code of Hammurabi (§266) relieved a shepherd of loss from a lion, and the law of Moses let a herdsman bring the torn remains as evidence and owe nothing (Exod 22:13; compare Amos 3:12). Laban demanded payment anyway, even for animals "stolen by day or stolen by night" (v39). Jacob endured heat by day, frost by night, and sleepless nights. The Tyndale notes point out the irony that Jacob, the quiet man of the tents (25:27), spent twenty years in the open country Esau loved.
- **"Fourteen years for your two daughters, and six years for your flock" (v41).** The timeline is clear: seven years for Rachel, seven more after the switch with Leah, then six on wages. Jacob would have left "empty-handed" (v42) if God had not intervened.
- **"The God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac" (v42).** "Fear" is *pachad*, "dread" or "awe." The lexicon lists this verse and v53 as a special use: a name for God himself, the one Isaac held in awe. Some scholars have proposed reading the word as "Kinsman of Isaac" from a related word in other Semitic languages, but the lexicon used here does not support that. It keeps the sense "dread." Henry suggests that Isaac, still alive, was "sanctifying the Lord in his heart, as his fear and his dread." Jacob still speaks of God as his fathers' God, not yet as his own. The line "God saw my affliction" echoes Leah's words at Reuben's birth, "the LORD has looked upon my affliction" (29:32). God sees, whether the sufferer is Leah or Jacob.
- **"All that you see is mine" (v43).** Laban's reply shows his heart. Daughters, grandchildren, flocks: all of it is "mine." But he cannot act on it, so he proposes a covenant. The Tyndale notes observe that it is Laban, not Jacob, who now needs the protection of a treaty.
- **Stone pillar and stone heap (vv45–46).** Jacob sets up a single standing stone, as he did at Bethel (28:18). His kinsmen pile up a heap of stones, and they eat by it. A shared meal sealed agreements in the ancient world (compare 26:30).
- **Two languages, one witness (v47).** Laban names the heap *Jegar-sahadutha*, and Jacob names it *Galeed*. The ESV footnote explains that both mean "heap of witness," the first in Aramaic and the second in Hebrew. These two words are the only Aramaic in Genesis. The family that came from the same grandfather now speaks two different languages, and the border between them is marked in both. The lexicon notes that *Galeed* seems meant to explain the name Gilead, the region where this happens.
- **"The LORD watch between you and me" (v49).** The heap is also called *Mizpah*, which the ESV footnote gives as "watchpost" and the lexicon as "outlook-point." The verb "watch" (*tsaphah*) is the word for a lookout or a watchman. In its setting this is not a tender blessing between friends. It is a warning between two men who do not trust each other: if either breaks faith when the other cannot see, God will. The terms in v50 protect Laban's daughters: Jacob must not mistreat them or take other wives.
- **"The God of Abraham and the God of Nahor" (v53).** Laban appeals to the God of Abraham, the God of his own grandfather Nahor, and "the God of their father," Terah. The verb "judge" is plural in Hebrew, which suggests Laban may be thinking of more than one god. Terah's family "served other gods" (Josh 24:2). Jacob does not take up Laban's formula. He swears "by the Fear of his father Isaac," the God of the promise alone.
- **Sacrifice and a meal (v54).** Jacob offers a sacrifice and calls "his kinsmen," which here probably includes Laban's party, to eat bread. The meal seals the peace.
- **"Kissed his grandchildren and his daughters and blessed them" (v55).** In the Hebrew Bible this verse is 32:1, as the ESV footnote notes. Laban gets the farewell he complained he was denied (v28). Then he "departed and returned home," and he never appears in the story again. The heap in Gilead became the line between Israel and Aram, two nations that would fight for centuries.
- **Commentators:** All three treat God's protection of Jacob as the heart of the chapter. Henry calls Laban's sons' resentment the way of the world: "Whom Heaven blesses hell curses." On v3 he says, "When the world begins to smile upon us we must remember it is not our home." On v4 he observes, "Where there is a mutual affection there will be a mutual confidence," and JFB repeats a similar line of Henry's, that wives who are true helpmeets "will never be their hindrances." On the flight, Henry and JFB both defend Jacob's secrecy as lawful and prudent, since Laban "might have detained him by violence or artifice" (JFB). Guzik is less kind, reading the secret departure as fear and self-reliance and citing Barnhouse's remark that Jacob slipped into the will of God by sneaking off. On the teraphim, JFB calls them "small images of human figures, used not as idols or objects of worship, but as talismans." Henry offers a generous guess that Rachel meant to show her father the folly of gods "which could not secure themselves." Guzik lists several possible motives without choosing, and he does not mention Nuzi. On God's warning to Laban, Henry says God "with one word ties his hands, though he does not turn his heart." On Jacob's outburst, Henry admits provocation but says it "was not justifiable, nor is it written for our imitation," while JFB says, "Great and long-continued provocations ruffle the mildest and most disciplined tempers." JFB adds that Eastern herdsmen "seldom kill the females for food except they are barren," which explains Jacob's mention of rams. On Mizpah, Guzik insists it was a warning, never meant as a sweet sentiment. Henry reads it more warmly, as an appeal to the God who "has his eye on them both," and he notes, "The name Jacob gave this heap (*Galeed*) stuck by it, not the name Laban gave it." On v53, JFB sees "a marked difference in the religious sentiments of the two." Henry, by contrast, thinks they "worshipped one and the same God." Henry closes on the parting: "After all this angry parley, they part friends." JFB has no notes on vv5, 7–13, 15–16, 19, 21, 25, 41–43, 45–51 or 54–55.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jacob's speech in vv38–40 is the portrait of a faithful shepherd who bore the loss of torn animals himself, endured heat and frost, and lost sleep over another man's flock. He did it for wages and a wife, and even then imperfectly. Jesus is the shepherd who goes further: "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). He loses none of those given to him (John 10:28), and no one can snatch them out of his hand. The chapter also shows the God who sees affliction (v42), protects his people when they cannot protect themselves (v24), and keeps the promise "I will be with you" (v3) long after the one who received it has stopped deserving it. That same God sent his Son so that his people would never be sent away "empty-handed."
