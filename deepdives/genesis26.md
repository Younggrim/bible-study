*Setting:* This is the only chapter in Genesis that is mostly about Isaac himself. Abraham has died and been buried by both his sons (25:8–9), and "after the death of Abraham, God blessed Isaac his son" (25:11). Chapter 25 told of the twins Esau and Jacob and the sold birthright. Chapter 26 steps back from their rivalry, and some of its events may come earlier, since passing Rebekah off as a sister is easier to picture before the twins were born. The closing note is dated to when Esau was forty and Isaac a hundred (25:26). Dates are approximate, somewhere in the early second millennium BC. Isaac lives in the dry Negeb in southern Canaan, where flocks and families live or die by their wells. Almost everything in the chapter echoes his father's life: a famine, a trip to Gerar, a wife called a sister, a king named Abimelech, a quarrel over wells, a treaty, and the name Beersheba. The chapter shows the promise passing to Abraham's quieter son, despite his failures.

#### God's Promise to Isaac (vv1–5)
- **"Besides the former famine that was in the days of Abraham" (v1).** The narrator points back to 12:10, when famine drove Abraham down to Egypt and into trouble. The reader is meant to wonder whether the son will repeat the father's path. Isaac heads toward Gerar, a town in the western Negeb, usually identified with Tel Haror, northwest of Beersheba. Abraham had lived there too (ch 20).
- **"Abimelech king of the Philistines" (v1).** The Tyndale notes point out that this is probably not the same king who dealt with Abraham, since the two episodes could be some ninety years apart. "Abimelech" ("my father is king") seems to have been a dynastic name or title. Psalm 34's heading calls the Philistine king Achish "Abimelech," which supports that. The name "Philistines" raises a historical question. The Philistines best known from archaeology arrived on the coast around 1200 BC, long after Isaac. Some scholars think earlier Aegean settlers lived in the area. Others think the narrator used the name later readers knew for that region.
- **"Do not go down to Egypt" (v2).** This is one of only two times the LORD appears to Isaac in Genesis (the other is v24). Isaac is the one patriarch who never leaves the land. He was not to go back to Mesopotamia (24:6–8), and now not to Egypt either, while Jacob is later told to go down to Egypt (46:3–4).
- **"Sojourn in this land, and I will be with you and will bless you" (v3).** "Sojourn" means to live as a resident foreigner. Isaac is promised the land but lives in it as a guest. "I will be with you" becomes a thread through the Bible, said to Jacob (28:15), Moses (Exod 3:12) and Joshua (Josh 1:5), and fulfilled in "Immanuel," God with us (Matt 1:23).
- **"I will establish the oath that I swore to Abraham" (vv3–4).** Everything in vv3–4 restates promises given to Abraham: land (12:7; 15:18), offspring like the stars (15:5; 22:17), and blessing for all nations (12:3; 22:18). Nothing new is promised, but now it is said to Isaac by name. "Offspring" (literally "seed") can be singular or plural, and Paul reads it as pointing finally to one offspring, Christ (Gal 3:16).
- **"Because Abraham obeyed my voice" (v5).** This repeats almost word for word what God said after Abraham offered Isaac on Mount Moriah: "because you have obeyed my voice" (22:18). Isaac had been on that altar. The list "my charge, my commandments, my statutes, and my laws" uses words that later describe the whole law of Moses (compare Deut 11:1). "Charge" is *mishmereth*, which the lexicon defines as something to be guarded or kept, an obligation. The Tyndale notes suggest that an Israelite reader would hear the whole Torah in these words, and that the point is Abraham's wholehearted obedience to whatever God told him. Jewish tradition went further. The Mishnah (Kiddushin 4:14) cites this verse to say Abraham kept the whole Torah before it was given. Christian readers usually stress that Abraham was counted righteous by faith first (15:6) and that his obedience was the fruit of that faith (Jas 2:21–23). God had already sworn the covenant (ch 15). Here he honors the obedient life that grew from Abraham's faith.
- **Commentators:** Henry asks what Isaac must have thought "of the promise when the promised land will not find him bread?" His answer is that Isaac "will still cleave to the covenant." He also has a striking theory about why Abraham was left free to go to Egypt, Isaac was forbidden, and Jacob was sent: "Isaac was a very good man, but not cut out for hardship; therefore he is forbidden to go to Egypt." That is Henry's guess about character, not something the text states. Guzik agrees with the Tyndale notes and JFB that Abimelech was a title, not a personal name. On v5 he stresses that Abraham's obedience was far from perfect, and God honored it because Abraham had first been declared righteous by faith. JFB comments only on v1 in this section. It notes that Isaac was "exposed to personal danger, as his father had been on account of his wife's beauty," and cites Psalm 105:14–15. It has no notes on vv2–5.

#### Isaac and Abimelech (vv6–35)
- **One long section.** The ESV's single heading runs to the end of the chapter. Abimelech or his people appear in nearly every scene, as Isaac moves from his weakest moment to peace with his enemies.
- **"She is my sister" (v7).** Isaac had just heard "I will be with you" (v3), and the first thing he does in Gerar is lie out of fear. It is the same lie Abraham told twice (12:13; 20:2). There is a difference. Sarah really was Abraham's half sister (20:12). Rebekah was Isaac's relative, the granddaughter of his father's brother Nahor (24:15), and Hebrew "sister" can stretch to cover a kinswoman, but Isaac plainly meant to deceive. And like his father, he protected himself by putting his wife at risk.
- **Like father, like son.** Some scholars see the three wife-sister stories (chs 12, 20, 26) as one old tale told three ways. The Tyndale notes disagree, pointing out that the differences are greater than the likenesses. Here no one takes Rebekah, and the truth comes out by accident. The repetition is the point: sons copy their fathers' sins.
- **"Isaac laughing with Rebekah his wife" (v8).** The verb is *tsachaq*, "laugh," in a form the lexicon defines here as toying with or caressing, "of conjugal caresses." The ESV footnote says the Hebrew may suggest an intimate relationship. It is a play on Isaac's own name, *Yitschaq*, "he laughs" (see 17:17; 18:12; 21:6). The same form describes Ishmael "laughing" at Isaac in 21:9, translated "mocking" in some versions. The Tyndale notes suggest the wordplay is pointed: the man whose name means "laughter," the child of promise, is making a mockery of the promise. The king saw from a window what Isaac had tried to hide.
- **"What is this you have done to us?" (v10).** Once again a Philistine king has to rebuke one of God's people for doing wrong. "Guilt" here is the word used later for an offense that needs atonement (Lev 5). A pagan king has a clearer moral sense here than the son of promise.
- **"Whoever touches this man or his wife shall surely be put to death" (v11).** God protects the marriage through the very ruler Isaac feared. Psalm 105 remembers these stories this way: God "allowed no one to oppress them; he rebuked kings on their account, saying, 'Touch not my anointed ones'" (Ps 105:14–15). The Tyndale notes observe that if this marriage had been broken, there would have been no Israel. God kept his promise even when Isaac endangered it.
- **"Reaped in the same year a hundredfold" (v12).** Isaac, a herdsman, also farms, and in a famine year he gets a harvest of a hundred times what he sowed. That is a remarkable yield. The Hebrew consonants for "measures" (*she'arim*) are the same as those for "barley" (*se'orim*), and the old Greek translation read it as "barley yielding a hundredfold." Either way, the narrator explains it at once: "The LORD blessed him."
- **"Gained more and more" (vv13–14).** The Hebrew piles up the verb "became great": the promise of v3 coming true. "Envied" is *qana*, which the lexicon gives here as "be envious of," the verb used of Rachel (30:1) and Joseph's brothers (37:11).
- **The stopped wells (v15).** The narrator adds this in parentheses. After Abraham died, the Philistines had filled his wells with earth. Abraham had even secured one well by treaty (21:25–31). Filling a well was a hostile way to say "you have no claim here."
- **"Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we" (v16).** This is the language Pharaoh later uses of Israel in Egypt: "the people of Israel are too many and too mighty for us" (Exod 1:9).
- **"Encamped in the Valley of Gerar" (v17).** The "valley" is a *wadi*, a dry streambed that runs with water only after rain. Water lies beneath it, so it is a good place to dig. Isaac reopens Abraham's wells and gives back their old names (v18), claiming his father's inheritance rather than starting over.
- **Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth (vv19–22).** The servants strike "spring water," literally "living water," a flowing source, the best kind (compare Jer 2:13; John 4:10). The herdsmen of Gerar claim it, so Isaac names it *Esek*, which the ESV footnote and the lexicon both give as "contention." The next well he calls *Sitnah*, "enmity" in the ESV footnote, "hostility" in the lexicon. It comes from the same root as *satan*, "adversary" or "accuser." Each time, Isaac does not fight. He moves on and digs again. The third well is left alone, and he names it *Rehoboth*, "broad places" or "room." The verb "made room" (*rachab*) means to make wide or enlarge. It is the same verb as in David's prayer, "you have given me relief [made room for me] when I was in distress" (Ps 4:1), and in Isaiah's call to "enlarge the place of your tent" (Isa 54:2). Isaac gives the credit to God: "now the LORD has made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land."
- **Was Isaac weak or wise?** Readers have seen his giving way in two lights. Some see a passive man, the same one later deceived by his family (ch 27). Others see a peacemaker, like Abraham letting Lot choose first (13:8–9). The text does not grade him, but it shows God honoring his patience.
- **"From there he went up to Beersheba" (v23).** Beersheba lies at the southern edge of the settled land, where Abraham had planted a tamarisk tree and "called there on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God" (21:33). Isaac returns to the place where his father worshiped.
- **"I am the God of Abraham your father. Fear not" (v24).** The LORD appears "the same night," as if answering the strain of being driven from well to well. "Fear not" are the words God first spoke to Abraham (15:1). The LORD introduces himself as "the God of Abraham." He promises his presence and blessing "for my servant Abraham's sake." God calls Abraham "my servant," a title later given to Moses (Num 12:7) and David (2 Sam 7:5). Isaac is blessed because of someone else's relationship with God. Scripture often speaks this way. God spared Judah "for the sake of David my servant" (1 Kgs 11:13).
- **Altar, tent, well (v25).** Isaac builds an altar and calls on the LORD's name, as Abraham did (12:7–8). Then he pitches his tent and his servants dig. Worship comes first, then home and work.
- **"Abimelech… with Ahuzzath his adviser and Phicol the commander of his army" (v26).** A Phicol also led Abimelech's army in Abraham's day (21:22). Like "Abimelech," the name may be a title or run in a family. Isaac is blunt with them: "you hate me" (v27). Even a peaceable man can name a wrong honestly.
- **"We see plainly that the LORD has been with you" (v28).** Outsiders can see what the LORD promised in v3. This is the blessing of 12:3 at work: those who want to share in Abraham's blessing come to make peace with his family. Abimelech even uses the covenant name, the LORD (*YHWH*), and calls Isaac "the blessed of the LORD" (v29). His version of events is generous to himself ("we have done to you nothing but good"). Isaac does not argue the point.
- **"He made them a feast, and they ate and drank" (v30).** In the ancient Near East a shared meal sealed an agreement. Eating together showed that the parties were now at peace and bound to each other (compare 31:54; Exod 24:11). The next morning they "exchanged oaths" (v31). Isaac sends them away "in peace," the word the king had used of him in v29, now true.
- **"We have found water" (v32).** "That same day," the day of the treaty, the servants report a new well. Isaac had let go of well after well. Now water comes without a fight.
- **"He called it Shibah" (v33).** The ESV footnote says *Shibah* sounds like the Hebrew for "oath," and the lexicon notes the old Greek simply translated it "Oath." Beersheba means "well of the oath," and the word can also suggest "seven," recalling the seven lambs of Abraham's treaty (21:28–31). As the Tyndale notes put it, the treaty was renewed, so the name was renewed.
- **Esau's Hittite wives (v34).** Esau marries at forty, the same age as his father (25:20), but he takes two wives, and both are "Hittites," part of the population of Canaan. Abraham had made his servant swear not to take a wife for Isaac "from the daughters of the Canaanites" (24:3). Esau does exactly what his grandfather refused. Genesis 36:2–3 lists Esau's wives with different names. People often had more than one name, so most interpreters see no contradiction, though the details are hard to line up.
- **"They made life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah" (v35).** The ESV footnote gives the literal Hebrew: "they were bitterness of spirit." The chapter that showed Isaac's outward prosperity ends with trouble inside his own tent. It sets up chapter 27, where Isaac still plans to bless Esau (see also 27:46). Hebrews calls Esau "unholy" for trading away his birthright (Heb 12:16), and these marriages show the same carelessness about the promise.
- **Commentators:** On the lie, Henry calls it "an unaccountable thing that both these great and good men should be guilty of so strange a piece of dissimulation," and notes "an aptness in us to imitate even the weaknesses and infirmities of those we have a value for." On v8 he notes that the word is "the same word with that from which Isaac had his name," and approves the scene: "Nowhere may a man more allow himself to be innocently merry than with his own wife and children." Guzik makes the point that Isaac's lie was worse than Abraham's, since Rebekah was not his sister in the same sense. He also argues that Scripture does not teach "generational curses," but that children absorb their parents' sins by example. JFB has no notes on vv2–11, 13–14, 16, 23–25 or 35. On v15 it says the "same base stratagem" of choking wells "is practiced still." On the wells, Henry and Guzik both turn Abraham's reopened wells into a picture of returning to the truths of earlier generations. Henry adds a balance: "Though we must use the light of former ages, it does not therefore follow that we must rest in it." On Isaac's patience he writes, "Those that follow peace, sooner or later, shall find peace," and he sees Rehoboth as a picture of heaven, "room enough there, for there are many mansions." For the treaty, Henry and JFB both cite Proverbs 16:7, that when a man's ways please the LORD, even his enemies are at peace with him. JFB says that Isaac, "being, however, of a pacific disposition," forgave the offense and "treated them to the banquet by which the ratification of a covenant was usually crowned." Henry reads the well found that same day as a reward: "Providence smiled upon what Isaac did." On Esau, Henry and JFB use nearly the same words: Esau showed he "neither desired the blessing nor dreaded the curse of God." JFB adds that these wives "never gained the affections of his parents." Guzik links Esau's choice to Abraham's charge in 24:3–4 and to Hebrews 12:16.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaac is blessed "for my servant Abraham's sake" (v24), not because he earned it. In this very chapter he lies, endangers his wife and needs a pagan king to correct him. Christians are blessed in a similar way, for the sake of another, the true Servant and the true Offspring of Abraham. God forgives "in Christ" (Eph 4:32), and in him "the blessing of Abraham" comes to the nations (Gal 3:14). The promise to Isaac, "I will be with you," reaches its fullest form in Jesus, Immanuel, who promises, "I am with you always" (Matt 28:20). The chapter's wells are a quieter picture. Living water found after strife and patience points to the one who sat by Jacob's well and offered water that becomes "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14). And Isaac's refusal to fight for his rights, while God made room for him, is a faint early outline of the one who "when he was reviled, he did not revile in return" (1 Pet 2:23) and was given the nations as his inheritance.
