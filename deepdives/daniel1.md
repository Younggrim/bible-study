*Setting:* The book opens in 605 BC, almost twenty years before Jerusalem fell. That summer, Nebuchadnezzar, crown prince of Babylon, crushed the Egyptian army at Carchemish on the Euphrates, and Babylon replaced Egypt as the master of Syria and Judah. Within weeks his father died, and he hurried home to take the throne. Judah's king Jehoiakim, who had been Egypt's puppet, now had to serve Babylon (2 Kings 24:1). Among the hostages taken that year were teenagers from Judah's leading families, including Daniel. Ezekiel, a few years later, names a Daniel as a byword for righteousness and wisdom (Ezek 14:14; 28:3). Some scholars think he meant an ancient hero of that name, but many think he meant this Daniel, already famous in Babylon. The book has two halves: stories of Daniel and his friends at a pagan court (chs 1–6), and Daniel's visions of the kingdoms to come (chs 7–12). Chapter 1 sets the scene for both, and asks the question the whole book answers: how do God's people stay faithful in a world that is not their home, and who is really in charge of history?

#### Daniel Taken to Babylon (vv1–7)
- **"In the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim" (v1).** Jeremiah dates the same events to Jehoiakim's "fourth year" (Jer 25:1; 46:2). The usual explanation is that kings' years were counted two ways. In the Babylonian system Daniel used, a king's first partial year was his "accession year," and year one began the following New Year. Judah often counted the partial year as year one. That puts both writers in 605 BC. Babylon's own chronicle records Nebuchadnezzar conquering "the whole of Hatti" (Syria and Palestine) that year, though it does not name Jerusalem.
- **"Came to Jerusalem and besieged it" (v1).** This was the first of three deportations. The second, in 597 BC, took King Jehoiachin and Ezekiel (2 Kings 24:10–16). The third, in 586 BC, destroyed the city and the temple (2 Kings 25). Daniel's exile came first, as a warning of what was coming.
- **"And the Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand" (v2).** This is the key to the whole book. "Lord" here is *Adonai*, "Master," the title of God's sovereign rule. To any onlooker, Babylon had won and its gods had beaten Judah's. Daniel says the opposite: God handed his people over, as he had warned through Moses and the prophets (Deut 28:36; Jer 25:8–11). The same word "gave" returns in vv9 and 17: God *gave* Daniel favor, and God *gave* the four young men learning. The God who gave them into exile is still giving in exile.
- **"With some of the vessels of the house of God" (v2).** Only some were taken this time. The rest went in 597 and 586 (2 Kings 24:13; 25:13–15). These vessels will reappear: Belshazzar drinks from them at his feast on the night Babylon falls (5:2–4), and Cyrus later sends them home (Ezra 1:7–11).
- **"To the land of Shinar, to the house of his god" (v2).** "Shinar" is an old, unusual name for Babylonia. It is where the tower of Babel was built in defiance of God (Gen 11:2–9). The Tyndale notes observe that Israel's rebellion has brought them back to where human rebellion began. "His god" was Marduk, the chief god of Babylon. Putting captured temple vessels in your own god's treasury was the standard way of saying your god had defeated theirs.
- **"Ashpenaz, his chief eunuch" (v3).** The word *saris* originally meant a royal official, and it sometimes means a castrated man, since eunuchs often served in royal households. Whether Daniel and his friends were made eunuchs is debated. Isaiah had warned Hezekiah that some of his descendants "shall be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon" (Isa 39:7), and some ancient Jewish and Christian writers thought this happened to Daniel. But the text never says so, and the Tyndale notes and Guzik both point out that the word often just means a court official (Gen 39:1, where Potiphar, who was married, is called a *saris*).
- **"Both of the royal family and of the nobility" (v3).** Taking the sons of leading families as hostages kept their parents loyal at home, and training them as officials created a class of administrators loyal to the empire.
- **"Youths without blemish… skillful in all wisdom… competent to stand in the king's palace" (v4).** They were probably in their mid-teens. "Without blemish" is the word used for priests and sacrifices that had to be physically whole (Lev 21:17–23; 22:21). Babylon chose its best, and so did God.
- **"The literature and language of the Chaldeans" (v4).** "Chaldeans" here means the learned class of Babylon, scholars and priests trained in divination (2:2). The curriculum meant learning Akkadian in cuneiform script and probably Aramaic, the international language. The Tyndale notes list what Babylonian libraries contained: creation myths, histories, legal texts, mathematics and astronomy, omen and dream manuals, magic spells. It was as thorough as the education Moses received in Egypt (Acts 7:22). Daniel learned it, and did not reject it. The book itself switches to Aramaic from 2:4 to 7:28.
- **"A daily portion of the food that the king ate, and of the wine" (v5).** Food from the royal table was an honor and a tool. Eating the king's food made the young men his dependents and bound them to him. Babylonian records list rations given to Jehoiachin from the palace (compare 2 Kings 25:29–30).
- **"Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah of the tribe of Judah" (v6).** Their Hebrew names all carry God's name. Daniel means "God is my judge." Hananiah means "the LORD has been gracious." Mishael means "Who is what God is?" Azariah means "the LORD has helped."
- **"The chief of the eunuchs gave them names" (v7).** Their new names honored Babylon's gods. **Belteshazzar** probably comes from Babylonian *Balatsu-usur*, "protect his life," a prayer to a god, and Nebuchadnezzar later says it was given "after the name of my god" (4:8). **Shadrach** and **Meshach** are uncertain, perhaps "command of Aku" and "who is like Aku?" (a moon god). **Abednego** means "servant of Nego," probably a deliberate twist of Nabu, the god of wisdom and writing. Renaming was a claim of ownership (2 Kings 23:34; 24:17). Babylon meant to give them new identities. The narrator keeps calling Daniel "Daniel" throughout the book.
- **Commentators:** Henry observes that Babylon "changed his name, but they could not change his nature." JFB explains "third year" by the same counting difference Guzik describes, and dates the deportation "shortly before the battle of Carchemish," though most modern scholars place it just after. JFB glosses Belteshazzar as "Bel's prince," a rendering older writers favored and modern scholars have mostly dropped. Guzik reads the training as an effort to remake the young men's minds through what they ate, what they were called, and what they were taught, and applies it to the pressures believers face in any culture.

#### Daniel's Faithfulness (vv8–21)
- **"But Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself" (v8).** Literally "Daniel set upon his heart." The decision came from inside before the test came from outside. "Defile" (*ga'al*) means to pollute or stain, a word used of ritual impurity (Mal 1:7, 12; Ezra 2:62).
- **Why the food, but not the names or the education?** Daniel accepted a Babylonian name, a Babylonian education and a Babylonian job. He drew the line at the king's table. Interpreters give several reasons, and they may overlap. First, the food would include meat that Moses' law called unclean, like pork, and meat not drained of blood (Lev 11; 17:10–14). Second, Babylonian meat and wine were routinely offered to the gods first, so eating it could look like sharing in idol worship (compare 1 Cor 10:20–21). But these reasons would not explain refusing the wine, and vegetables could have been offered too. So many add a third: eating from the king's table was a sign of dependence and loyalty, and Daniel refused to let Nebuchadnezzar become his provider (compare Ps 141:4). Later in life Daniel did eat meat and wine, abstaining only during a time of mourning (10:3), so this was not a rule against meat as such. It was about this table and this king.
- **"Therefore he asked the chief of the eunuchs" (v8).** Daniel asks. He does not refuse defiantly, stage a protest, or condemn the other captives. He seeks permission. Conviction and courtesy go together here.
- **"And God gave Daniel favor and compassion in the sight of the chief of the eunuchs" (v9).** The second "gave." The same was said of Joseph in prison (Gen 39:21) and of Israel in Egypt (Exod 3:21). "Compassion" (*rachamim*) is the word for the tender mercy God promised his people would find even among their captors (1 Kings 8:50; Ps 106:46).
- **"So you would endanger my head with the king" (v10).** Ashpenaz is sympathetic but afraid, and with reason: Nebuchadnezzar later orders whole groups of wise men executed (2:12–13). Daniel does not brush off his fear.
- **"Daniel said to the steward" (v11).** The KJV reads "Melzar" as a name, but the word is a Babylonian title for a guardian or overseer. Daniel takes his request one step down the chain, to the man who handled their food every day.
- **"Test your servants for ten days" (v12).** This is a creative, wise proposal: short, measurable, and safe for the steward. "Vegetables" (*zero'im*) is literally "things sown," food grown from seed, which would include grains and legumes, not only greens. The KJV calls it "pulse." The Tyndale notes suggest it may also have been a form of fasting in mourning for Jerusalem.
- **"Deal with your servants according to what you see" (v13).** Daniel leaves the outcome open and trusts God with it.
- **"Better in appearance and fatter in flesh" (v15).** In a world of scarcity, "fatter" meant healthy and well-fed. A ten-day diet of vegetables does not normally outdo royal meat and wine. The text presents the result as God's doing, not a nutrition lesson. Moses' words fit: "man does not live by bread alone" (Deut 8:3).
- **"So the steward took away their food and the wine" (v16).** The arrangement became permanent, quietly, without confrontation.
- **"God gave them learning and skill in all literature and wisdom" (v17).** The third "gave." They studied hard, but their excellence was a gift. **"Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams."** Babylon prized dream interpretation, and had manuals full of omens for it. Daniel's gift comes from God, not the manuals, and it prepares for chapter 2. Joseph had the same gift in Pharaoh's court (Gen 40–41), and the parallels between the two exiles run all through these chapters.
- **"The king spoke with them, and among all of them none was found like Daniel" (v19).** After three years, Nebuchadnezzar examined the whole class himself. **"Therefore they stood before the king"** means they entered royal service as advisers.
- **"Ten times better than all the magicians and enchanters" (v20).** "Ten times" is literally "ten hands," a Hebrew idiom for many times over (Gen 31:7). "Magicians" (*chartummim*) is the word used for Pharaoh's magicians in Genesis 41 and Exodus 7, another link with Joseph and Moses. "Enchanters" (*'ashshaphim*) comes from a Babylonian word for those who chanted spells. Babylon's best experts trained for life in the arts of omens and spells. God's four young men surpassed them all.
- **"And Daniel was there until the first year of King Cyrus" (v21).** Cyrus of Persia took Babylon in 539 BC, and his first year as its ruler was the year he let the Jews go home (Ezra 1:1–4). Daniel was still alive and receiving visions two years later (10:1), so "until" does not mark his death. It means he outlasted the whole Babylonian empire. A teenage hostage taken in 605 saw Nebuchadnezzar, his successors and the empire itself come and go, and he was still there, still faithful, when the exile ended.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes on v8, "When God's people are in Babylon they have need to take special care that they partake not in her sins," and on the outcome, "pulse and water shall be the most nourishing food if God speak the word." Guzik lists the same three likely reasons for the refusal as above, unclean meat, idol offerings, and the fellowship a shared table implied, and draws several applications: settle convictions before the test comes, object courteously, accept that faithfulness may cost something, and avoid forcing personal scruples on everyone else. JFB says eating would have meant sanctioning idolatry, since the food was first offered to the gods, and notes that Daniel made his request quietly, without show. JFB reads "ten times" literally as "ten hands" and takes v21 to mean Daniel lived to see the end of the captivity, not that his service stopped that year.

#### Pointing to Christ
Daniel was a son of Judah's royal house, carried off for his people's sins, who refused the king's table and was found faithful in a hostile court. He prefigures a greater Son of David. Jesus was tempted with bread in the wilderness and answered, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matt 4:4), and he said, "My food is to do the will of him who sent me" (John 4:34). Where Daniel stayed undefiled in Babylon, Jesus lived wholly undefiled in a fallen world, "holy, innocent, unstained" (Heb 7:26), and his faithfulness counts for his people. The God who "gave" Judah into Babylon's hand and "gave" Daniel favor and wisdom is the same God who "gave his only Son" (John 3:16), so that exiles might come home.
