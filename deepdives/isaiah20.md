*Setting:* Chapter 20 is a short prose story with a firm date. In 713 BC Ashdod, one of the five Philistine cities on the coast west of Judah, rebelled against Assyria. Its rebel king, Yamani, counted on help from Egypt and Cush, the Nile powers then ruled by the Cushite (Twenty-fifth) dynasty. Assyrian records say Ashdod also invited Judah, Edom and Moab to join. Judah's King Hezekiah was under real pressure to sign on. For three years, while the revolt brewed and was crushed, Isaiah walked the streets of Jerusalem "naked and barefoot" as a living sign. In 711 BC the Assyrian army took Ashdod. The message for Judah was plain: Egypt and Cush, the powers everyone hoped in, would themselves be led away as captives. Chapters 18–19 announced God's dealings with Cush and Egypt. This chapter shows what trusting them would look like up close.

#### A Sign Against Egypt and Cush (vv1–6)
- **"In the year that the commander in chief, who was sent by Sargon the king of Assyria, came to Ashdod" (v1).** "Commander in chief" translates *tartan*, an Assyrian title (*turtanu*) for the army's top general, not a personal name. Older versions print "Tartan" as a name. The same office appears in 2 Kings 18:17, when another *tartan* led Sennacherib's forces to Jerusalem.
- **Sargon II.** He ruled Assyria from 722 to 705 BC and finished the conquest of Samaria. This is the only place the Bible names him. For a long time scholars knew nothing of him outside this verse, and some doubted he existed. Then in the 1840s archaeologists uncovered his huge palace at Khorsabad, near Mosul in Iraq, with inscriptions describing his campaigns, including the capture of Ashdod. JFB mentions those Khorsabad inscriptions, which claim 27,280 Israelites were deported from Samaria. Sargon's own records also report that Yamani of Ashdod fled to the border of Egypt and Cush, and that the king of Cush handed him over to Assyria in chains. The great power Ashdod trusted gave up its fugitive to save itself.
- **"Ashdod… fought against it and captured it" (v1).** Ashdod lay about 35 miles west of Jerusalem near the coast, on the main road to Egypt. It was where the Philistines once took the ark into the temple of Dagon (1 Sam 5:1–5). Its fall put Assyria's army close to Judah and on the road to Egypt.
- **"Loose the sackcloth from your waist and take off your sandals from your feet" (v2).** Sackcloth was a rough garment of goat or camel hair. The Tyndale notes say prophets characteristically wore it as a sign of mourning over the people's sin (compare Elijah and John the Baptist, 2 Kgs 1:8; Mark 1:6). Removing it and his sandals left Isaiah dressed like a prisoner of war.
- **"Walking naked and barefoot" (v2).** The Hebrew *'arom* can mean completely naked, but it is often used of someone stripped to the undergarment, wearing only a short tunic (compare John 21:7, where Peter was "stripped for work"). JFB, Guzik and most commentators take it this way: Isaiah took off the outer garment and kept the inner one. The Tyndale notes allow either possibility. Either way, for a respected man of Jerusalem it was deeply shameful. In the ancient world, being stripped and barefoot was what happened to captives and slaves.
- **"And he did so" (v2).** Three short words of obedience. Isaiah does not argue. Other prophets acted out humiliating signs too: Jeremiah wore a yoke (Jer 27:2), Ezekiel lay on his side for months (Ezek 4:4–6), Hosea married an unfaithful wife (Hos 1:2), and Micah went "stripped and naked" in mourning (Mic 1:8). Isaiah had already said, "I and the children whom the LORD has given me are signs and portents in Israel" (8:18).
- **"As my servant Isaiah has walked naked and barefoot for three years" (v3).** God calls him "my servant," the honored title used for Moses, David and later the great Servant of chapters 42–53. The sign was costly, and God honors the one who carried it. "Three years" probably covers the whole period of the Ashdod crisis, about 713–711 BC. JFB and Guzik suggest he did not go about this way every hour of every day but at intervals, enough to keep the sign before the people.
- **"A sign and a portent against Egypt and Cush" (v3).** A "sign" (*'ot*) is a visible pointer to something beyond itself. A "portent" (*mofet*) is a striking warning. Isaiah's body becomes a sermon. Words could be ignored; a half-dressed prophet in the streets for three years could not.
- **"So shall the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptian captives and the Cushite exiles… naked and barefoot, with buttocks uncovered" (v4).** Assyrian palace reliefs show exactly this: lines of prisoners stripped, barefoot, and roped together as they are marched away. JFB notes that such captives also appear on Egyptian monuments. The phrase "the nakedness of Egypt" is a deliberate shame word. It came true when Esarhaddon invaded Egypt in 671 BC and Ashurbanipal sacked Thebes in 663 BC, taking captives "young and old."
- **"They shall be dismayed and ashamed because of Cush their hope and of Egypt their boast" (v5).** "They" are those who trusted Egypt and Cush: Ashdod and the other small states, and anyone in Judah tempted to do the same. "Their hope" is literally "their expectation," what they kept looking toward. "Their boast" is what they gloried in. Isaiah later says the same in sharper words: "the protection of Pharaoh shall turn to your shame" (30:3).
- **"The inhabitants of this coastland will say in that day" (v6).** The "coastland" is the Philistine coast, and perhaps the whole Mediterranean seaboard. They will look at captive Egyptians and see their own future: "this is what has happened to those in whom we hoped."
- **"And we, how shall we escape?" (v6).** The chapter ends with a question, not an answer. If Egypt could not save itself, it could not save Ashdod. The implied answer for Judah is the one Isaiah gave from the start: "In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength" (30:15). Hezekiah apparently stayed out of the Ashdod revolt, but a decade later, after Sargon's death, he did join an anti-Assyrian alliance and paid dearly for it (ch 36).
- **Commentators:** Henry describes the sign as "the prophet's going for some time barefoot and almost naked, like a poor captive," and draws the lesson "never to trust in an arm of flesh, because thus it will deceive them." JFB explains *'arom* as "uncovered," with Isaiah keeping "the tunic or inner vest," and reads "three years" as the duration of a sign performed "at intervals." JFB also thinks the *tartan* may have been the same general later sent against Hezekiah, though the word is a title, so it could have been a different man holding the same office. Guzik sets the events in 711 BC and stresses the personal cost to Isaiah. For Judah, caught between empires, the point was that Egypt offered no real security; only God did.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah, "my servant," was stripped and shamed in public so that his people would be warned and turn to God instead of to Egypt. Centuries later the true Servant was stripped of his garments before a watching crowd (John 19:23–24) and bore shame he did not deserve, "despised and rejected by men" (53:3). Isaiah's sign warned of captivity; Christ's shame broke it. The chapter's closing question, "How shall we escape?", finds its answer in him: "How shall we escape if we neglect such a great salvation?" (Heb 2:3).
