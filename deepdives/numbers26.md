*Setting:* The plains of Moab, across the Jordan from Jericho, in the fortieth year after the exodus. The plague that followed Israel's sin at Peor has just ended (ch 25). Almost forty years earlier, at Sinai, God had Moses count the men of fighting age (ch 1). That generation refused to enter the land, and God said they would die in the wilderness (14:29–35). Now God orders a second count. It is a turning point: the book began with the census of a generation that would fail, and this second half begins with the census of the generation that will enter. The list itself is mostly names of clans and tribal totals. What matters is what the numbers show, and the short notes woven between them.

#### Census of the New Generation (vv1–65)
- **"After the plague" (v1).** The census follows directly on the last judgment of the wilderness years. The count shows who is left, and that God has kept his people through everything.
- **"Moses and… Eleazar the son of Aaron, the priest" (v1).** At Sinai it was Moses and Aaron (1:3). Aaron has died (20:28), and his son has taken his place. Even the leadership marks a new generation.
- **"All in Israel who are able to go to war" (v2).** Like the first census, this is a military muster, men twenty and older. But this one has a second purpose too: it is the basis for dividing the land (vv52–56). The first count prepared an army that would not fight. This one prepares an army that will, and then settle.
- **The clan names (vv5–50).** The clans are named after the sons and grandsons of Jacob, matching closely the list of those who went down to Egypt (Gen 46:8–27), with some differences in spelling and a few in membership, as clans died out or grew. They show that the promise to Abraham's family has carried through four hundred years in Egypt and forty in the desert. This is the same family God promised to bless, now a nation.
- **Dathan and Abiram, Reubenites (vv8–10).** The list pauses on Reuben to recall the rebels who joined Korah against Moses and were swallowed by the earth (16:1–35). **"They became a warning."** The census is not only a count of the living but a reminder of what rebellion costs.
- **"But the sons of Korah did not die" (v11).** Korah's sons were spared. Their descendants became temple singers and gatekeepers, and eleven psalms bear their name (Pss 42, 44–49, 84–85, 87–88). Among them is "As a deer pants for flowing streams" (Ps 42). From a family nearly wiped out by judgment came some of Israel's best-loved worship songs.
- **Judah's lost sons (v19).** "Er and Onan died in the land of Canaan," a reminder of the sad story in Genesis 38. The line of Judah continued through Perez (v20), the ancestor of David and of Jesus (Ruth 4:18–22; Matt 1:3).
- **The daughters of Zelophehad (v33).** A man with no sons, only five daughters, named one by one. Their names are a set-up for the next chapter, where they ask for their father's inheritance, and win (27:1–11).
- **"The daughter of Asher was Serah" (v46).** One of the very few women named in a tribal list, as in Genesis 46:17. Nothing more is known of her. Later Jewish legend made much of her, but the text is silent.
- **Rising and falling tribes.** Comparing the two censuses (ch 1 and ch 26): seven tribes grew and five shrank.
- **Simeon fell from 59,300 to 22,200 (v14),** a loss of nearly two-thirds. Zimri, the leader killed at Peor, was a Simeonite (25:14), and many think the plague fell hardest on his tribe. In Moses' blessing of the tribes (Deut 33), Simeon is not even mentioned, and later it was absorbed into Judah's territory (Josh 19:1, 9).
- **Manasseh rose from 32,200 to 52,700 (v34),** the largest gain. Ephraim, Joseph's other son, fell from 40,500 to 32,500 (v37). Jacob had put the younger Ephraim ahead of Manasseh (Gen 48:19), and Ephraim did become the greater tribe later, but at this moment the order is reversed.
- **Judah is still the largest tribe, at 76,500 (v22).** In the first count it was also first, at 74,600. Jacob's promise that the scepter would belong to Judah (Gen 49:10) has its numbers behind it.
- **"601,730" (v51).** The first total was 603,550 (1:46). After forty years, the nation is almost exactly the same size, down 1,820. A whole generation died, and a whole generation replaced it. God did not let Israel grow in the desert, but he did not let it disappear either. The faithlessness of the parents did not cancel God's promise to the children.
- **The land divided by size and by lot (vv52–56).** Two principles at once: larger tribes get larger territory, and the lot decides where. Casting lots, probably with marked stones, was a way of letting God decide and preventing favoritism (Prov 16:33; Josh 14:2; Acts 1:26). Joshua 13–19 records how it was carried out.
- **The Levites (vv57–62).** They are counted separately, from a month old, as before (3:15), because they will receive no tribal territory. The LORD is their inheritance (18:20), and they will be given towns to live in (35:1–8). Their number is 23,000, up from 22,000 (3:39).
- **"Jochebed the daughter of Levi, who was born to Levi in Egypt" (v59).** Moses' mother is named here, along with Aaron and Miriam. If "daughter of Levi" means literally Levi's daughter, she would have been very old when Moses was born. Many think it means a woman of Levi's line, as Hebrew genealogies often skip generations and "daughter" can mean descendant (compare Luke 1:5, "the daughters of Aaron").
- **"Nadab and Abihu died when they offered unauthorized fire" (v61).** Another warning written into the family list (Lev 10:1–2).
- **"Not one of them was left, except Caleb… and Joshua" (vv64–65).** This is the point of the chapter. Everything God said in chapter 14 has happened: the men who refused to enter the land have died, and the two who trusted him remain. God's warnings come true as surely as his promises. The statement concerns the tribes counted for war. Eleazar and other Levites, who were not counted at Sinai with the twelve tribes, were still alive.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "the sum total was nearly the same," yet seven tribes increased, and that "none of the tribes decreased so much as Simeon did." On Manasseh he writes, "when the Lord saw that Manasseh was despised, he thus multiplied him exceedingly." On Korah's sons, Henry says "they died not… doubtless because they kept themselves pure from the infection." JFB offers two possibilities: they "were not parties to their father's crime," or they "withdrew from it by timely repentance." JFB also links Simeon's loss to the guilt of Baal-peor, says the lot settled "the section of the country" each tribe would get but not its size, and points out that v64 "must not be considered absolute," since Eleazar and Ithamar were still living. Guzik reads the nearly unchanged total as a picture of the wilderness years: no growth, only survival. He adds that Israel was well organized at the first census too, but organization cannot replace trust in God.

#### Pointing to Christ
A list of names is not the most exciting chapter in the Bible, but it carries good news. God kept every word, both the warning and the promise. The generation of unbelief died, and still the family of Abraham came to the edge of the land intact, ready to inherit. The line of Judah through Perez is here, pointing toward David and toward Jesus. The sons of Korah were spared from judgment and became singers of God's praise. Hebrews uses this very generation as a warning and a hope: "They were unable to enter because of unbelief," but "a Sabbath rest remains for the people of God" (Heb 3:19; 4:9). Jesus, the true Joshua, leads his people into that rest, and he knows his own by name (John 10:3).
