*Setting:* Jerusalem, sometime in the early-to-mid 30s AD. Stephen, one of the seven chosen to serve the church's widows (6:5), is on trial before the Sanhedrin, the council that condemned Jesus. False witnesses have charged him with speaking against Moses, the law and the temple, and with saying that Jesus would destroy "this place" (6:13–14). His face shines like an angel's (6:15). This chapter is his answer, the longest speech in Acts, and it ends with the first Christian martyrdom. A young man named Saul is watching.

#### Stephen's Speech (vv1–53)
- **"Are these things so?" (v1).** The high priest, probably still Caiaphas, gives him the floor. Stephen does not answer point by point. He retells Israel's story, and the story itself becomes his answer and then his charge against the court.
- **The shape of the speech.** Stephen walks through three eras: the patriarchs (vv2–16), Moses (vv17–43), and the tabernacle and temple (vv44–50), then turns on his hearers (vv51–53). Two threads run through it all. First, God has never been tied to one place: he meets his people in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Midian and the desert, all outside the holy land. Second, Israel has always rejected the deliverers God sent before it recognized them. Joseph and Moses were both rejected by their brothers first.
- **"Brothers and fathers" (v2).** A respectful address. Stephen speaks as a Jew to his own people. Paul opens his defense to a Jerusalem crowd with the same words (22:1).
- **"The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham when he was in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran" (v2).** The speech opens and closes with glory (v55). God revealed himself to Abraham in pagan Mesopotamia, far from any temple. Genesis 12:1 tells the call after the family reached Haran, but Genesis 15:7 and Nehemiah 9:7 say God brought Abram "out from Ur of the Chaldeans," so the call began there. Philo and Josephus, Jewish writers of the time, tell it the same way.
- **"After his father died" (v4).** By the ages in Genesis (11:26, 32; 12:4), Terah seems to have lived for decades after Abram left Haran. But the Samaritan version of Genesis gives Terah a shorter life, and Philo also says Abraham left after his father's death, so Stephen may be following a known reading. Or Terah may not have been Abram's eldest son, so the years work out differently.
- **"Not even a foot's length" (v5).** Abraham lived in the promised land for a century and owned none of it except a grave. His faith rested on a promise, not a deed of ownership (Heb 11:8–10).
- **"Four hundred years… and worship me in this place" (vv6–7).** From Genesis 15:13–14, a round number (Exod 12:40 gives 430). The final words are Stephen's blend of Genesis with Exodus 3:12, where God says Israel will worship "on this mountain," Sinai. God's own worship began in the wilderness.
- **"The covenant of circumcision" (v8).** Given to Abraham before there was a temple or a law of Moses (Gen 17). Stephen lays the groundwork for v51: Israel bears the sign in the flesh but is "uncircumcised in heart."
- **"The patriarchs, jealous of Joseph, sold him into Egypt; but God was with him" (v9).** The first rejected deliverer. His own brothers sold him, and God was with him in Egypt, outside the land. The word "jealous" is the same one used of the high priest's party in 5:17.
- **"On the second visit Joseph made himself known to his brothers" (v13).** The first time they did not recognize him. Only on the second visit did they see who he was. Stephen's hearers had missed Jesus the first time.
- **"Seventy-five persons in all" (v14).** The Hebrew text of Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 says seventy. The Greek Old Testament says seventy-five, adding five of Joseph's grandsons and great-grandsons, and a Dead Sea Scrolls copy of Exodus also reads seventy-five. Stephen, a Greek-speaking Jew, quotes the Bible his hearers knew. The two numbers simply count different people.
- **"Carried back to Shechem… the tomb that Abraham had bought… from the sons of Hamor" (v16).** In Genesis, Abraham bought the cave of Machpelah at Hebron from Ephron the Hittite (Gen 23), and Jacob was buried there (Gen 50:13). It was Jacob who bought land at Shechem from the sons of Hamor (Gen 33:19), and Joseph was buried there (Josh 24:32). Stephen's summary folds the two purchases and two burials into one sentence. Many see this as the kind of compression common in Jewish retellings of history. Others suggest that Abraham, who built an altar at Shechem (Gen 12:6–7), also bought ground there. A point worth noticing: Shechem was in Samaria, the region despised by Jerusalem. The patriarchs were buried there, not in Judea.
- **"Another king who did not know Joseph" (v18).** From Exodus 1:8. **"Forced our fathers to expose their infants" (v19)**, leaving babies out to die, as Pharaoh commanded in Exodus 1:22.
- **"Beautiful in God's sight" (v20).** Literally "beautiful to God," a Hebrew way of saying exceedingly fine (Exod 2:2; Heb 11:23).
- **"Instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (v22).** Egypt was famous for learning in mathematics, astronomy, medicine and writing. Exodus does not say this, but it follows from being raised in Pharaoh's household, and Philo and Josephus say the same. **"Mighty in his words and deeds."** Moses said he was "slow of speech" (Exod 4:10), but his recorded words in Deuteronomy are powerful. Luke describes Jesus with almost the same phrase: "mighty in deed and word" (Luke 24:19).
- **Moses' life in three forties (vv23, 30, 36).** Forty in Egypt, forty in Midian, forty leading Israel, matching his death at 120 (Deut 34:7). Stephen's forty-year divisions follow a Jewish tradition. Exodus gives only his ages at the start and end.
- **"He supposed that his brothers would understand… but they did not understand" (v25).** This is Stephen's own comment, not in Exodus. It is the key to the whole speech: God was offering salvation through Moses, and Israel did not see it.
- **"Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?" (v27).** Quoted from Exodus 2:14, and then repeated in v35 for emphasis. The man who pushed Moses away was the one in the wrong.
- **"In a flame of fire in a bush" (v30).** God meets Moses at Sinai, desert ground in a foreign land, and calls it **"holy ground"** (v33). Holiness goes wherever God is. That answers the charge that Stephen spoke against "this holy place" (6:13).
- **"This Moses, whom they rejected… this man God sent as both ruler and redeemer" (v35).** Stephen piles up "this" four times in vv35–38. "Redeemer" (*lytrōtēs*) is used only here in the New Testament. The one Israel rejected is the one God made deliverer, which is exactly what Peter said of Jesus (2:36; 4:11).
- **"A prophet like me from your brothers" (v37).** Deuteronomy 18:15, the same verse Peter used (3:22). Moses himself pointed beyond himself. Stephen is not against Moses. He is taking Moses at his word.
- **"The congregation in the wilderness" (v38).** The word is *ekklēsia*, the same word as "church." The KJV translated it "the church in the wilderness." **"Living oracles."** Stephen calls the law living words from God. He honors the law, answering the charge that he blasphemed it.
- **"In their hearts they turned to Egypt" (v39).** Rejection is not only of a man but of a whole way of life. Even with freedom behind them, they wanted their chains.
- **"They made a calf… rejoicing in the works of their hands" (v41).** The golden calf (Exod 32). The phrase "works of their hands" is the language of idols in the prophets (Isa 2:8; Jer 1:16), and it sets up v48, where God does not live in houses "made by hands."
- **"God turned away and gave them over to worship the host of heaven" (v42).** God's judgment was to let them have what they wanted. Paul describes the same pattern (Rom 1:24–28).
- **Amos 5:25–27 (vv42–43).** Stephen quotes the Greek version. The Hebrew names two Mesopotamian star-gods, Sakkuth and Kaiwan, a name for the planet Saturn. The Greek translators read them as "the tent of Moloch" and "the star of Rephan," another name for Saturn. Amos said "beyond Damascus." Stephen says "beyond Babylon," because the judgment ran on past Assyria's exile of the north to Babylon's exile of Judah. Idolatry did not end with the calf. It ran through Israel's whole history.
- **"The tent of witness in the wilderness" (v44).** The tabernacle was God's design, made "according to the pattern" Moses saw (Exod 25:40), and it was portable. It traveled with God's people for centuries.
- **"Until the days of David… But it was Solomon who built a house for him" (vv45–47).** David asked to build God a dwelling (2 Sam 7; Ps 132:5). God said his son would build it. Stephen does not condemn the temple. Solomon himself prayed at its dedication, "heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27).
- **"The Most High does not dwell in houses made by hands" (v48).** "Made by hands" was the stock word for idols in the Greek Old Testament. Stephen's point is pointed: treating the temple as if it contained God turns even God's house into an idol. Paul tells the Athenians the same thing (17:24). Jesus had spoken of a temple "not made with hands," his own raised body (Mark 14:58; John 2:19–21).
- **Isaiah 66:1–2 (vv49–50).** "Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool." The God who made everything cannot be housed by what he made.
- **Was Stephen against the temple?** Some read his speech as rejecting the temple outright. Most see it as relativizing it: the temple was a gift, but a temporary one, and never God's only meeting place. Its purpose is fulfilled in Jesus. Within forty years Rome destroyed it (AD 70).
- **"You stiff-necked people, uncircumcised in heart and ears" (v51).** Language straight from Moses and the prophets (Exod 33:5; Deut 10:16; Jer 6:10; 9:26). A stiff-necked ox will not bend to the yoke. The speech has not changed tone. It has reached its point. **"You always resist the Holy Spirit. As your fathers did, so do you."**
- **"Which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute?" (v52).** Jesus said much the same (Luke 11:47–51; 13:34). **"The Righteous One"** is a title for the Messiah (3:14; 22:14; Isa 53:11). They have now "betrayed and murdered" him.
- **"You who received the law as delivered by angels and did not keep it" (v53).** Jewish tradition held that angels were present at Sinai and helped give the law (Deut 33:2 in the Greek; Gal 3:19; Heb 2:2). The final reversal: they charged Stephen with breaking the law, but they are the lawbreakers.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Stephen meant to show he "deeply reverenced" Israel's history and that resisting the gospel repeated its old pattern, and Guzik agrees that the speech is more proclamation of Christ than self-defense. Both see Joseph and Moses as pictures of Christ rejected first and recognized later. On the seventy-five, Guzik says the number is not wrong, only counted another way, following the Greek Old Testament, as the Tyndale notes also explain. JFB takes the four hundred years as "a round number" and points out that Amos's "Damascus" becomes "Babylon" in Stephen's quote. JFB says nothing on the Shechem verse. Matthew Henry: "God owned Joseph in his troubles, and was with him by the power of his Spirit," and on the temple, "It was no dishonour, but an honour to God, that the tabernacle gave way to the temple."

#### The Stoning of Stephen (vv54–60)
- **"They were enraged, and they ground their teeth at him" (v54).** "Enraged" is the same "sawn through" as 5:33. Grinding teeth is a picture of fury (Ps 35:16; 37:12).
- **"He, full of the Holy Spirit, gazed into heaven and saw the glory of God" (v55).** The speech began with "the God of glory" appearing to Abraham. It ends with Stephen seeing that glory himself. The council sees an angry room. Stephen sees heaven.
- **"Jesus standing at the right hand of God" (vv55–56).** Everywhere else Jesus is *seated* at God's right hand (2:34; Heb 1:3). Why standing? Several suggestions, which can overlap: rising to welcome his first martyr home; standing as a witness or advocate in the heavenly court, as Jesus promised to acknowledge before the Father those who acknowledge him (Luke 12:8); or standing as judge over the council that is about to condemn his servant.
- **"The Son of Man standing" (v56).** This is the only time in the New Testament someone other than Jesus calls him "the Son of Man." Before this same council Jesus had said, "from now on the Son of Man shall be seated at the right hand of the power of God" (Luke 22:69). They called it blasphemy then. Stephen now says he sees it. The title comes from Daniel 7:13–14, a heavenly figure given everlasting rule.
- **"They cried out with a loud voice and stopped their ears" (v57).** They will not hear what sounds to them like blasphemy, and they drown it out. Stephen had called them "uncircumcised in… ears" (v51), and now they block them. **"Rushed together at him"** uses *hormaō*, the verb for the pigs rushing down the bank in Mark 5:13.
- **"They cast him out of the city and stoned him" (v58).** The law required execution for blasphemy by stoning outside the camp (Lev 24:14–16). Jesus too suffered "outside the gate" (Heb 13:12). Under Rome, the Jewish council was not allowed to carry out executions (John 18:31), so this was most likely a mob action that skipped any formal verdict. Some think it happened in AD 36, when Pilate had just been removed and Roman control in Judea was briefly weak.
- **"The witnesses laid down their garments" (v58).** The law required the witnesses to throw the first stones (Deut 17:7). They took off their outer cloaks to throw more freely.
- **"At the feet of a young man named Saul" (v58).** Luke's first mention of the man who will dominate the second half of Acts. "Young man" (*neanias*) could describe a man anywhere from his twenties to about forty. Saul later says, "I cast my vote against them" (26:10), which some take to mean he sat on the Sanhedrin, though it may mean only that he approved. He never forgot this day: "when the blood of Stephen your witness was being shed, I myself was standing by and approving" (22:20).
- **"Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (v59).** On the cross Jesus prayed, "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46, from Ps 31:5). Stephen prays the same prayer to Jesus. A Jew who had just been accused of blasphemy entrusts his soul to Jesus as he would to God.
- **"Lord, do not hold this sin against them" (v60).** Echoing Jesus' "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). Stephen kneels, the posture of prayer, and cries it out loudly, so everyone hears. One of those who heard was Saul. Augustine said that if Stephen had not prayed, the church would not have had Paul.
- **"He fell asleep" (v60).** The New Testament often describes believers' death this way (1 Thess 4:13–14; 1 Cor 15:20), because death for them is not the end and waking is coming. The violent scene closes with a word of peace.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads Jesus standing as showing "the eager interest with which He watched from the skies," and Guzik pictures it as Jesus rising in honor of his servant, both close to the Tyndale notes' view that he stood to welcome and defend Stephen. JFB notes that "fell asleep" is "never said of the death of Christ," because his death was death in full. Guzik notes that *hormaō* in v57 is the word used of the herd of pigs, and the lexicon bears that out. He also takes "young man" to mean a man in his prime, old enough perhaps to sit on the council, which is possible but not certain. Matthew Henry: "Stephen offered up two short prayers in his dying moments," one for himself and one for his killers. Guzik passes on Augustine's line about Paul.

#### Pointing to Christ
Stephen's history lesson is really about Jesus. Joseph, rejected by his brothers and later their savior; Moses, pushed away and later sent back as ruler and redeemer; the Prophet Moses promised; the true dwelling of God, not made by hands. Israel's story kept saying the same thing: God's deliverer is rejected first. And Stephen's death retells Jesus' own: false witnesses, a council, a charge about the temple, a body taken outside the city, a spirit committed and a prayer for forgiveness. Over it all stands the Son of Man in glory, the one Stephen preached, now welcoming him home.
