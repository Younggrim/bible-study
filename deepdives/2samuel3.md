*Setting:* About 1010–1003 BC, during David's seven and a half years as king of Judah in Hebron (2:11). After Saul's death the kingdom split in two. Judah made David king, while Abner, Saul's cousin and army commander, set up Saul's son Ish-bosheth as king over the northern tribes from Mahanaim, east of the Jordan (2:8–9). The two sides met at Gibeon, where a contest of champions turned into a battle. Abner's men were routed, and Abner killed Asahel, the youngest brother of David's commander Joab (2:18–23). This chapter shows the north starting to come apart from the inside. Abner falls out with his puppet king, offers to bring all Israel over to David, and then is murdered by Joab before he can finish. Through it all the narrator works hard to show one thing: David had no part in the killing.

#### David's Sons Born at Hebron (vv1–5)
- **"There was a long war between the house of Saul and the house of David" (v1).** The ceasefire at Gibeon (2:28) did not end the fighting. "House" here means royal family and its supporters. The verse sums up years of conflict in one sentence: David "grew stronger and stronger," while Saul's house "became weaker and weaker." The rest of the chapter shows how.
- **"Sons were born to David at Hebron" (v2).** Six sons, from six different mothers. In the ancient world a king's growing family was a sign of strength, and the narrator places the list right after "David grew stronger." But readers who know the story ahead will wince. Deuteronomy 17:17 warned that Israel's king "shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away." These sons will bring David some of his deepest grief.
- **"Amnon, of Ahinoam of Jezreel" (v2).** The firstborn. He will rape his half-sister Tamar and be murdered for it (ch 13).
- **"Chileab, of Abigail" (v3).** Abigail was Nabal's widow, the wise woman of 1 Samuel 25. 1 Chronicles 3:1 calls this son Daniel. He is never mentioned again, and most think he died young.
- **"Absalom the son of Maacah the daughter of Talmai king of Geshur" (v3).** Geshur was a small Aramean kingdom northeast of the Sea of Galilee. This was a political marriage, sealing an alliance on the far side of Ish-bosheth's territory. Absalom will kill Amnon, flee to his grandfather in Geshur (13:37–38), and later lead a rebellion against his father (chs 15–18).
- **"Adonijah the son of Haggith" (v4).** He will try to seize the throne when David is old, and Solomon will have him executed (1 Kgs 1–2). So three of these six sons die violent deaths, and all three are tied to grabbing for power or pleasure.
- **"Eglah, David's wife" (v5).** Only she is called "David's wife," which has puzzled readers. Some Jewish interpreters thought she was Michal under another name, but Michal had no children (6:23). More likely it is just the way the list ends.
- **Commentators:** Guzik is direct: David's many wives broke God's command for kings (Deut 17:17), and the troubles that came through Amnon, Absalom and Adonijah were the fruit of it. JFB notes the political marriage to Maacah and gives a short verdict: "Piety was made to yield to policy." Henry focuses instead on the long war: "The length of this war tried the faith and patience of David, and made his settlement at last the more welcome."

#### Abner Joins David (vv6–21)
- **"Abner was making himself strong in the house of Saul" (v6).** Abner is the real power in the north. Ish-bosheth wears the title.
- **"Saul had a concubine whose name was Rizpah" (v7).** A concubine was a secondary wife, often from a lower social standing, without a full wife's rights. When a king died, his wives and concubines passed to his successor, so sleeping with a dead king's concubine was a way of claiming his throne. Absalom will do it publicly to David's concubines (16:21–22), and Solomon reads Adonijah's request for David's nurse Abishag the same way (1 Kgs 2:22). Whether Abner really did it, the text does not say. Rizpah will appear again, unforgettably, guarding her sons' bodies (21:8–14).
- **"Am I a dog's head of Judah?" (v8).** Calling someone a dog was a deep insult (1 Sam 17:43; 24:14). Abner means "Do you think I'm a traitor working for Judah?" Then he lists his loyalty: "I keep showing steadfast love (*hesed*) to the house of Saul." It is true that he alone had kept Saul's house alive. His pride is wounded, and he lets Ish-bosheth know he holds his life in his hands.
- **"What the LORD has sworn to him" (v9).** Abner admits that he has known all along that God promised the kingdom to David. "From Dan to Beersheba" (v10) is the standard way of saying the whole land, from the far north to the far south. If he knew this, his years of fighting for Saul's son were fighting against God's word. He changes sides now not because he repents but because he has been insulted.
- **"Ish-bosheth could not answer Abner another word, because he feared him" (v11).** Saul's son is a king who cannot speak to his own general. The silence says it all.
- **"To whom does the land belong?" (v12).** Abner's message is ambiguous on purpose. It could mean "the land is yours, David," or "the land is mine to deliver." Either way he wants a covenant, a formal agreement. He likely hopes to be David's commander over all Israel.
- **"Bring Michal, Saul's daughter" (v13).** David's condition is his first wife. Saul had given Michal to David after David paid an extraordinary bride-price of Philistine foreskins (1 Sam 18:25–27). Saul had asked for a hundred, and David brought two hundred; here David claims only the hundred that was agreed. After Michal helped David escape, Saul gave her to another man (1 Sam 25:44). By reclaiming her, David puts right a wrong done to him and also strengthens his claim to Saul's throne as Saul's son-in-law.
- **"Ish-bosheth sent and took her" (v15).** David asks Ish-bosheth directly, through official channels, and Ish-bosheth obeys. He has no power left.
- **"Her husband went with her, weeping after her all the way to Bahurim" (v16).** This is one of the saddest small scenes in the Bible. Paltiel loved her. He follows, weeping, until Abner orders him home at Bahurim, on the edge of Benjamin's territory near Jerusalem. Michal's feelings are not recorded. She is moved like a piece on a board, and the coldness she shows David later (6:16, 20–23) may have roots here.
- **"For some time past you have been seeking David as king over you" (v17).** Abner tells the northern elders what many already want. He even quotes a promise from the LORD: "By the hand of my servant David I will save my people Israel from the hand of the Philistines" (v18). This exact wording is not found earlier in the Bible, but it sums up what God had said through Samuel (1 Sam 16:1; 15:28). Saul was anointed to save Israel from the Philistines (1 Sam 9:16) and failed. David will do it.
- **"Abner also spoke to Benjamin" (v19).** Saul's own tribe was the hardest to win over, and Abner, as a Benjaminite and Saul's kinsman, was the one who could do it.
- **"David made a feast for Abner" (v20).** A shared meal sealed friendship and agreement. Abner promises to gather all Israel to make a covenant with David. **"So David sent Abner away, and he went in peace" (v21).** The phrase "he went in peace" is repeated three times (vv21, 22, 23). The narrator wants it beyond doubt: David and Abner parted as friends.
- **Commentators:** Guzik says Abner knew the truth about David for years but acted on it only when his pride was hurt, which is knowing God's will without living by it. Henry is blunter: "Many mean to serve their own purposes; and will betray those who trust them, when they can get any advantage." JFB says Abner spoke the truth about David but "acted a base and hypocritical part." On Michal, Henry sees "real generosity both to Michal and to the memory of Saul" in David's taking her back, while JFB calls the demand "perfectly fair" but likely strategic in its timing. Guzik gives both reasons. He also notes that David waited for the tribes to invite him rather than seizing the north by force.

#### Abner Killed by Joab (vv22–30)
- **"Just then the servants of David arrived with Joab from a raid" (v22).** The timing is tense. Joab comes in from a raid with plunder, just after Abner has left.
- **"What have you done?" (v24).** Joab speaks to his king and uncle with no respect at all. He calls Abner a spy who came "to know your going out and your coming in," a Hebrew way of saying all his movements (v25). It is possible he believed it. But he also had two other reasons to want Abner gone. Abner killed his brother Asahel (2:23), and if Abner joined David he would likely replace Joab as commander.
- **"He sent messengers after Abner… But David did not know about it" (v26).** The narrator again shields David. The cistern of Sirah was a well a short distance from Hebron, perhaps about two miles north.
- **"Joab took him aside into the midst of the gate to speak with him privately" (v27).** The city gate was where business was done and justice was given (Ruth 4:1). Joab turns that public place into a private ambush. **"He struck him in the stomach"** is exactly where Abner had struck Asahel (2:23). Joab even uses the same wound. It is revenge, not justice.
- **Was it lawful blood vengeance?** Joab could claim to be the "avenger of blood" for Asahel (Num 35:19). But Abner killed Asahel in battle, in self-defense, after warning him twice (2:21–22). That was not murder. And Hebron was one of Israel's six cities of refuge (Josh 20:7), where a killer was supposed to get a hearing before the congregation (Num 35:12, 24). Joab gave Abner none of that. Later, David will name this killing as shedding "the blood of war in peace" (1 Kgs 2:5).
- **"I and my kingdom are forever guiltless before the LORD" (v28).** David immediately declares his innocence before God.
- **"May it fall upon the head of Joab and upon all his father's house" (v29).** David's curse lists the things that would shame a family line: a man with "a discharge," a bodily flow that made him ritually unclean (Lev 15); one with a skin disease, the Hebrew word covering many conditions besides what we now call leprosy; one "who holds a spindle," which could mean a man fit only for women's work, or, as some translate, one who leans on a crutch; one who dies by violence; one who goes hungry. It is a fierce curse, but it is also all David does. He does not punish Joab.
- **"So Joab and Abishai his brother killed Abner" (v30).** Abishai is named too. The brothers acted together.
- **Commentators:** Guzik lists three motives for Joab: suspicion that Abner was a spy, the duty of a blood avenger, and rivalry for command. He points out that Hebron was a city of refuge and suggests Joab killed Abner at the gate to get around that law, which made the act more calculated. JFB says Joab was "determined, therefore, to get Abner out of the way." Henry: "Judgments are prepared for such scorners as Abner; but Joab, in what he did, acted wickedly." Guzik wonders whether David failed to act against Joab because he could not afford to lose so capable a general.

#### David Mourns Abner (vv31–39)
- **"Tear your clothes and put on sackcloth and mourn before Abner" (v31).** David makes Joab himself put on mourning in public. **"King David followed the bier,"** the open frame on which a body was carried to the grave. A king walking behind the body was an extraordinary honor.
- **"They buried Abner at Hebron" (v32).** He is buried in David's capital, not taken home to Benjamin. David treats him as an honored friend.
- **"Should Abner die as a fool dies?" (v33).** "Fool" is *naval*, the same word as the name of Nabal, Abigail's first husband (1 Sam 25:25). In the Old Testament a "fool" is not just unwise but morally careless. David's short lament says Abner died in a way he did not deserve. "Your hands were not bound; your feet were not fettered" (v34). He was not a captured criminal. He was a free man, tricked and stabbed, "as one falls before the wicked."
- **"David swore… if I taste bread… till the sun goes down" (v35).** He fasts all day. Fasting was a way of showing grief that cost something.
- **"As everything that the king did pleased all the people" (v36).** David's sincerity was visible, and the people trusted him. **"All Israel understood that day that it had not been the king's will to put to death Abner" (v37).** This was essential. If the northern tribes had believed David had their leader murdered, the union of the kingdom could have failed before it began.
- **"A prince and a great man has fallen this day in Israel" (v38).** David honors Abner's greatness, despite all the years he fought against him.
- **"I was gentle today, though anointed king. These men, the sons of Zeruiah, are more severe than I" (v39).** "Gentle" is literally "soft" or "weak." David admits he is not strong enough to punish his own nephews yet. He leaves it with God: "The LORD repay the evildoer according to his wickedness!" But that is not the end. On his deathbed David will tell Solomon not to let Joab "go down to Sheol in peace" (1 Kgs 2:5–6), and Joab will be executed (1 Kgs 2:28–34).
- **Commentators:** JFB says David's "sorrow was sincere and profound," and calls the short lament "an effusion of indignation as much as of sorrow." Henry: "David laid Abner's murder deeply to heart, and in many ways expressed his detestation of it." Guzik reads v39 as an honest admission of how little control David had over his own forces. The Tyndale notes draw the parallel: just as Ish-bosheth could not control Abner, David could not control Joab.

#### Pointing to Christ
God's promise that David would rule all Israel moves forward through proud men, political deals and murder, and none of it stops God's purpose. David himself refuses to win the kingdom by violence and grieves the death of a former enemy. But he is too weak to restrain the "sons of Zeruiah." There will be another King who is never too weak. Jesus rebuked his own followers when they wanted to call fire on their enemies (Luke 9:54–55) and told Peter to put away his sword (Matt 26:52). He builds his kingdom not by violence but by his own death, and he will judge every evildoer in perfect justice (Acts 17:31). David could only pray, "The LORD repay the evildoer." The Son of David will one day do it rightly.
