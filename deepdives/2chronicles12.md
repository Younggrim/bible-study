*Setting:* About 926–925 BC, the fifth year of Rehoboam, king of Judah. For three years after the kingdom split, Judah walked "in the way of David and Solomon," strengthened by faithful priests, Levites and worshipers who came south from Jeroboam's kingdom (11:13–17). Rehoboam fortified fifteen cities against attack from the south and west (11:5–12). Now the fortified, secure kingdom turns away from God, and the attack it prepared for arrives. Kings tells the same events briefly (1 Kgs 14:21–31). Chronicles adds the prophet's message, the repentance and God's answer, and so turns a raid into a lesson the post-exile readers knew from their own history: unfaithfulness brings judgment, and humbling yourself before God brings mercy.

#### Egypt Plunders Jerusalem (vv1–12)
- **"When the rule of Rehoboam was established and he was strong, he abandoned the law of the LORD" (v1).** The danger came not in weakness but in strength. Moses warned of exactly this: when you have eaten and are full and your silver and gold multiply, "take care lest you forget the LORD your God" (Deut 8:11–14). The word "abandoned" (*'azab*, forsake) is a key word in Chronicles, and it will come back in v5.
- **What the abandonment looked like.** Kings fills in the details: Judah built high places, sacred pillars and Asherah poles "on every high hill and under every green tree," and there were male cult prostitutes in the land (1 Kgs 14:22–24). Chronicles simply calls it abandoning the law.
- **"And all Israel with him" (v1).** The people followed the king. Again, the Chronicler calls Judah "Israel," because the faithful of every tribe had gathered there.
- **"Because they had been unfaithful to the LORD" (v2).** "Unfaithful" (*ma'al*) means to break faith, to betray a trust. It is the Chronicler's word for the sin that finally sent Judah into exile (1 Chr 9:1; 2 Chr 36:14). Kings mentions Shishak's invasion without giving a reason. Chronicles gives the reason plainly.
- **"Shishak king of Egypt" (v2).** He is Shoshenq I, founder of Egypt's Twenty-Second Dynasty, a Libyan-descended ruler who reigned roughly 945–924 BC and reunited a divided Egypt. He had sheltered Jeroboam when Solomon tried to kill him (1 Kgs 11:40). He is the first Pharaoh in the Bible whose personal name is given, and the first point at which biblical history can be firmly matched to Egyptian records.
- **Archaeology.** On the wall of the great temple of Amun at Karnak (in Thebes, modern Luxor), Shoshenq carved a victory relief listing about 150 places he conquered in Canaan. Many are in the Negev and in the northern kingdom, including Megiddo, where a fragment of a Shoshenq victory stele has been found. Jerusalem does not appear clearly on the surviving list. That fits this chapter well: Jerusalem was not taken by storm. Rehoboam paid it off.
- **"1,200 chariots and 60,000 horsemen. And the people were without number" (v3).** A huge army by the standards of the day. The numbers are given only in Chronicles.
- **"Libyans, Sukkiim, and Ethiopians" (v3).** Libyans came from west of Egypt, Shishak's own ancestral people. The Sukkiim are named only here. Egyptian texts mention *Tjukten*, scouts or auxiliaries from the Libyan desert oases, and many scholars connect the two. "Ethiopians" translates *Cushites*, from Cush, the Nile lands south of Egypt (modern Sudan), not modern Ethiopia.
- **"He took the fortified cities of Judah and came as far as Jerusalem" (v4).** The forts Rehoboam had built so carefully fell one after another. Walls were no defense once Judah had abandoned the LORD.
- **"Shemaiah the prophet came to Rehoboam and to the princes of Judah" (v5).** The same prophet who stopped Rehoboam's civil war (11:2–4). The leaders had gathered in Jerusalem as refugees from the fallen towns.
- **"You abandoned me, so I have abandoned you to the hand of Shishak" (v5).** The same verb, *'azab*, both times. The punishment matches the sin. This is the warning Azariah will give Asa a generation later: "If you seek him, he will be found by you, but if you forsake him, he will forsake you" (15:2; see also 1 Chr 28:9).
- **"The princes of Israel and the king humbled themselves and said, 'The LORD is righteous'" (v6).** They do not blame Egypt or bad luck. They agree with God's verdict: he is in the right, and we are in the wrong. "Humbled themselves" (*kana'*) is the first word of God's great promise to Solomon at the temple's dedication: "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land" (7:14). This chapter is the first test case of that promise after Solomon, and it works.
- **"They have humbled themselves. I will not destroy them, but I will grant them some deliverance" (v7).** "Some deliverance" is literally "a little escape" or "deliverance in a little while." God's answer is quick but partial. Jerusalem is not destroyed.
- **"Nevertheless, they shall be servants to him, that they may know my service and the service of the kingdoms of the countries" (v8).** This is one of the most striking lines in Chronicles. Judah had grown tired of serving the LORD. Now it will taste what serving a foreign king is like, and learn the difference. The Hebrew word for "service" (*'abodah*) is the same word used for the Levites' work in the temple (1 Chr 23:28). The post-exile readers had lived for generations under Babylon and Persia and knew exactly what this meant (Neh 9:36–37).
- **"He took away the treasures of the house of the LORD and the treasures of the king's house. He took away everything" (v9).** Solomon's vast wealth (ch 9) was gone within five years of his death. Kings records the same stripping (1 Kgs 14:26). The phrase "he took away everything" is blunt.
- **"The shields of gold that Solomon had made" (v9).** Solomon made 200 large shields and 300 small ones of beaten gold for the House of the Forest of Lebanon (9:15–16). Gold is too soft and heavy for battle, so these were ceremonial, a display of royal glory.
- **"Rehoboam made in their place shields of bronze" (v10).** Bronze shields replaced gold ones, kept in the guardroom and carried out only when the king went to the temple (v11). Everything looked much the same from a distance, but the glory was gone. It is a sad picture of keeping up appearances after the reality has been lost.
- **"When he humbled himself the wrath of the LORD turned from him, so as not to make a complete destruction" (v12).** The Chronicler repeats the lesson so it cannot be missed.
- **"Moreover, conditions were good in Judah" (v12).** Literally "in Judah there were good things." It could mean that some prosperity remained, or that there was still some good in Judah, faithful people and true worship. Chronicles uses the same phrase in 19:3 when a prophet tells Jehoshaphat, "some good is found in you." The second reading is more likely, but both may be in view.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry sees v1 as the proverb in action, "the prosperity of fools destroys them" (Prov 1:32), and on v5 writes, "God never leaves any till they first leave him." On the repentance: "Even kings and princes must either bend or break before God." On v8, "The service of virtue is perfect liberty; the service of lust is perfect slavery." Guzik makes the same point through Adam Clarke, and through Trapp adds that some people learn the worth of God's service only by bitter experience. Guzik dwells on the bronze shields as a picture of a church covering spiritual loss with imitation, quoting Payne and G. Campbell Morgan. He also points out that the problem started with Solomon's gold shields, glory for display rather than for use. JFB notes the cause of Shishak's attack is not stated in human terms, and identifies the Sukkiim either as tent-dwelling Arabs or, as it prefers, "Arab troglodytes" living in caves near the Red Sea. The Tyndale notes connect them instead with Libyans from the desert oases, which is the view most modern scholars take.

#### Rehoboam's Reign Summarized (vv13–16)
- **"So King Rehoboam grew strong in Jerusalem and reigned" (v13).** After the humbling he recovered. The chapter that began with a dangerous kind of strength (v1) ends with a chastened one.
- **"Forty-one years old when he began to reign, and he reigned seventeen years" (v13).** About 930–913 BC. These details usually open a king's account in Kings (1 Kgs 14:21). The Chronicler moves them to the end as a summary.
- **"The city that the LORD had chosen out of all the tribes of Israel to put his name there" (v13).** A reminder of Jerusalem's high calling, which makes Rehoboam's unfaithfulness worse. To "put his name" in a place means God chose to be known and worshiped there (Deut 12:5; 2 Chr 6:6).
- **"His mother's name was Naamah the Ammonite" (v13).** Mentioning the king's mother is normal in Kings and Chronicles. But this detail is pointed: Naamah came from Ammon, whose god Molech Solomon built a shrine for (1 Kgs 11:5–7). Solomon married her before he became king, since Rehoboam was born a year before Solomon's reign began. Rehoboam grew up with a foreign mother in a court where idolatry was tolerated.
- **"And he did evil, for he did not set his heart to seek the LORD" (v14).** This is the Chronicler's verdict, and his diagnosis. "Set his heart" means to prepare it, to fix it firmly, like setting a foundation. Rehoboam had moments of obedience (11:4) and humility (12:6), but he never settled the direction of his heart. The faithful northerners did exactly what he did not: they "set their hearts to seek the LORD" (11:16). Contrast Jehoshaphat, of whom a prophet says, "you have set your heart to seek God" (19:3), and Ezra, who "had set his heart to study the Law of the LORD" (Ezra 7:10).
- **"The chronicles of Shemaiah the prophet and of Iddo the seer" (v15).** The Chronicler cites written sources often. Here it is the prophets' records. Iddo also wrote about Solomon (9:29) and Abijah (13:22). These works are lost. The Hebrew adds "for genealogical record," which suggests they included family registers.
- **"There were continual wars between Rehoboam and Jeroboam" (v15).** Shemaiah had stopped a full-scale invasion (11:4), but border fighting went on all through both reigns (1 Kgs 14:30; 15:6).
- **"Rehoboam slept with his fathers and was buried in the city of David" (v16).** A standard and peaceful end. "Slept with his fathers" means he died and joined his ancestors. Abijah, Maacah's son, whom Rehoboam had already chosen (11:22), succeeds him.
- **Commentators:** Guzik highlights v14 as the root of everything: Rehoboam never prepared his heart to seek the LORD. He quotes Spurgeon that Rehoboam was always shifting, a man whose religion held him only some of the time. Through Selman, Guzik adds that Chronicles presses repentance and God's mercy harder than Kings does, so that even a failing king becomes an encouragement for later readers. Matthew Henry makes the same diagnosis: "He never quite cast off God," yet "he did evil because he was never determined for that which is good," and "those are easily drawn by Satan to any evil who are wavering and inconstant in that which is good." JFB says Rehoboam "did not adhere firmly to the good course of reformation he had begun," and calls the Egyptian invasion "a mere predatory expedition, not extending beyond the limits of Judah," though Shishak's own Karnak list, read since JFB's day, names many northern towns as well.

#### Pointing to Christ
Rehoboam's bronze shields stood for a glory that had been lost and could only be imitated. Judah learned the hard way that serving God and serving the kingdoms of this world are not the same. Jesus offers the opposite trade: "Take my yoke upon you… for my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matt 11:29–30). The promise of 7:14 held even for a half-hearted king who humbled himself. In Christ the same promise holds without limit: "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble… Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you" (Jas 4:6, 10). The glory Rehoboam lost is restored in the Son of David, in whom "the glory of God" shines (2 Cor 4:6).
