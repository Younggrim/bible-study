*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." It was meant for public worship. Henry thinks it most likely comes from the years when Saul was hunting David, and points to the narrow escapes at Keilah, a walled town whose own citizens were ready to hand David over (1 Sam 23:7–13), and in the wilderness of Maon, where Saul's army was closing in on one side of the mountain while David fled down the other (1 Sam 23:26). The "besieged city" of v21 fits Keilah well, though the psalm does not say. It is a psalm that rides the waves, as Boice (cited by Guzik) puts it: trust (vv1–8), then a plunge into grief and loneliness (vv9–13), then trust again (vv14–18), and finally praise and a call to all God's people (vv19–24). It is also one of the most quoted psalms in the Bible. Jesus prayed v5 with his last breath (Luke 23:46). Jonah prayed parts of it from inside the fish (Jonah 2:4, 8). Jeremiah took its cry "terror on every side" as his own (Jer 20:10). It picks up the language of the psalms before it: "rock," "refuge," "let me not be put to shame" (25:2), and it ends with almost the same words as Psalm 27: "Be strong, and let your heart take courage" (27:14).

#### Into Your Hand I Commit My Spirit (vv1–8)
- **"In you, O LORD, do I take refuge" (v1).** The opening three verses appear again, almost word for word, in Psalm 71:1–3. "Take refuge" (*chasah*) is the word of 2:12, "Blessed are all who take refuge in him." It is the first word of trust in the psalm, and everything else flows from it.
- **"Let me never be put to shame" (v1).** To be "put to shame" is to have your trust exposed as foolish, to be seen relying on someone who let you down. David has staked everything on God, and he asks that God not let that trust be made a joke (25:2–3, 20).
- **"In your righteousness deliver me!" (v1).** God's righteousness here is his faithfulness to do what is right, to keep his promises and defend those who are his. Guzik tells the story of Martin Luther, who as a young professor was terrified by this verse. He could only think of God's righteousness as the justice that condemns sinners. Working through it alongside Romans 1:17, he came to see a righteousness God gives freely to those who believe. Luther later wrote that he felt "reborn," as if he had "gone through open doors into paradise."
- **"Incline your ear to me; rescue me speedily!" (v2).** "Incline your ear" is a picture of God bending down to listen, as an adult leans down to hear a child. "Speedily" shows how close the danger is.
- **"Be a rock of refuge for me… For you are my rock and my fortress" (vv2–3).** David asks God to be what he already is. Boice, cited by Guzik, sees this as the pattern of Christian prayer: "You are… then be." "Fortress" (*metsudah*) is a mountain stronghold, the same word used for the wilderness strongholds where David hid from Saul (1 Sam 22:4; 24:22).
- **"For your name's sake you lead me and guide me" (v3).** The same reason as 23:3: "He leads me in paths of righteousness for his name's sake." God's guidance rests on his own reputation, not on David's worth.
- **"You take me out of the net they have hidden for me" (v4).** Hunters hid nets to trap birds or animals. David's enemies laid hidden traps, as Saul did when he tried to lure David with his daughter Michal, or tried to trap him at Keilah (1 Sam 18:21; 23:7).
- **"Into your hand I commit my spirit" (v5).** "Commit" (*paqad*) means to entrust, to deposit for safekeeping, as one leaves valuables in the care of a trusted friend. "Spirit" (*ruach*) is his life, his very breath. In context David is not dying. He is handing his whole life to God for protection while enemies close in. Jesus took these words onto his lips on the cross, adding the word "Father": "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46). Stephen prayed the same way as he was stoned, but to Jesus: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59). Peter told suffering believers to "entrust their souls to a faithful Creator" (1 Pet 4:19). The line became part of the Jewish prayers said at bedtime, and Christians through the centuries made it their dying prayer. Spurgeon (cited by Guzik) names Polycarp, Bernard, Huss, Luther and Melanchthon. Boice adds that when Jan Huss was condemned and the bishop said, "We commit your soul to the devil," Huss answered with this verse.
- **"You have redeemed me, O LORD, faithful God" (v5).** "Redeemed" (*padah*) means to ransom or rescue. Kidner, cited by Guzik, notes that in the Old Testament it is mostly about rescue from trouble rather than payment for sin. "Faithful God" is literally "God of truth" (*'el 'emet*), the God who is reliable. David entrusts his spirit to God because God has already proved he can be trusted.
- **"I hate those who pay regard to worthless idols" (v6).** Literally "vanities of emptiness" (*havle-shav'*), things that are breath and nothing. Jonah repeats the line: "Those who pay regard to vain idols forsake their hope of steadfast love" (Jonah 2:8). Henry extends it to every kind of false security, omens, fortune-telling, and even "Goliath's sword itself." "But I trust in the LORD" is the contrast. The Hebrew text has "I hate," while the Greek translation and some other ancient versions have "you hate," and some modern translations follow them. Either way, the point is that trusting God means refusing every false refuge.
- **"I will rejoice and be glad in your steadfast love, because you have seen my affliction" (v7).** God *saw* and *knew*. The same verbs are used when God tells Moses, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people… I know their sufferings" (Exod 3:7). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik: "Man, know thyself" is good advice, but "Man, thou art known of God" is a far greater comfort.
- **"You have set my feet in a broad place" (v8).** Distress in Hebrew is a narrow, tight place. Rescue is a wide open one. The Tyndale notes point back to 18:19: "He brought me out into a broad place." "Not delivered me into the hand of the enemy" is literally "not shut me up," the word used when David asked whether the men of Keilah would "surrender" him to Saul (1 Sam 23:11–12). JFB makes the same link.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Faith and prayer must go together. He that believes, let him pray… and he that prays, let him believe." JFB on v5: Jesus used the words "not as prophetical, but, as many pious men have done, as expressive of His unshaken confidence in God." Many other readers see the psalm as truly pointing ahead to Christ. The Tyndale notes say the psalm "foreshadows" his suffering. Both can be held: Jesus prayed the prayer of a righteous sufferer, and in doing so he fulfilled what every righteous sufferer before him had only partly lived. Guzik passes on Spurgeon's point that past redemption is a strong plea for present help.

#### Be Gracious to Me, O LORD (vv9–13)
- **"Be gracious to me, O LORD, for I am in distress" (v9).** The confident tone of vv1–8 suddenly gives way. The distress has not gone away. Psalms are honest that trust and tears can sit side by side. Henry notes that David has moved from appealing to God's righteousness to appealing to his mercy.
- **"My eye is wasted from grief; my soul and my body also" (v9).** "Body" is literally "belly," the inner organs, which Hebrew thought linked with the deepest feelings. The grief is not only in his mind. It shows in his eyes and his body. The Tyndale notes call it a vivid picture of how despair takes a physical toll.
- **"My strength fails because of my iniquity" (v10).** This is the one place in the psalm where David connects his suffering with his own sin. The Hebrew text reads "my iniquity" (*'avoni*). The Greek and Syriac versions read "my affliction" or "poverty," a very similar word in Hebrew (*'oni*), and some translations follow them. JFB allows either sense: "iniquity—or, suffering by it." The Hebrew reading is honest and fits the Psalms well (32:3–5; 38:3–4). Suffering is not always caused by our own sin, but a believer in trouble still confesses whatever sin is there.
- **"I have become a reproach, especially to my neighbors" (v11).** The circle of rejection tightens. First his enemies, then his neighbors, then his acquaintances, and finally strangers in the street who see him coming and cross the road. JFB traces "the progress of his disgrace to the lowest degree." When Saul was hunting David, anyone who helped him risked their life. The priests of Nob were slaughtered for giving him bread (1 Sam 22:18–19). People were afraid to be seen with him.
- **"I have been forgotten like one who is dead; I have become like a broken vessel" (v12).** A broken clay pot was swept up and thrown on the rubbish heap. It had no value and no one mourned it. Jeremiah uses the same picture for King Jehoiachin, "a despised, broken pot" (Jer 22:28).
- **"For I hear the whispering of many—terror on every side!" (v13).** "Terror on every side" (*magor missaviv*) is a phrase Jeremiah uses again and again (Jer 6:25; 20:3, 10; 46:5; 49:29). Jeremiah 20:10 repeats this line almost word for word: "For I hear many whispering. Terror is on every side!" The prophet, also hunted and slandered, found his own experience in David's.
- **"As they scheme together against me, as they plot to take my life" (v13).** This is not just gossip. It is a conspiracy to kill. Saul's officials, the Ziphites and the men of Keilah all plotted to betray David (1 Sam 23:12, 19–20).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that David seems by nature to have been cheerful and bold, a ruddy, musical, daring young man, "yet here we see what he is brought to." He calls him "herein… a type of Christ, who was intimately acquainted with grief." JFB reads "a broken vessel" as "contemned as a useless broken vessel." Guzik passes on Spurgeon: "Of the dead we say nothing but good, but in the Psalmist's case they said nothing but evil."

#### My Times Are in Your Hand (vv14–18)
- **"But I trust in you, O LORD; I say, 'You are my God'" (v14).** The "but" is the turning point of the whole psalm. Everything in vv9–13 is true, and yet. Henry: his enemies "robbed him of his reputation among men, but they could not rob him of his comfort in God."
- **"My times are in your hand" (v15).** "Times" (*'ittot*) means the seasons and events of a life, its turning points, its length, its circumstances. The enemies plot to take his life (v13), but his life is not in their hands. It is in God's. Notice the two hands of vv5 and 15: "into your hand I commit my spirit," "my times are in your hand." Henry has a striking application. When David had Saul in his power in the cave, his men told him, "Here is the day of which the LORD said… I will give your enemy into your hand" (1 Sam 24:4). David refused. His times were in God's hand, and God's time would not require sin.
- **"Rescue me from the hand of my enemies" (v15).** A third hand, the enemy's. The whole question of the psalm is which hand holds David's life.
- **"Make your face shine on your servant" (v16).** These are the words of the priestly blessing: "The LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you" (Num 6:25). A shining face means favor, warmth and acceptance.
- **"Let the wicked be put to shame; let them go silently to Sheol" (v17).** The shame David asked to be spared (v1) he asks God to bring on the wicked. This is a prayer for justice, left in God's hands (see 28:4). The Tyndale notes say David is appealing to God's rule that people reap what they sow.
- **"Let the lying lips be mute" (v18).** Slander has been the weapon all along (v13). David prays that the lying mouths would be shut. In God's courtroom, the false witnesses will have nothing to say.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Thou art my God; I have chosen thee for mine, and thou hast promised to be mine," and "if he be ours… it is enough, when we can call nothing else ours." JFB reads "times" as "course of life" and notes that "deliver… hand" answers the "shut me up" of v8. Guzik passes on Spurgeon: those who believe their times are in God's hand will meet difficulty by saying, "I am now going to see the wonders of God."

#### Oh, How Abundant Is Your Goodness (vv19–24)
- **"Oh, how abundant is your goodness, which you have stored up for those who fear you" (v19).** God's goodness is pictured as treasure "stored up" (*tsaphan*, hidden away, kept safe), like grain in a storehouse. Some of it is kept for later. Henry connects it with the inheritance "kept in heaven for you" (1 Pet 1:4). Some is "worked" openly now, "in the sight of the children of mankind," so that even the watching world can see.
- **"In the cover of your presence you hide them" (v20).** The same root *tsaphan* is used again: God stores up goodness for his people, and he stores his people in safety. "The cover of your presence" is literally "the hiding place of your face." The face that David asked to shine on him (v16) becomes his hiding place. JFB compares the similar picture in 27:5.
- **"You store them in your shelter from the strife of tongues" (v20).** "Shelter" (*sukkah*) is a booth or tent. The danger here is "the strife of tongues," the slander and accusation that run through the whole psalm. God's people may not be able to silence what others say about them, but they can be hidden from its power.
- **"Blessed be the LORD, for he has wondrously shown his steadfast love to me when I was in a besieged city" (v21).** David moves from God's people in general to his own story. "Wondrously" means in a way that marks God's hand. Henry links the "besieged city" with Keilah, where David rescued the town from the Philistines and then escaped before its people could betray him to Saul (1 Sam 23:1–13). Others take the city as a picture of being surrounded. It may be both: God was a fortified city to him even when the real city was a trap.
- **"I had said in my alarm, 'I am cut off from your sight'" (v22).** "In my alarm" (KJV "in my haste") is the word for hurried flight or panic. It is used of David hurrying to get away from Saul in the wilderness of Maon (1 Sam 23:26). In the panic, David concluded God had abandoned him. He was wrong. Jonah prayed nearly the same words from inside the fish: "I am driven away from your sight; yet I shall again look upon your holy temple" (Jonah 2:4).
- **"But you heard the voice of my pleas for mercy" (v22).** What David said in panic was not the last word. God heard anyway. The very prayer he prayed while thinking God had stopped listening was heard.
- **"Love the LORD, all you his saints!" (v23).** David turns to the congregation. This is one of the few places where the Psalms command God's people to love him. Maclaren, cited by Guzik, notes that the psalmist "hungers alone, but he feasts in company." Spurgeon, also cited, adds that it is a shame God's people should need to be told to love him, but they do.
- **"The LORD preserves the faithful but abundantly repays the one who acts in pride" (v23).** God keeps those who keep faith with him, and he pays back the proud, the arrogant enemies of vv13 and 18, in full. JFB reads "the faithful" as "the Lord is keeping faith," that is, with his people. The Hebrew can be read either way, as the faithful people or as faithfulness itself.
- **"Be strong, and let your heart take courage, all you who wait for the LORD!" (v24).** This is almost exactly the close of Psalm 27 (27:14). Psalm 27 spoke to one person. Psalm 31 speaks to "all you who wait." What David learned alone in the besieged city he now passes on to everyone.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Those very persons whom men load with slanders God loads with benefits and honours." He reads the "besieged city" as Keilah and says God kept David safe "in caves and woods, as safe as if he had been in a strong city." On v23 he notes that some older interpreters read "the proud doer" in a good sense, the bold or excellent doer whom God rewards richly. JFB on v22: "I, thus favored, was despondent." He reads "in my haste" as "in my terror." Guzik passes on Spurgeon's comment on v24, that despondent believers must "rouse" themselves, because courage here is commanded, not just felt.

#### Pointing to Christ
This is a psalm Jesus prayed. He was the one betrayed by friends, whispered against by many, treated as a broken vessel and "forgotten like one who is dead." The nets of the religious leaders were laid for him again and again. False witnesses spoke against him with "lying lips" (Matt 26:59–61). And at the end, with darkness over the land, he took David's words, made them his own and spoke them to his Father: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46). David committed his spirit to God to be kept alive. Jesus committed his spirit to God in death, trusting the Father to raise him, and the Father did. Because the Son was cut off from the land of the living, believers who cry in their panic, "I am cut off from your sight," are never actually cut off. Their times are in his hand. And because the hands that received his spirit are the hands that hold theirs, Christians can pray the same prayer at the end, as Stephen did: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."
