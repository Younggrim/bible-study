*Setting:* Two years after the vision of the four beasts, "in the third year of the reign of King Belshazzar" (v1), around 551 BC. Babylon still rules, and Persia has not yet conquered it. At this point the book switches language. Daniel 2:4 to 7:28 was in Aramaic, the international language. From 8:1 to the end the book returns to Hebrew, the language of Israel, and the focus narrows from the nations in general to what will happen to God's people and his temple. Chapter 7 surveyed four empires. Chapter 8 zooms in on the second and third, Medo-Persia and Greece, and above all on one cruel king who will come out of the Greek world and attack Jerusalem's worship. Unlike chapter 7, this vision names its empires outright (vv20–21).

#### Daniel's Vision of the Ram and the Goat (vv1–14)
- **"Susa the citadel… at the Ulai canal" (v2).** Susa (the KJV's "Shushan") was the old capital of Elam, east of Babylon in what is now south-west Iran. Under the Persians it became a royal capital, the setting of Esther (Esth 1:2) and of Nehemiah's service to the king (Neh 1:1). Daniel may have been there in person on royal business, or only seen himself there in the vision. The text can be read either way. The "canal" was probably a large artificial watercourse near the city. A vision about Persia's future is set in what would become Persia's capital.
- **"A ram… two horns, and both horns were high, but one was higher… and the higher one came up last" (v3).** Gabriel explains: "the kings of Media and Persia" (v20). Media was the older power. Persia, the later horn, rose higher under Cyrus, who took over Media in about 550 BC. A ram suits Persia: the Persian king was said to wear a golden ram's head when leading his army, though that detail comes from a late source.
- **"Charging westward and northward and southward" (v4).** Persia's conquests ran west to Lydia and Asia Minor, north to Armenia and the Caspian, and south to Egypt. **"He did as he pleased and became great."** That phrase, used here of Persia, will be used of Greece (v8) and of the little horn (vv9–11). Each power grows great, and each is brought down.
- **"A male goat came from the west… without touching the ground" (v5).** "The goat is the king of Greece" (v21). Alexander the Great crossed into Asia in 334 BC and in about three years had defeated the Persians in three great battles (Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela). The picture of a goat that does not touch the ground matches the winged leopard of 7:6: astonishing speed. **"A conspicuous horn between his eyes"** is "the first king" (v21), Alexander himself.
- **"He ran at him in his powerful wrath" (vv6–7).** Greek writers presented Alexander's campaign as revenge for the Persian invasions of Greece in 490 and 480 BC, so "wrath" fits. **"There was no one who could rescue the ram"** echoes v4 exactly. What Persia did to others is done to Persia.
- **"When he was strong, the great horn was broken" (v8).** Alexander died in Babylon in 323 BC, aged thirty-two, at the height of his power. **"Four conspicuous horns toward the four winds"**: after years of fighting among his generals, the empire settled into four main kingdoms. Two of them matter for the Bible: the Ptolemies in Egypt to the south and the Seleucids in Syria and Mesopotamia to the north. Judea lay between them.
- **"Out of one of them came a little horn" (v9).** This is a different little horn from 7:8. That one grew out of the fourth beast. This one grows out of one of the four Greek horns. Gabriel's description (vv23–25) and the events of history point clearly to the Seleucid king Antiochus IV (175–164 BC), who took the name "Epiphanes," God "made manifest." Some of his own subjects punned it into "Epimanes," "the madman."
- **"Toward the south, toward the east, and toward the glorious land" (v9).** Antiochus campaigned in Egypt (south) and Parthia and Persia (east). "The glorious land" (*hatsevi*, "the beauty" or "the ornament") is the land of Israel (Jer 3:19; Ezek 20:6), and the title returns in 11:16, 41.
- **"Even to the host of heaven… some of the stars it threw down" (v10).** The "host" may mean the angelic armies, or God's people pictured as stars (Gen 15:5; Dan 12:3), or both, since in Daniel what happens on earth is tied to what happens in heaven (10:13, 20). Antiochus killed thousands in Jerusalem and tried to wipe out the Jewish faith. That was an attack on heaven itself.
- **"As great as the Prince of the host" (v11).** The Prince is God himself (compare "the Prince of princes," v25). **"The regular burnt offering was taken away"**: the Hebrew is *tamid*, "continual," the lamb offered every morning and evening (Exod 29:38–42). In 167 BC Antiochus banned the sacrifices, outlawed the Sabbath and circumcision, and set up an altar to Zeus on the altar of burnt offering (1 Macc 1:44–59). Jewish writers called it "the desolating sacrilege."
- **"Because of transgression" (v12).** The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, and translations vary. The ESV reads it as God handing his people over for a time because of sin. That fits the history: many leading Jews had already embraced Greek ways, and the high priesthood was being bought and sold (2 Macc 4:7–17). **"It will throw truth to the ground"**: Antiochus had copies of the Law torn up and burned (1 Macc 1:56).
- **"For how long?" (v13).** One "holy one," an angel, asks another the question God's suffering people have always asked (Ps 13:1; 74:10; Rev 6:10). **"The transgression that makes desolate"** anticipates the "abomination that makes desolate" of 9:27, 11:31 and 12:11, which Jesus says will appear again (Matt 24:15).
- **"2,300 evenings and mornings" (v14).** Two main readings. The phrase may mean 2,300 days, about six and a third years, which runs roughly from the murder of the faithful high priest Onias III in 171 BC to the cleansing of the temple. Or it may count the two daily sacrifices separately, giving 1,150 days, a little over three years, which fits the time from the desecration in December 167 BC to the rededication in December 164 BC (1 Macc 1:54; 4:52–53). Either way, the number is a limit. Then **"the sanctuary shall be restored to its rightful state"**, literally "shall be made right." Judas Maccabeus cleansed the temple, and Jews still remember it as Hanukkah, the feast of Dedication that Jesus attended (John 10:22).
- **The year-for-a-day reading.** Some groups have treated the 2,300 days as 2,300 years. William Miller used it to predict Christ's return in 1844, and the Seventh-day Adventist movement grew from the aftermath. Nothing in the chapter points to that method, and nearly all interpreters, including the three below, reject it.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the ram "the second monarchy, of which the kingdoms of Media and Persia were the two horns," and the little horn "a great persecutor of the church and people of God." He reckons 2,300 days as "six years and three months, and about eighteen days," and traces the disaster to Israel's own sin. JFB says the goat not touching the ground "implies the incredible swiftness of his conquests," names the four horns as Seleucus, Cassander, Ptolemy and Lysimachus, and dates the 2,300 days from 171 to 165 BC. Guzik also prefers 2,300 literal days counted back from the rededication, gives serious weight to the 1,150-day reading, and rejects Miller's year-for-a-day scheme. He dates the rededication to 165 BC where the Tyndale notes have 164; ancient sources allow both.

#### The Interpretation of the Vision (vv15–27)
- **"One having the appearance of a man" (v15).** Then a voice calls from over the river: **"Gabriel, make this man understand the vision"** (v16). This is the first angel named in the Bible. The name means "man of God" or "God is mighty," and he appears again in 9:21 and in Luke 1:19, 26, where he announces the births of John and Jesus. The voice that commands him is not identified, and some see it as the LORD himself.
- **"I was frightened and fell on my face" (v17).** Every vision of heavenly beings in Scripture overwhelms the one who sees it (Ezek 1:28; Rev 1:17). **"O son of man"** here simply means "mortal," the same address God uses for Ezekiel about ninety times. It contrasts the weak human with the heavenly messenger.
- **"The vision is for the time of the end" (vv17, 19).** This is the hardest phrase in the chapter. The events clearly fit the second century BC. So "the end" may mean the end of this particular time of wrath (**"the latter end of the indignation"**, v19), when God's anger against his people's sin is complete. Or the vision may look past Antiochus to a final enemy at the end of history, with Antiochus as a preview. Many interpreters hold both.
- **"He touched me and made me stand up" (v18).** This happens again and again in the visions of chapters 8–10 (10:10, 16, 18). God's messengers do not leave his servants on the ground.
- **"The kings of Media and Persia… the king of Greece" (vv20–21).** These are the only empires after Babylon named in the visions, and they leave no doubt about the second and third kingdoms. The Hebrew for Greece is *Yavan*, "Ionia," the Greek-speaking coast of western Turkey, the source of English "Ionian" (compare Gen 10:2).
- **"Four kingdoms… but not with his power" (v22).** None of Alexander's successors matched him.
- **"When the transgressors have reached their limit" (v23).** God lets sin run its course to a point and no further (compare Gen 15:16; 1 Thess 2:16). **"A king of bold face"** means hard-faced and shameless (Deut 28:50). **"One who understands riddles"** means a master of deceit and double dealing. Antiochus seized the throne from his nephew, the rightful heir, and won ground by flattery and broken promises (11:21–24).
- **"His power shall be great, but not by his own power" (v24).** Most take this to mean that God allowed it, since every power in Daniel is given from above (4:17; 5:21). **"The people who are the saints"** are God's faithful in Israel.
- **"Without warning he shall destroy many" (v25).** In 167 BC an official of Antiochus came to Jerusalem speaking peace, then attacked the city on the Sabbath (1 Macc 1:29–32; 2 Macc 5:24–26). **"He shall even rise up against the Prince of princes"**, God himself. Antiochus's coins call him "God made manifest."
- **"He shall be broken, but by no human hand" (v25).** Antiochus died in 164 BC in Persia, of a sudden illness. Ancient Jewish accounts describe a terrible wasting disease and say he died in agony, and Polybius, a Greek historian, says he went mad. God, not an army, brought him down. Compare the stone "cut out by no human hand" that destroys the statue (2:34, 45).
- **"Seal up the vision, for it refers to many days from now" (v26).** To "seal" a document meant to preserve it, closed and authenticated, until it was needed (compare 12:4, 9; Isa 8:16). The events lay almost four hundred years ahead.
- **"I, Daniel, was overcome and lay sick for some days" (v27).** He had seen his people's future suffering, and it made him ill. Then **"I rose and went about the king's business."** He carried on with his ordinary work in the Babylonian government, still puzzled. Knowing the future did not excuse him from the present.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the "king of fierce countenance" will "spare neither old nor young," and reads "broken without hand" as "God's special visitation," with Antiochus's death as the primary fulfilment. On v27 he says Daniel was sick "through grief at the calamities coming on my people." Guzik reads the little horn as a prophecy with two fulfilments, Antiochus near at hand and the Antichrist at the end. He points to the shared traits: cruelty, deceit, self-exaltation and a downfall God brings, and he cites 2 Thessalonians 2. He also draws a lesson from v27: an interest in prophecy should make believers more faithful in ordinary duties. The Tyndale notes add a political reason for sealing the vision: under Belshazzar, a prophecy of Babylon's successors might have looked like treason.

#### Pointing to Christ
Antiochus called himself "God made manifest," banned the daily offering and set an idol in God's house. He was broken by no human hand. The true God made manifest came quietly. Jesus stood in the temple at the feast that remembered Antiochus's defeat and said, "I and the Father are one" (John 10:22–30). He did not abolish the daily sacrifice by force. He fulfilled it, offering himself "once for all" (Heb 10:10–14). Where Antiochus defiled the sanctuary, Jesus called his own body the temple (John 2:19–21). Through him God's people become a temple that no tyrant can overthrow (1 Cor 3:16; Eph 2:21–22).
