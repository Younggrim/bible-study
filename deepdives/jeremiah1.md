*Setting:* Judah, 627/626 BC, the thirteenth year of King Josiah. A hundred years earlier Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel, and the long, wicked reign of Manasseh (2 Kings 21) had filled Judah with idols. Josiah was the last good king. He began seeking God as a teenager, and a few years after Jeremiah's call he would find the Book of the Law in the temple and lead a sweeping reform (2 Kings 22–23). But the reform did not reach the people's hearts, and Assyria's empire was collapsing while Babylon rose to take its place. Jeremiah would preach for about forty years, through five kings, and live to see Jerusalem burned in 586 BC. Chapter 1 is his call: a reluctant young man, a God who has already chosen him, and two small visions that set the course of his life.

#### The Call of Jeremiah (vv1–19)
- **"The words of Jeremiah, the son of Hilkiah" (v1).** Jeremiah's name probably means "the LORD exalts" or "the LORD establishes," though its exact sense is uncertain. Hilkiah was a common priestly name. Most think this is not the high priest Hilkiah who found the Book of the Law (2 Kings 22:8), since the text would surely say so. Jeremiah was born into the priesthood, but God called him to be a prophet.
- **"One of the priests who were in Anathoth" (v1).** Anathoth was a small priestly town in Benjamin, about three miles north-east of Jerusalem, close enough to see the city from the hills. Solomon had banished the priest Abiathar there for backing a rival to the throne (1 Kings 2:26–27). So Jeremiah may have come from a priestly family pushed to the edges of power. The men of Anathoth would later plot to kill him (11:21–23), and yet he bought a field there as a sign of hope (32:6–15).
- **"In the days of Josiah… Jehoiakim… Zedekiah" (vv2–3).** The heading gives the span of his ministry, from 627 to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, "the captivity of Jerusalem in the fifth month" (2 Kings 25:8). Two kings, Jehoahaz and Jehoiachin, are left out, because each reigned only three months. Jeremiah kept preaching after the fall, in Judah and then in Egypt (chs 40–44), but the heading covers the main body of the book.
- **"Now the word of the LORD came to me" (v4).** This is the prophet's own account. The phrase "the word of the LORD came" marks a real message from outside himself. Jeremiah did not decide to be a prophet. He was told he already was one.
- **"Before I formed you in the womb I knew you" (v5).** "Formed" is the potter's word (*yatsar*), the one used of God shaping Adam from the dust (Gen 2:7). "Knew" (*yada*) means more than knowing facts about him. It is the knowledge of choice and relationship, as when God says of Abraham, "I have chosen him" (Gen 18:19, literally "I have known him"). Paul uses the same picture of himself: God "set me apart before I was born, and… called me by his grace" (Gal 1:15). The verse is also often cited for the value of life before birth, which it supports: God knew Jeremiah as a person before he was born.
- **"I consecrated you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations" (v5).** "Consecrated" means set apart for holy use, like the temple vessels or the priests. Jeremiah was a prophet to Judah mainly, but his message reached far beyond it. Chapters 46–51 are oracles against Egypt, Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Damascus, Kedar, Elam and Babylon, and he told kings of other nations to submit to Babylon (27:2–11).
- **"Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth" (v6).** "Ah" is a cry of dismay. "Youth" is *naʿar*, a word that covers anyone from an infant to a young man (Joseph at seventeen, Gen 37:2). Jeremiah was probably a teenager or barely twenty. In a culture where age gave authority, a young man speaking against kings and priests would not be taken seriously. His excuse echoes Moses: "I am slow of speech" (Exod 4:10).
- **"Do not say, 'I am only a youth'" (v7).** God does not argue that Jeremiah is older or more gifted than he thinks. He changes the subject from Jeremiah to himself. The two parts of the job are simple: go where sent, say what commanded.
- **"Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you" (v8).** "I am with you" is the promise God gave Moses (Exod 3:12), Joshua (Josh 1:5) and Gideon (Judg 6:16), and Jesus gave his disciples (Matt 28:20). Notice what it does not promise. Jeremiah will be beaten, put in stocks, imprisoned and lowered into a muddy cistern (20:2; 37:15; 38:6). The promise is rescue, not an easy life. **"Declares the LORD"** (*neʾum YHWH*) is the formal seal of a royal message. It appears well over 150 times in Jeremiah, far more than in any other book.
- **"The LORD put out his hand and touched my mouth" (v9).** Isaiah's lips were touched with a burning coal to cleanse them (Isa 6:6–7). Jeremiah's mouth is touched to fill it: **"I have put my words in your mouth."** That is the very description of the prophet like Moses in Deuteronomy 18:18, "I will put my words in his mouth." The words of the book are Jeremiah's voice and God's message at once.
- **"To pluck up and to break down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (v10).** Six verbs, four of tearing down and two of building up. The first pair and the last pair are farming words (uprooting, planting), and the middle are building words (demolishing, building). The balance tells the story of the book: most of Jeremiah's preaching was judgment, but it ended in promise. The same verbs return at key moments (18:7–9; 24:6; 31:28; 45:4). Jeremiah himself had no army. God "set" him over nations in the sense that his word announced what God would do to them.
- **"Jeremiah, what do you see?" "I see an almond branch" (v11).** The almond is the first tree to blossom in Israel, flowering white in late January or February while other trees are still bare. Its Hebrew name is *shaqed*, "the wakeful one."
- **"You have seen well, for I am watching over my word to perform it" (v12).** The ESV footnote explains the pun: "watching" is *shoqed*, almost the same sound as *shaqed*. Every time Jeremiah saw an almond tree in bloom, he would remember that God is awake and his word will come true. The same verb returns in 31:28: just as God watched over Israel to tear down, he "will watch over them to build and to plant." For a prophet whose warnings would be mocked for decades, this was the encouragement he needed.
- **"I see a boiling pot, facing away from the north" (v13).** A large cooking pot over a fire, tipped so that its scalding contents are about to pour south. Boiling water from the north is the picture of an invading army.
- **"Out of the north disaster shall be let loose" (v14).** Babylon lies east of Judah, but the Arabian desert lay between them. Armies followed the Fertile Crescent up the Euphrates and came down through Syria, so invaders always arrived from the north. The enemy is not named until later (20:4; 25:9). "Disaster" is *raʿah*, a word that can also mean "evil." The ESV footnote notes the range. It is God's judgment, and it answers Judah's own "evil" in v16.
- **"Every one shall set his throne at the entrance of the gates of Jerusalem" (v15).** The city gate was where elders judged cases and rulers held court. Foreign kings sitting there would mean Judah had lost its government. Jeremiah 39:3 records exactly this: Babylon's officials "sat in the middle gate."
- **"For all their evil in forsaking me" (v16).** The charge is stated in three steps: they forsook God, made offerings to other gods, and worshiped what their own hands had made. The rest of the book fills in the details, beginning in chapter 2.
- **"Dress yourself for work" (v17).** Literally "gird up your loins" (ESV footnote): tuck the long robe into the belt so that it does not trip a man running or fighting. **"Do not be dismayed by them, lest I dismay you before them"** uses the same Hebrew verb twice (*chatat*, to be shattered or terrified). If Jeremiah is broken by fear of people, God will let him be broken in front of them. The fear of God is the cure for the fear of people.
- **"A fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls" (v18).** God turns the picture of the siege around. Jerusalem will fall to a siege, but Jeremiah will be a city that cannot be taken. His enemies are listed: kings, officials, priests and the people of the land, which is to say everyone. One young man is set against the whole nation.
- **"They will fight against you, but they shall not prevail against you" (v19).** The promise repeats v8 and closes the chapter with "I am with you… to deliver you." Through forty years of rejection it held true. Jeremiah outlived the kings who opposed him. God repeats the promise almost word for word in 15:20, when Jeremiah is close to despair.
- **Commentators:** Henry ties v5 to Jeremiah's confidence: "he who gave him his commission is the same that gave him his being." He also argues that Jeremiah's father was "not that Hilkiah… who was high priest," since a high priest would be named as such. JFB takes "sanctified" in v5 as set apart for office, not cleansed from original sin, and reads Jeremiah's reluctance as proof that he did not take up the role out of "self-deceiving fanaticism." Guzik calls the youth objection true but beside the point, and lists young people God has used, from David and Timothy to later missionaries. Guzik and JFB both note that Jeremiah's commission has four verbs of destruction and two of construction. JFB says his message was "chiefly, and in the first instance, denunciatory." On the almond, Henry calls it "a hasty tree," God hastening his word, while JFB gives "the wakeful tree." The lexicon root *shaqad* means "to watch, be wakeful," so both get at the same pun. JFB adds that "dismayed" and "confound" in v17 (KJV) translate one Hebrew word meaning "to break," which the ESV captures by using "dismay" twice. Guzik, following Poole, stresses that God promises to go with his messenger, not merely send him the way a king sends an ambassador.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah was known before he was born, given God's words to speak, sent to people who would fight him, and promised that they would not prevail. Every line of that call finds its fullest meaning in Jesus. He was the eternal Son before he was formed in Mary's womb. He said, "the words that you gave me I have given them" (John 17:8). He was opposed by kings, rulers, priests and people together (Acts 4:26–27). His enemies seemed to prevail at the cross, but they could not hold him, and he rose to build and to plant a people for God. Jeremiah's six verbs describe his whole work: judgment carried by him, and a new kingdom planted through him.
