*Setting:* Like chapter 35, this chapter steps back from the final siege to the reign of Jehoiakim. It opens in his fourth year, 605 BC, a turning point in world history. That year Nebuchadnezzar crushed the Egyptian army at Carchemish on the Euphrates (46:2), became king of Babylon, and began taking hostages from Judah, including the young Daniel (Dan 1:1–6). The same year Jeremiah delivered his sermon on the seventy years of Babylonian rule (25:1, 11). Jehoiakim, Josiah's son, was a king who built himself a fine palace with unpaid labor (22:13–17) and had already executed the prophet Uriah for preaching what Jeremiah preached (26:20–23). Jeremiah had been preaching for twenty-three years (25:3). Now God tells him to put it all in writing. The chapter tells how the book of Jeremiah began, and how a king tried to destroy it.

#### Jehoiakim Burns Jeremiah's Scroll (vv1–32)
- **"Take a scroll and write on it all the words that I have spoken to you" (v2).** A scroll was made of sheets of papyrus or leather joined into a long strip and rolled up. **"Against Israel and Judah and all the nations, from the day I spoke to you, from the days of Josiah until today."** Jeremiah's call came in 627 BC (1:2). The scroll was a collection of more than twenty years of preaching, likely including much of what is now in chapters 1–25.
- **"It may be that the house of Judah will hear… so that every one may turn from his evil way, and that I may forgive their iniquity" (v3).** The purpose of the warnings was mercy. God's threats of judgment were an invitation to repent. "It may be" does not mean God was unsure. It shows that the door of repentance was really open (compare 26:3; Jonah 3:9).
- **"Then Jeremiah called Baruch the son of Neriah, and Baruch wrote on a scroll at the dictation of Jeremiah" (v4).** Baruch was a trained scribe from a prominent family. His brother Seraiah served at court (51:59). Dictating to a professional scribe was the normal way to produce a document. Paul later did the same (Rom 16:22).
- **"I am banned from going to the house of the LORD" (v5).** The word means "restrained" or "shut up." It probably does not mean Jeremiah was in prison, since in v19 he is free to go into hiding. More likely he had been banned from the temple after his temple sermon and the uproar that followed (7:1–15; 20:1–2; 26:7–11).
- **"On a day of fasting in the hearing of all the people" (v6).** A fast day would bring crowds from all over Judah into the temple, people already in a mood to seek God. Baruch's reading was a risky job. Uriah had been killed for less.
- **"It may be that their plea for mercy will come before the LORD" (v7).** Literally "their supplication will fall before the LORD," like a petitioner falling on his face before a king.
- **"In the fifth year… in the ninth month… proclaimed a fast" (v9).** The ninth month, Kislev, fell in November–December. The date is about a year after the command of v1, so the scroll may have taken time to prepare, or Baruch waited for the right occasion. This was not one of the regular feasts. The Babylonian Chronicle, a series of clay tablets recording Nebuchadnezzar's campaigns, says that in Kislev of 604 BC he captured and plundered Ashkelon, the Philistine port on Judah's doorstep. A fast called in alarm at that news fits the date exactly.
- **"In the chamber of Gemariah the son of Shaphan the secretary, which was in the upper court, at the entry of the New Gate" (v10).** Shaphan had been Josiah's royal secretary, the man who read the newly found Book of the Law to the king (2 Kgs 22:8–10). His family stood by Jeremiah again and again. His son Ahikam saved Jeremiah's life (26:24), his son Elasah carried his letter (29:3), and his grandson Gedaliah would protect him after Jerusalem fell (39:14). A clay seal impression reading "Gemaryahu son of Shaphan" was found in excavations in the City of David in the 1980s, very likely belonging to this man. The New Gate was where Jeremiah had stood trial years before (26:10).
- **"When Micaiah the son of Gemariah, son of Shaphan, heard all the words of the LORD from the scroll" (v11).** Three generations of one family are named. Micaiah's grandfather had heard the Book of the Law read and taken it to King Josiah. Now the grandson hears God's word read and takes it to the officials.
- **"All the officials were sitting there: Elishama the secretary, Delaiah the son of Shemaiah, Elnathan the son of Achbor, Gemariah the son of Shaphan, Zedekiah the son of Hananiah" (v12).** The royal cabinet, meeting in the palace. Elnathan's father Achbor had been one of Josiah's officials (2 Kgs 22:12). Elnathan himself had been sent to Egypt to bring back the prophet Uriah for execution (26:22). Here he urges the king not to burn the scroll (v25), so he may have had second thoughts. This Zedekiah is not the later king.
- **"Then all the officials sent Jehudi the son of Nethaniah, son of Shelemiah, son of Cushi" (v14).** An unusually long family line, perhaps to show that Jehudi ("a Judahite") was of good Israelite stock despite a great-grandfather named Cushi, which can mean "the Cushite," from the region south of Egypt.
- **"Sit down and read it" (v15).** The officials treat Baruch respectfully. **"When they heard all the words, they turned one to another in fear" (v16).** Real fear, the right response to God's word. **"We must report all these words to the king."** Not a threat against Baruch. They knew the matter was too serious to keep quiet.
- **"How did you write all these words? Was it at his dictation?" (v17).** They want to be sure these are Jeremiah's words, not Baruch's. **"He dictated all these words to me, while I wrote them with ink on the scroll" (v18).** Baruch's answer is plain. He supplied the pen and ink, and Jeremiah supplied the words. Ink was made from soot or lampblack mixed with gum and water.
- **"Go and hide, you and Jeremiah, and let no one know where you are" (v19).** The officials knew their king. They remembered what happened to Uriah.
- **"Having put the scroll in the chamber of Elishama the secretary" (v20).** They left the scroll behind, perhaps hoping to protect it. Jehoiakim sent for it anyway (v21).
- **"The king was sitting in the winter house, and there was a fire burning in the fire pot before him" (v22).** Jerusalem winters are cold and wet. Wealthy homes had warmer rooms or wings for winter (Amos 3:15). The "fire pot" was a brazier, a metal or clay pan of burning charcoal, since houses had no fireplaces or chimneys.
- **"As Jehudi read three or four columns, the king would cut them off with a knife and throw them into the fire" (v23).** "Columns" is literally "doors," the panels of writing side by side down the length of a scroll. The "knife" is a scribe's knife, a small blade used to sharpen reed pens and trim scrolls. Every few columns Jehoiakim sliced off the part just read and dropped it in the fire. It was not an outburst of rage. It was slow, deliberate contempt, repeated column after column **"until the entire scroll was consumed."**
- **"Yet neither the king nor any of his servants who heard all these words was afraid, nor did they tear their garments" (v24).** A deliberate echo. When the Book of the Law was read to Jehoiakim's father Josiah, "he tore his clothes" and humbled himself (2 Kgs 22:11, 19). The son does the opposite. There is also a wordplay in the Hebrew. The verb for the king "cutting" the scroll in v23 is *qara'*, the same verb as "tear" in v24. Jehoiakim tore up God's word instead of tearing his robes. Note the contrast, too, between the officials who "turned one to another in fear" (v16) and the king and his servants, who were "not afraid."
- **"Even when Elnathan and Delaiah and Gemariah urged the king not to burn the scroll, he would not listen to them" (v25).** Three men stood up to the king, which took courage. They could not stop him, but their protest was recorded.
- **"The king commanded Jerahmeel the king's son… to seize Baruch the secretary and Jeremiah the prophet" (v26).** "The king's son" may mean a royal prince or simply an officer of the royal household, a title for police duties. **"But the LORD hid them."** The officials told them to hide, and they did, but the text gives the credit to God. Human prudence and divine protection worked together (Ps 31:20).
- **"Take another scroll and write on it all the former words that were in the first scroll" (v28).** Jehoiakim could burn the scroll. He could not burn the word. "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever" (Isa 40:8; 1 Pet 1:24–25). The pattern goes back to Sinai, when Moses broke the first tablets and God had the words written again (Exod 34:1).
- **"Why have you written in it that the king of Babylon will certainly come and destroy this land?" (v29).** This was the king's real objection. He did not like what it said. Jehoiakim had sworn loyalty to Babylon but planned to rebel, and he did rebel about 601 BC (2 Kgs 24:1).
- **"He shall have none to sit on the throne of David, and his dead body shall be cast out to the heat by day and the frost by night" (v30).** Jehoiakim died in December 598 BC, as Babylon's army was coming to punish his rebellion. His son Jehoiachin reigned only three months before surrendering and being taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 24:8–15). So no son of his truly sat on David's throne, and the next king to reign for any length of time was not his son but his brother Zedekiah. Jeremiah had already said he would receive "the burial of a donkey," dragged and dumped outside Jerusalem's gates (22:18–19). 2 Kings 24:6 says only that he "slept with his fathers," which can simply mean he died. Scripture does not describe his burial, so some think his body was later thrown out when the Babylonians arrived, and others think the phrase was never meant to be taken literally.
- **Jehoiachin and the line of the Messiah.** Jehoiachin, also called Jeconiah or Coniah, was cursed too: "none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David" (22:30). Yet Matthew lists him in the royal line that leads to Joseph, Jesus' legal father (Matt 1:11–12). Luke traces Jesus' descent through another son of David, Nathan (Luke 3:31). Many interpreters see here how God kept both his curse and his promise: Jesus received David's legal claim through Joseph without being a physical descendant of the cursed king.
- **"I will punish him and his offspring and his servants for their iniquity… but they would not hear" (v31).** The courtiers who sat by and were not afraid would share his fate.
- **"Then Jeremiah took another scroll and gave it to Baruch… And many similar words were added to them" (v32).** The new scroll was longer than the old one. Burning God's word did not reduce it, it increased it. This verse also tells us how the book of Jeremiah grew: Jeremiah's prophecies were collected, written by Baruch, and expanded over time. Chapter 45, a personal word to Baruch from this same year, shows what the work cost him.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Jehoiakim's act "a piece of as daring impiety as a man could lightly be guilty of," and on v26 says, "God will find out a shelter for his people." JFB notes that the "leaves" were columns on a single long scroll, that the fire was a charcoal brazier since Eastern homes had no chimneys, and that Baruch's answer in v18 means he "added nothing save the hand, pen, and ink." On v30 JFB says Jehoiachin's three-month reign "is counted as nothing." Guzik, drawing on Kidner, stresses that the king's slow cutting showed calm, considered rejection, and he sees fear behind the show of boldness, since the king immediately ordered the arrests. All three contrast Jehoiakim with Josiah tearing his clothes in 2 Kings 22. Guzik applies the "penknife" to modern ways of cutting out parts of the Bible one dislikes, citing Meyer, who compares it to a ship's captain destroying a chart that shows the rocks: the rocks remain. He also gives historical examples of Bibles burned and the Scriptures surviving. Henry adds that "the writing of the scripture is by divine appointment," and JFB compares the second scroll to the second set of tablets at Sinai.

#### Pointing to Christ
Jehoiakim sat by his fire and burned the word of God a few columns at a time, and the word outlived him. Centuries later the Word himself came, "and the Word became flesh" (John 1:14). He too was rejected by the rulers of his people, and they tried to destroy him completely. But just as the burned scroll came back longer than before, the crucified Christ rose again, and his word went out to all nations. Jesus said, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35). And the curse on Jehoiakim's line did not cancel God's promise to David. In Jesus, a Son of David sits on the throne forever.
