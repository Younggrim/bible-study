*Setting:* This is Paul's last letter, written from a Roman prison, most likely in the mid-60s AD under Nero, with execution close (4:6). This imprisonment is harsher than the house arrest of Acts 28: he is chained "as a criminal" (v9), and friends had to search to find him (1:17). Timothy is still leading the church at Ephesus, where false teaching keeps spreading. Chapter 1 called him to fan God's gift into flame, not to be ashamed of the gospel or of Paul, and to guard "the good deposit" of the truth (1:14). It ended with a painful contrast: "all who are in Asia turned away from me," including Phygelus and Hermogenes, while Onesiphorus searched Rome for Paul and "was not ashamed of my chains" (1:15–18). Chapter 2 turns to Timothy: "You then." In a time of desertion, he must be strong, pass the truth on, endure, handle the word rightly and deal gently but firmly with those who oppose it.

#### A Good Soldier of Christ Jesus (vv1–13)
- **"You then, my child" (v1).** The "then" ties this back to the deserters and Onesiphorus. Others have walked away. Timothy must not. "My child" (*teknon*) is tender: Timothy was Paul's son in the faith (1 Tim 1:2), probably converted on Paul's first visit to Lystra (Acts 14; 16:1–3).
- **"Be strengthened by the grace that is in Christ Jesus" (v1).** The verb is passive and ongoing: keep being made strong. Timothy is not told to grit his teeth, but to draw his strength from grace, the same grace that saved him (1:9). Paul had learned this himself: "my grace is sufficient for you" (2 Cor 12:9).
- **"Entrust to faithful men, who will be able to teach others also" (v2).** Four generations sit in one verse: Paul, Timothy, faithful men, and others. "Entrust" (*paratithēmi*) is the verb behind "the deposit" (*parathēkē*) of 1:12–14. Guarding the truth does not mean locking it away. It means handing it on to reliable people. The "many witnesses" may be the elders at Timothy's commissioning (1 Tim 4:14; 6:12) or simply the many who heard Paul teach and could confirm what he said. The ESV footnote notes that *anthrōpoi*, "men," can mean people in general, though the teaching office in view is the elders of 1 Timothy 3.
- **"Share in suffering as a good soldier" (v3).** One compound word, *synkakopatheō*, "suffer hardship together with." Paul uses it in 1:8 too. He is not asking Timothy to suffer alone, but alongside him. Then come three quick pictures from everyday life: the soldier, the athlete and the farmer, each showing a different side of endurance. Paul uses all three elsewhere (1 Cor 9:7, 24–27).
- **"No soldier gets entangled in civilian pursuits" (v4).** "Entangled" (*emplekō*) means woven in or tangled up. Roman legionaries were barred from trade and farming while they served. The point is not that ordinary work is bad. Paul himself made tents. It is that a soldier's first loyalty is "to please the one who enlisted him." Nothing else may tie his hands.
- **"An athlete is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules" (v5).** Athletes at the Olympic games swore an oath that they had trained for ten months and would keep the rules of the contest. The "crown" was a wreath of leaves. Faithfulness means finishing God's way, not inventing a shortcut. Paul will soon speak of his own "crown of righteousness" (4:8).
- **"The hard-working farmer who ought to have the first share of the crops" (v6).** The stress falls on "hard-working." Farming is slow and unglamorous, and the harvest comes later. Paul may also hint at a right to be supported, as in 1 Corinthians 9:7–10 and 1 Timothy 5:17–18, but the main point is that labour comes before reward.
- **"Think over what I say, for the Lord will give you understanding" (v7).** Paul leaves the pictures half-explained on purpose. Timothy must think, and the Lord will give insight. Effort and grace work together, as in v1.
- **"Remember Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, the offspring of David" (v8).** This is the whole gospel in a line, close to the summary in Romans 1:3–4. "Risen" is a perfect participle in Greek: raised and still alive. "Offspring of David" means he is the promised King of 2 Samuel 7:12–16. For a suffering servant, the risen Christ is the reason to keep going: suffering is not the end of the story. Paul names the resurrection first here, which matters given the error in v18.
- **"Bound with chains as a criminal" (v9).** "Criminal" (*kakourgos*) is the word Luke uses for the two criminals crucified with Jesus (Luke 23:32–33). Paul shares his Lord's place among the wrongdoers.
- **"But the word of God is not bound!" (v9).** The messenger is chained. The message is not. Paul had seen it from his first Roman imprisonment, where the gospel reached the palace guard (Phil 1:12–14), and Acts ends with him preaching "without hindrance" (Acts 28:31).
- **"I endure everything for the sake of the elect" (v10).** The "elect" are God's chosen people, including those not yet converted who will come to faith through the preaching. Election does not make Paul passive. It is why he endures: God has people to save, and the gospel must reach them. His sufferings do not save anyone, but they carry the message that does (Col 1:24).
- **"The saying is trustworthy" (v11).** This is the fourth of five "trustworthy sayings" in the Pastoral Letters (1 Tim 1:15; 3:1; 4:9; Titus 3:8). What follows is four balanced lines, probably an early Christian hymn or baptismal confession that Paul quotes.
- **"If we have died with him, we will also live with him" (v11).** The Greek tense points to a definite past event, most likely union with Christ in his death at conversion and baptism (Rom 6:3–8). For Paul, near martyrdom, the line may also carry a literal sense.
- **"If we endure, we will also reign with him" (v12).** Endurance now, rule with Christ later (Rom 8:17; Rev 3:21).
- **"If we deny him, he also will deny us" (v12).** This is Jesus' own warning: "whoever denies me before men, I also will deny before my Father" (Matt 10:33). The tense here is future: it speaks of a settled, final denial, not a moment of failure like Peter's, from which he was restored (John 21:15–19).
- **"If we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself" (v13).** The pattern breaks. We would expect "he will be faithless too," but he cannot be. Readers take this two ways. Many read it as comfort: when believers waver, Christ does not let them go (Rom 3:3–4). Others read it as the back side of v12: Christ stays true to his own word, including his warnings. Both agree on the main thing: Christ's faithfulness rests on his own character, not on ours. The Tyndale notes list several possible senses and do not choose one.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws from v2 the need for "a succession in the ministry, that the office might not die with him." Guzik agrees but says true succession is faithful teaching passed on, not an unbroken chain of ordinations, and that faithfulness matters more than talent in choosing whom to train. On v4 JFB says the danger is "the becoming entangled, or over-engrossed" in ordinary business, not doing it at all. On v5 Henry writes that a Christian "must aim at mastering his own lusts and corruptions." On v9 JFB puts Paul's point in his mouth: "my tongue and my pen are not" bound, and Henry: "Though the preachers of the word are often bound, yet the word is never bound." Guzik adds the warning that preachers can bind the word in their own pulpits by sidelining it. On v13 JFB and Henry read the line as Christ keeping his word; Henry says he "cannot recede from any word that he hath spoken." Guzik leans on the comforting side, likening it to the father receiving the prodigal.

#### A Worker Approved by God (vv14–26)
- **"Charge them before God not to quarrel about words" (v14).** "Quarrel about words" is one Greek verb, *logomacheō*, "word-fight," found only here. Paul used the noun in 1 Timothy 6:4. He is not against careful words. He is against verbal sparring that "does no good, but only ruins the hearers." "Ruins" is *katastrophē*, from which English gets "catastrophe." Some manuscripts read "before the Lord" rather than "before God," as the ESV footnote notes; the sense is the same.
- **"Do your best to present yourself to God as one approved" (v15).** "Do your best" (*spoudazō*) means make every effort, be eager and diligent. The audience is God, not the crowd. "Approved" (*dokimos*) describes metal or coin that has been tested and found genuine. The ESV footnote: "one who has been tested."
- **"A worker who has no need to be ashamed" (v15).** Shame runs through this letter: Paul is not ashamed (1:12), Onesiphorus was not ashamed (1:16), and Timothy must not be (1:8). Here the shame to avoid is a workman's embarrassment when his work is inspected.
- **"Rightly handling the word of truth" (v15).** The verb *orthotomeō* means literally "to cut straight." It appears only here in the New Testament. The Greek Old Testament uses it of God making a road straight (Prov 3:6). The lexicon gives the root meaning "cut straight" and, metaphorically, to handle or teach correctly. The KJV's "rightly dividing" has sometimes been used to support systems that split the Bible into separate eras, but the word itself is about handling the truth accurately and keeping it on course, not slicing it into compartments. Readers have pictured a road cut straight, a furrow ploughed straight, or a steward cutting bread fairly for a household. All point the same way: no twisting, no detours.
- **"Avoid irreverent babble" (v16).** "Irreverent" means profane, treating holy things as common. "Babble" is empty talk. Paul used the same pair in 1 Timothy 6:20. Such talk claims to "advance," but it advances only "into more and more ungodliness." The false teachers may have liked the language of progress.
- **"Their talk will spread like gangrene" (v17).** The word is *gangraina*, an eating, spreading sore that kills healthy flesh. It appears only here in the New Testament. Ancient doctors knew it could not be argued with, only cut out. Error spreads the same way, quietly and fatally.
- **Hymenaeus and Philetus (v17).** Hymenaeus was named with Alexander in 1 Timothy 1:20 as one Paul had "handed over to Satan" so that he might learn not to blaspheme. Either that discipline had not worked, or he was still active outside the church. Philetus appears only here.
- **"Saying that the resurrection has already happened" (v18).** They probably took Paul's own teaching, that believers have been "raised with Christ" (Col 3:1; Eph 2:6), and turned it into the whole truth: the resurrection is spiritual, it is done, and there is no bodily resurrection to come. Many Greeks thought the body worthless anyway (Acts 17:32; 1 Cor 15:12). Later Gnostic groups held something similar. The error could lead two ways: to harsh self-denial (1 Tim 4:3) or to careless living, since the body no longer mattered. Either way it was "upsetting the faith of some." This is why v8 put "risen from the dead" first.
- **"But God's firm foundation stands" (v19).** Whatever is shaking, the foundation is not. Readers have taken the foundation as the church (1 Tim 3:15), Christ himself, or God's saving purpose. It may be all three seen from different angles. Ancient buildings sometimes carried an inscription on the foundation stone stating who owned the building and what it was for.
- **"The Lord knows those who are his" (v19).** This is almost word for word from the Greek Old Testament of Numbers 16:5, Moses' answer to Korah's rebellion. The setting fits closely: men inside God's people challenged God's appointed leaders, and God showed who were truly his. Some who stood in the camp were not his, and he knew it.
- **"Let everyone who names the name of the Lord depart from iniquity" (v19).** This is not an exact quotation but gathers Old Testament language: in the same chapter Israel was told to move away from the rebels' tents (Num 16:26), and Isaiah 52:11 says, "Depart, depart… touch no unclean thing." The two inscriptions belong together. God's side: he knows his own. Our side: those who are his turn from sin. Assurance and holiness are carved on the same stone.
- **"In a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver but also of wood and clay" (v20).** A wealthy household had fine tableware and also cheap pots for rubbish and waste. The "great house" is the visible church, where the true and the false mix for now, as wheat and weeds grow together until harvest (Matt 13:24–30). Paul used similar language in Romans 9:21, though there the potter's point is God's freedom. Here the point is the believer's responsibility.
- **"If anyone cleanses himself from what is dishonorable" (v21).** The Greek says "from these," as the ESV footnote notes. It could mean from the dishonorable vessels, that is, from the false teachers and their influence, or from the dishonorable things themselves. The context of vv16–19 suggests both: separate from error and from the sin it breeds. The result is a vessel "set apart as holy" (sanctified), "useful to the master of the house, ready for every good work." "Useful" here is *euchrēstos*, the word Paul used of Onesimus (Philem 11).
- **"Flee youthful passions" (v22).** Timothy was likely in his thirties, still "young" by ancient standards (1 Tim 4:12). "Youthful passions" certainly includes sexual temptation, which is to be fled, not managed (Gen 39:12; 1 Cor 6:18). In context it probably also includes the restless traits of youth: love of argument, impatience, pride and the urge to win. The next verse is about controversies.
- **"Pursue righteousness, faith, love, and peace" (v22).** Flee one thing, chase another. The same pattern appears in 1 Timothy 6:11. And Timothy is not to pursue them alone but "along with those who call on the Lord from a pure heart." To "call on the name of the Lord" is the Old Testament phrase for worshipping the true God, used in the New Testament of Christians (Joel 2:32; Rom 10:13; 1 Cor 1:2).
- **"Foolish, ignorant controversies… breed quarrels" (v23).** "Ignorant" means uninstructed or undisciplined: questions that teach nothing. Not every question is foolish. Paul himself argued in synagogues and lecture halls. But some debates exist only to fight.
- **"The Lord's servant must not be quarrelsome but kind to everyone" (v24).** "The Lord's servant" (*doulos kyriou*) echoes the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah, who "will not quarrel or cry aloud" and will not break a bruised reed (Isa 42:1–3; Matt 12:18–20). The qualities match the elder lists: "able to teach," "not quarrelsome" (1 Tim 3:2–3). "Patiently enduring evil" is one word, *anexikakos*, found only here, which the lexicon gives as "patiently forbearing": able to put up with wrong without resentment.
- **"Correcting his opponents with gentleness" (v25).** "Correcting" (*paideuō*) is the word for training or disciplining a child. Gentleness (*praütēs*, meekness) is not weakness. It is strength under control, the way Moses (Num 12:3) and Jesus (Matt 11:29) are described.
- **"God may perhaps grant them repentance" (v25).** Repentance is God's gift (Acts 5:31; 11:18). The servant cannot argue anyone into it. That frees him from anger: his job is to teach truly and kindly, and leave the heart to God. "Perhaps" is honest. Not all will turn.
- **"Come to their senses" (v26).** The verb *ananēphō* means to sober up, to return to clear thinking after drunkenness. Error intoxicates.
- **"Escape from the snare of the devil, after being captured by him to do his will" (v26).** "Captured" is *zōgreō*, "to take alive," the word Jesus used when he told Peter he would be "catching men" (Luke 5:10). The devil hunts people alive too. The Greek uses two different pronouns ("by him… to do *his* will"), and readers differ. The ESV and most modern translations take both as the devil: captured by him to do his will. Others read the second as God: rescued from the devil's snare to do God's will. A third view has the Lord's servant capturing them alive for God's will. The first is the simplest reading, but the Greek leaves room for the others.
- **Commentators:** Henry on v19: "Whatever errors are introduced into the church, the foundation of God standeth sure, his purpose can never be defeated." Guzik draws out the two seals: one marks people for God, the other separates them from sin, and someone who never turns from sin should ask whether they belong to Christ. On v15 JFB reads the word as "cutting 'straight'" and pictures a steward dividing food for a household. Guzik lists several images (a road, a furrow, a sacrifice, a meal) and uses the misreading of "judge not" (Matt 7:1) as an example of handling a verse wrongly. JFB calls the great house "the visible professing Christian Church," and Henry agrees that "in the church there are some vessels of honour and some of dishonour." On v22 JFB stresses rashness and love of strife over sensual sin, while Henry simply warns that "youthful lusts are very dangerous." On v24–25 Henry: "The servant of the Lord must be gentle to all men," and he calls the trap in v26 the "worst snare, because it is the devil's." On v26 JFB takes the third view: the different pronouns mean these people are "taken alive" by the Lord's servant to follow God's will, rescued rather than enslaved. It is a fair reading of the Greek, though most translations do not follow it.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every picture in this chapter is drawn from Christ before it is applied to Timothy. He is the soldier who pleased the Father, the one who ran the course and kept the rules, the farmer who sowed in tears and will share the harvest. He is "risen from the dead, the offspring of David," the Servant who did not quarrel or break bruised reeds, and the Lord who "knows those who are his." He was counted among criminals, and his word still runs free. Above all, when his people falter, "he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself."
