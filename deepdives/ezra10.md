*Setting:* Jerusalem, winter of 458 BC. Ezra has just learned that many of the returned exiles, priests and leaders among them, have married women from the surrounding peoples who worship other gods (9:1–2). He tore his clothes, sat appalled until the evening sacrifice, and then prayed a prayer of confession that ended with no request at all: "Behold, we are before you in our guilt" (9:15). Chapter 10 tells what happened next. The narrative switches from Ezra's "I" back to "Ezra" in the third person. The people respond, a public assembly gathers in the rain, a three-month investigation is carried out, and the book ends with a list of the men involved. It is one of the hardest endings in Scripture, and readers have wrestled with it ever since.

#### The People Confess Their Sin (vv1–17)
- **"While Ezra prayed and made confession, weeping and casting himself down" (v1).** The Hebrew form suggests he kept throwing himself to the ground. He was "before the house of God," in the temple court, in full public view. Ezra did not scold the people. He grieved, and his grief moved them. "A very great assembly of men, women, and children" gathered, and they "wept bitterly." Whole families were affected, because the sin touched whole families.
- **Shecaniah son of Jehiel, "of the sons of Elam" (v2).** A layman speaks up. In the list at the end of the chapter, a Jehiel of the sons of Elam is among the guilty (v26). If that is his father, Shecaniah's proposal cost his own family dearly. He was not pointing at others.
- **"We have broken faith with our God" (v2).** The same word as 9:2, *ma'al*, breach of trust. He says "we," as Ezra did.
- **"But even now there is hope for Israel in spite of this" (v2).** This is the turning point of the chapter. Ezra's prayer ended in silence. Shecaniah answers with hope. Not hope that the sin did not matter, but hope because the people were grieving over it and were ready to act. Where there is true sorrow for sin, there is hope (2 Cor 7:10).
- **"Let us make a covenant with our God to put away all these wives and their children" (v3).** "Put away" means send away, the language of divorce (Deut 24:1–2). "Their children" would go with their mothers, the normal custom in the ancient world when a marriage ended (compare Hagar and Ishmael, Gen 21:14). "According to the Law" (v3) points back to the commands Ezra quoted (Deut 7:3–4). The Law forbade such marriages. It said nothing about how to undo them, so Ezra and the leaders had to work that out.
- **"Those who tremble at the commandment of our God" (v3).** The same group from 9:4. Their reverence for God's word is the backbone of the reform.
- **"Arise, for it is your task, and we are with you; be strong and do it" (v4).** Ezra had authority from both God and the king (7:25–26), but he needed the community's backing. These are the words God gave Joshua, and David gave Solomon: "be strong and courageous… do it" (Josh 1:6–9; 1 Chr 28:10, 20). Shecaniah gives a grieving leader the push he needs.
- **"Made the leading priests and Levites and all Israel take an oath" (v5).** Ezra started with the leaders, who had led the way into the sin (9:2). Now they lead the way out.
- **"The chamber of Jehohanan the son of Eliashib" (v6).** The temple had rooms around its courts for priests and storage. Nehemiah 12:22–23 names a high priest Johanan, the son (or grandson) of Eliashib, the high priest in Nehemiah's time (Neh 3:1). Some scholars take this as evidence that Ezra came after Nehemiah. Others answer that both names were common, or that the room was known by a later name when the book was written. The text does not settle it.
- **"Neither eating bread nor drinking water" (v6).** A total fast, rare in Scripture. Moses fasted this way when Israel sinned with the golden calf (Deut 9:18). Ezra mourned as if someone had died.
- **"All his property should be forfeited, and he himself banned" (v8).** The penalties come from the king's grant of authority (7:26). "Forfeited" is the word for something devoted, given over entirely (*charam*), and "banned" means excluded from the community. Anyone who ignored the summons was choosing to stand outside the covenant people.
- **"The ninth month, on the twentieth day" (v9).** Kislev, roughly early December. The early rains of winter had come. "Trembling because of this matter and because of the heavy rain" is a vivid line: people shivering in a flooded square, partly from the cold and partly from fear of God. "Judah and Benjamin" were the main tribes of the returned community, though people from other tribes were among them.
- **"You have broken faith… make confession to the LORD… and do his will" (vv10–11).** Ezra's speech is short. Name the sin, confess it to God, and act on the confession. Confession without change would only be words. "Separate yourselves" (v11) is the same verb used in 9:1, where the leaders reported that the people had not separated themselves.
- **"It is so; we must do as you have said" (v12).** The whole assembly answers with one loud voice. This is not a reform imposed by a foreign official on an unwilling people. They agree it must be done.
- **"But the people are many, and it is a time of heavy rain" (v13).** A very practical objection, and a wise one. A fair process could not be done in a day, in the open, in the rain. They propose that officials represent the assembly, and each case be heard at an appointed time with the elders and judges of each town, who knew the families (v14). Justice that is careful takes time.
- **"Only Jonathan… and Jahzeiah… opposed this" (v15).** Four men stood against the plan. Their reasons are not given. They may have wanted to protect themselves or relatives, or thought the plan too harsh, or wanted faster action. The Hebrew phrase is literally "stood upon this," and older translations such as the KJV took it to mean these men *oversaw* the matter. Most modern translations read it as opposition. Meshullam may be the same man who is among the guilty in v29, though the name is common.
- **Three months of hearings (vv16–17).** From the first day of the tenth month (around late December) to the first day of the first month (around late March), Ezra and the heads of families examined each case. The text does not tell us what questions they asked. Many interpreters think they asked whether each wife had turned to the LORD, since the Law's concern was idolatry, but the text does not say so.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls Shecaniah's hope "sad, but it is not desperate; the disease threatening, but not incurable," and writes, "Weeping, in this case, is good, but reforming is better." JFB notes that Shecaniah was not himself guilty but that his father and five uncles were (v26), so he "deemed it better to obey God than to please his nearest relatives." Guzik links the response to times of revival, when conviction of sin leads people to confess and act. Both JFB and Guzik (following Adam Clarke) assume the husbands made provision for the wives and children who were sent away, which is reasonable but not stated. Henry adds that under the gospel such marriages "are not null" (compare 1 Cor 7:12–14).

#### Those Guilty of Intermarriage (vv18–44)
- **"Some of the sons of the priests" (v18).** The list starts at the top, with the family of Jeshua son of Jozadak, the high priest who had led the first return with Zerubbabel (3:2). The high priest's own relatives head the list of the guilty. Malachi 2:7–8 had already charged the priests with leading many astray.
- **"They pledged themselves" (v19).** Literally "they gave their hand," the gesture that sealed a promise (2 Kgs 10:15; Ezek 17:18).
- **"Their guilt offering was a ram of the flock" (v19).** The Law required exactly this offering for *ma'al*, the breach of faith named in 9:2 and 10:2: "If anyone commits a breach of faith… he shall bring to the LORD as his compensation a ram without blemish" (Lev 5:15). The priests did not only put the problem right. They sought atonement for it in the way God had appointed.
- **Priests, Levites, singers and gatekeepers (vv18–24).** Seventeen priests, six Levites, one singer and three gatekeepers. Many of the temple's own servants had been compromised.
- **"And of Israel" (vv25–43).** Eighty-four laymen from eleven families, most of them families from the first return (ch 2). The list totals about 111 men in all. "Of the sons of Binnui" (v38) is the ESV's reading of a difficult Hebrew phrase, following the Greek Old Testament.
- **How big was the problem?** If the community numbered around 30,000 or more, about 111 marriages was a small fraction, well under one percent of the people. But it included leading families of priests and nobles, which made it far more dangerous than the number suggests. The Law saw the risk in what such marriages would teach the next generation (9:12).
- **"Some of the women had even borne children" (v44).** The last verse is the hardest. The Hebrew is difficult, and the ESV smooths it. The parallel account in 1 Esdras, a later Greek retelling, says plainly that they sent the women away with their children. Ezra ends without comment, without a word about how it felt or what became of them. The silence is honest. This was costly obedience.
- **How should Christians read this?** Faithful readers have answered differently. Many see it as a necessary, painful act that saved the identity and faith of a tiny community at a moment when it could have vanished. God's promise of a Messiah through Israel depended on a people who remained distinct. Others see it as a severe measure that the narrator records honestly, which later Scripture does not hold up as a pattern. Both readings agree on several points: idolatry, not ethnicity, was the issue (Ruth the Moabite was welcomed); God hates the breaking of marriage covenants (Mal 2:14–16); and the New Testament gives different instructions. Believers are not to marry unbelievers (1 Cor 7:39; 2 Cor 6:14), but a believer already married to an unbeliever is to stay in the marriage if the spouse is willing, because "the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife" and the children are "holy" (1 Cor 7:12–14). Under the new covenant, faith is passed on not by keeping a nation separate but by the Spirit working through faithful believers in every family.
- **The book ends without resolution.** Ezra disappears until Nehemiah 8, about thirteen years later, when he reads the Law to the gathered people. Nehemiah faced the same problem again (Neh 13:23–27), and the people renewed the same vow (Neh 10:30). The reform was real but not permanent. The deepest problem, a heart that drifts from God, was not one any reformer could fix for good.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws exactly that lesson: "The best reformers can but do their endeavour; when the Redeemer himself shall come to Zion, he shall effectually turn away ungodliness from Jacob," and "the blood of Christ, our Sin-offering, is the only atonement which takes away our guilt." JFB says the number of men of rank on the list shows how "great and complicated" the reform was, and explains "they gave their hands" as a solemn pledge. Guzik holds that those listed were men whose wives would not turn to the LORD. That reading is possible, but it goes beyond the text. Guzik also cites John Trapp's claim that a ram, rather than a goat, showed they had sinned knowingly. The Law's own reason is simpler: a ram is the offering for a breach of faith (Lev 5:15).

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezra's reform shows both the seriousness of sin and the limits of reform. It took tears, oaths, a winter assembly in the rain, three months of hearings and broken households, and within a generation the same sin was back. The law could name the sin and require a ram for the guilt, but it could not change the heart. Jesus came for exactly that. He is the guilt offering, the one whose soul was made "an offering for guilt" (Isa 53:10). He does not keep his people pure by sending outsiders away. He brings outsiders in, making Jew and Gentile "one new man" and breaking down "the dividing wall of hostility" (Eph 2:14–15), while still calling his people to be holy. The holiness Ezra fought to protect from the outside, Christ creates from the inside by his Spirit.
