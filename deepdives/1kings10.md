*Setting:* The middle years of Solomon's reign, around 950–940 BC. The temple and palace are finished (ch 6–7), the temple has been dedicated (ch 8), and God has answered with a promise and a warning (ch 9). Chapter 9 ended with Solomon's fleet sailing from the Red Sea port of Ezion-geber to bring back gold from Ophir. Chapter 10 shows what the world saw when it looked at Israel: a foreign queen who travels a great distance and goes home amazed, and a flood of gold, ivory and horses pouring into Jerusalem. This is the peak of Israel's glory in the Old Testament. It is told with real admiration, but the details quietly line up with the three things Deuteronomy told a king not to multiply: gold, horses and wives (Deut 17:16–17). The gold and horses are here, and the wives arrive in chapter 11. Second Chronicles 9:1–28 tells the same story.

#### The Queen of Sheba (vv1–13)
- **"The queen of Sheba" (v1).** Sheba (Hebrew *Sheva*) was the kingdom of the Sabeans in southwestern Arabia, modern Yemen, about 1,200–1,500 miles from Jerusalem by the incense road. It grew rich on frankincense and myrrh, which grew there and were in demand across the ancient world for worship, medicine and burial. Sabean inscriptions name later rulers but have not named this queen. Ethiopian tradition claims her too, and holds that she bore Solomon a son, Menelik, founder of Ethiopia's royal line. That story first appears in writing more than two thousand years later and is not in the text.
- **"Heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD" (v1).** An unusual phrase. Solomon's fame was tied to his God. His wisdom was a gift from the LORD (3:12), and the temple was "a house for the name of the LORD" (8:20). This is exactly what Solomon prayed for in 8:41–43: a foreigner who hears of God's great name and comes from a far country.
- **"She came to test him with hard questions" (v1).** The word is *chidot*, riddles or puzzling sayings, the word used for Samson's riddle (Judg 14:12). Riddles and proverbs were a way of testing a ruler's wisdom across the ancient Near East. There was probably also business on the table: Solomon's fleet in the Red Sea was now cutting into the trade routes her kingdom depended on, and a queen arriving **"with camels bearing spices and very much gold"** (v2) looks like a trade delegation too.
- **"She told him all that was on her mind" (v2).** Literally "all that was in her heart." This was not only a contest. She brought her real questions. **"There was nothing hidden from the king that he could not explain to her"** (v3).
- **What she saw (vv4–5).** The list moves from wisdom to the house, the table, the officials, the servants and their uniforms, the cupbearers, and finally worship. Then the last item, **"his burnt offerings that he offered at the house of the LORD."** The Hebrew can also be read "the ascent by which he went up to the house of the LORD" (ESV footnote), meaning a grand stairway or covered passage from the palace to the temple. Either way, the tour ends at the temple.
- **"There was no more breath in her" (v5).** The word is *ruach*, breath or spirit. She was breathless. The same phrase is used of the Canaanite kings who lost heart when they heard Israel had crossed the Jordan (Josh 5:1).
- **"The half was not told me" (v7).** She had not believed the reports, and seeing it for herself, she found the truth bigger than the rumor. This line has become proverbial.
- **"Happy are your men! Happy are your servants" (v8).** "Happy" is *ashrei*, the word that opens Psalm 1, "Blessed is the man." She envies those who get to stand near wisdom every day. The Hebrew here and in 2 Chronicles 9:7 reads "your men." The Greek Old Testament reads "your wives," and a few scholars think that was the original. The ESV follows the Hebrew.
- **"Blessed be the LORD your God, who has delighted in you" (v9).** She praises Israel's God by name. Whether this was personal faith or the courteous language of a foreign ruler honoring her host's god is debated. Hiram used similar words (5:7). Either way, her words are good theology: God made Solomon king **"because the LORD loved Israel forever… that you may execute justice and righteousness."** Kingship exists for the people's sake and for justice, not for the king's glory. That is the very standard Solomon is about to forget.
- **"120 talents of gold, and a very great quantity of spices" (v10).** About four and a half tons of gold, the same amount Hiram had sent (9:14). **"Never again came such an abundance of spices."** The magi brought the same kinds of gifts, gold and frankincense and myrrh, to another son of David (Matt 2:11; compare Isa 60:6, "those from Sheba shall come. They shall bring gold and frankincense").
- **"Almug wood" (vv11–12).** This aside about Hiram's fleet interrupts the queen's story. The wood is unidentified. Many think it was red sandalwood from India, and the word appears in texts from Ugarit and Alalakh as a prized wood. Chronicles spells it "algum" (2 Chr 2:8; 9:10). The ESV says it was used for **"supports"** for the temple and palace. The Hebrew word is uncertain and may mean railings, steps or inlay. It was also used for **"lyres and harps for the singers."**
- **"Gave to the queen of Sheba all that she desired" (v13).** Royal visits ended with an exchange of gifts. Solomon gave her what she asked "besides" his ordinary royal gifts. The romantic legends later built on "all that she desired" go beyond the text.
- **Commentators:** Henry takes the queen to be from southern Arabia and says she came "to prove him," but also to learn of his God. He says Solomon "made things surprisingly easy which she apprehended insuperably difficult." He follows the KJV's "ascent," a stairway to the temple. He turns "the half was not told" into a promise that the half has not been told "of the pleasures of Wisdom's ways." JFB favors Yemen over Ethiopia, calls the hard questions "enigmas or riddles," and mentions Jewish writers who thought she converted. Guzik is cautious: he thinks her words may have been courteous rather than a confession of faith, and warns that impressive buildings alone do not bring seekers to commitment. He calls the Menelik legend fanciful. The Tyndale notes say the same of the later romance stories.

#### Solomon's Great Wealth (vv14–29)
- **"666 talents of gold" (v14).** About 25 tons a year. Some readers notice that 666 is also the number of the beast in Revelation 13:18. There is no sign the writer of Kings intended any symbolism, and Revelation does not cite this verse. The two numbers may simply be the same. But it is fair to say the wealth described here is exactly what the law warned a king not to pile up: "nor shall he acquire for himself excessive silver and gold" (Deut 17:17).
- **"Besides that which came from the explorers and… merchants" (v15).** Solomon also taxed trade. Israel lay across the main land routes between Egypt, Arabia, Mesopotamia and Anatolia. **"All the kings of the west"** can also be read "all the kings of Arabia," which is what 2 Chronicles 9:14 has. The Hebrew words for "west" and "Arabia" differ only in their vowels.
- **"200 large shields of beaten gold; 600 shekels of gold went into each" (v16).** About 15 pounds of gold apiece, hammered over a frame. The 300 smaller shields took **"three minas"** each (v17). Chronicles gives 300 shekels for these smaller shields (2 Chr 9:16). If a mina was 100 shekels, as in some ancient systems, the two agree. Gold is too soft and heavy for battle, so these were ceremonial, carried by the palace guard (14:27–28). They were hung in the **House of the Forest of Lebanon** (7:2). Within five years of Solomon's death, Pharaoh Shishak carried them all off, and Rehoboam replaced them with bronze (14:25–28). Gold became bronze in one generation.
- **"A great ivory throne… overlaid with the finest gold" (v18).** Probably wood inlaid with ivory panels and gilded, since solid ivory thrones were not made. Carved ivory panels from royal furniture have been found at Samaria and at Nimrud in Assyria. **Six steps, a round top, armrests, and lions:** two beside the armrests and twelve on the steps (vv19–20), perhaps one for each tribe. The lion was the emblem of Judah (Gen 49:9) and a common symbol of royal power. **"The like of it was never made in any kingdom."**
- **"Silver was not considered as anything in the days of Solomon" (v21).** All the drinking cups were gold. Verse 27 adds that he made **"silver as common in Jerusalem as stone."**
- **"Ships of Tarshish" (v22).** Tarshish was a far western port, usually identified with Tartessus in Spain or a Phoenician colony in the western Mediterranean. Jonah tried to flee there (Jonah 1:3). "Ship of Tarshish" became the name for a large, ocean-going trading ship, the way "clipper" or "East Indiaman" was used later. These sailed from the Red Sea, so they cannot have gone to Spain. **"Once every three years"** suggests long voyages, perhaps to Africa or India.
- **"Gold, silver, ivory, apes, and peacocks" (v22).** The last two words are uncertain. "Apes" (*qophim*) may be monkeys, and "peacocks" (*tukkiyim*) is the traditional rendering from old Jewish and Syriac translations. Some connect the word to a South Indian word for peacock, which would point to India. Others read it as "baboons." Exotic animals were trophies for ancient kings, proof of reaching the ends of the earth.
- **"King Solomon excelled all the kings of the earth in riches and in wisdom" (v23).** This is the promise of 3:13 fulfilled: God gave him what he did not ask for, riches and honor. **"Which God had put into his mind" (v24)**: literally "into his heart." The writer keeps reminding readers where the wisdom came from.
- **"Every one of them brought his present" (v25).** "Present" (*minchah*) is the word for tribute as well as gift. Foreign kings came year by year. The scene looks ahead to Psalm 72, a psalm "of Solomon": "May the kings of Tarshish and of the coastlands render him tribute; may the kings of Sheba and Seba bring gifts!" (Ps 72:10). Isaiah 60 pictures the nations streaming to Zion with wealth in the age to come.
- **"1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen" (v26).** Israel had barely used chariots before. David hamstrung captured chariot horses (2 Sam 8:4). Solomon built a standing chariot army in his **chariot cities** (9:19). For scale, the Assyrian records of the battle of Qarqar in 853 BC say King Ahab of Israel brought 2,000 chariots. Long-pillared buildings at Megiddo were once called "Solomon's stables," but they are now usually dated later, and whether they were stables is debated.
- **"Cedar as plentiful as the sycamore of the Shephelah" (v27).** The Shephelah is the low hill country between Judah's highlands and the coast, where the common sycamore-fig grew everywhere. Expensive imported cedar became as ordinary as backyard trees.
- **"Solomon's import of horses was from Egypt and Kue" (v28).** Kue was Cilicia, in southeastern Turkey, famous for horses. Some scholars think "Egypt" (*Mitsrayim*) here should be "Musri," a land in Anatolia with a similar name, which would put both sources in the north. Most translations keep Egypt, which fits the warning of Deuteronomy 17:16: the king "must not acquire many horses for himself or cause the people to return to Egypt in order to acquire many horses."
- **"A chariot… for 600 shekels of silver and a horse for 150" (v29).** These were the going rates, and Solomon's agents acted as middlemen, selling on to the **"kings of the Hittites and the kings of Syria."** The Hittites here are the Neo-Hittite city-states of northern Syria. Israel had become an arms dealer to its neighbors.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sets the wealth against Deuteronomy 17:16–17 and reads the horse trade as plain disobedience, adding that serious disobedience often begins with a clever rationalisation. He also notes that gold shields gave Solomon the image of a warrior king without the substance. He mentions 666 and Revelation 13:18 but does not press it. JFB says the shields were normally "made of wood and covered with leather," and that the throne was "not of solid ivory, but veneered." He takes Tarshish as Tartessus and then "a general term for the West," and thinks the apes and peacocks were bought on the African coast. JFB skips most of vv23–29 and refers readers to Chronicles. The Tyndale notes read the section more favorably, as Solomon using wealth wisely, and add the comparison with Ahab's 2,000 chariots.

#### Pointing to Christ
The queen of Sheba traveled a thousand miles to hear wisdom and went home breathless. Jesus pointed to her as a rebuke to people who would not cross the street to hear him: "The queen of the South will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and behold, something greater than Solomon is here" (Matt 12:42). Solomon's wisdom drew gold and tribute to Jerusalem, and that wealth became a trap. Christ "became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Cor 8:9), and in him "are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Col 2:3). The nations' gifts finally come to the king who uses his throne for "justice and righteousness" (v9) without fail (Rev 21:24–26).
