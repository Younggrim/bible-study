*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. Chapters 18–20 told of David's victories, with God giving him success "wherever he went." Now, near the end of his reign, David sins in a way that brings death on thousands of his people. The story is also told in 2 Samuel 24, which comes at the very end of that book as a kind of appendix. The Chronicler places it here for a reason: this disaster leads David to the place where the temple will be built. A plague stops at a threshing floor just north of the city, an angel's sword is sheathed there, and God answers David's sacrifice with fire from heaven. The next verse (22:1) names the spot: "Here shall be the house of the LORD God." Solomon's temple was later built on this very site, which Chronicles identifies with Mount Moriah, where Abraham once offered Isaac (2 Chr 3:1). The Chronicler left out David's sin with Bathsheba, but he tells this sin in full, because out of judgment and mercy here came the place where Israel would meet God for centuries.

#### David's Census Brings Pestilence (vv1–17)
- **"Then Satan stood against Israel and incited David to number Israel" (v1).** 2 Samuel 24:1 says, "the anger of the LORD was kindled against Israel, and he incited David." Chronicles names Satan. This is not a contradiction but two angles on one event, as in Job 1–2, where Satan attacks Job but only by God's permission and within God's limits. God, in judgment on Israel for reasons not stated, allowed Satan to tempt David, and David freely chose to do it. All three are true at once: God is sovereign, Satan is malicious, and David is responsible. Scripture holds them together elsewhere too (Gen 50:20; Acts 2:23).
- **Is "Satan" a name here?** The Hebrew word *satan* means "adversary" or "accuser." In Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3:1–2 it has the article: "the satan," "the accuser." Here it has no article, which may make it a personal name, the only clear case in the Old Testament. Some scholars think it means simply "an adversary," a human enemy (the same word in 1 Kings 11:14). Most read it as the devil. The Tyndale notes take the Chronicler to be reading Samuel's account in light of a fuller understanding of Satan as the enemy of God and of people.
- **"Go, number Israel, from Beersheba to Dan" (v2).** From the far south to the far north, the whole kingdom. "That I may know their number" shows David's aim: to measure his strength. Censuses in themselves were not wrong. God commanded two in Numbers 1 and 26. But God had commanded those. This one was David's idea, and Joab, who managed it, was told to count "men who drew the sword" (v5), fighting men.
- **Why was this a sin?** The text does not say directly, and commentators have offered several reasons. The Law required that whenever Israel was counted, each man pay a half-shekel "ransom for his life… that there be no plague among them" (Exod 30:12). That may not have been done. More likely the heart of it was pride and self-reliance: the king of a nation God had built from nothing was measuring his own power. Israel belonged to God, not to David. Joab's protest points the same way (v3).
- **"May the LORD add to his people a hundred times as many as they are!… Why should it be a cause of guilt for Israel?" (v3).** Joab is not a man of tender conscience. He has murdered men in cold blood (2 Sam 3:27; 18:14). Yet even he can see this is wrong. His argument: the people are already yours, and God can multiply them as he wills, so what is gained by counting them? "Guilt" (*ashmah*) is the word for guilt that requires a guilt offering. Joab saw that the whole nation would pay for the king's pride.
- **"But the king's word prevailed against Joab" (v4).** 2 Samuel 24:8 says the count took nine months and twenty days.
- **"1,100,000 men… and in Judah 470,000" (v5).** 2 Samuel 24:9 gives 800,000 for Israel and 500,000 for Judah. The differences may come from what was included (for example, David's standing army of 288,000 in 27:1–15), from the census being left unfinished (27:24 says "Joab… began to count, but did not finish"), or from copying. Some scholars also think the Hebrew *eleph*, "thousand," could sometimes mean a military unit, which would make these figures much smaller. The text does not let us settle it.
- **"He did not include Levi and Benjamin" (v6).** Only Chronicles has this detail. The Levites were never counted with the fighting men (Num 1:47–49). Benjamin may have been left out because Joab stopped before finishing (27:24), or because the tabernacle at Gibeon stood in Benjamin's territory. The stated reason is that "the king's command was abhorrent to Joab." Even the hardened general would not carry it out completely.
- **"But God was displeased with this thing, and he struck Israel" (v7).** In Samuel, David's conscience strikes him first (2 Sam 24:10). In Chronicles, God's displeasure comes first, and David's confession follows.
- **"I have sinned greatly… I have acted very foolishly" (v8).** David's confession is immediate and has no excuses. He uses three words for wrongdoing: "sinned," "iniquity" and "foolishly." In Scripture, "folly" is not a lack of intelligence but a moral failure, acting as if God did not matter. This is why David remains "a man after God's own heart." He was not sinless, but he did not defend himself when God exposed him.
- **"Gad, David's seer" (v9).** Gad had been with David since his fugitive years (1 Sam 22:5). He later helped organize the temple musicians (2 Chr 29:25), and Chronicles cites "the Chronicles of Gad the seer" as a source (29:29). A "seer" is an older term for a prophet (1 Sam 9:9).
- **"Three years of famine, or three months of devastation… or else three days of the sword of the LORD" (v12).** Each option is worse in intensity and shorter in time. 2 Samuel 24:13 in the standard Hebrew text reads "seven years of famine," but the ancient Greek translation of Samuel has "three," matching Chronicles and the pattern of threes. A copying error in Samuel is the likely explanation. "The sword of the LORD" is explained as "pestilence… with the angel of the LORD destroying." Plague was the judgment the Law warned would come from an unransomed census (Exod 30:12).
- **"Let me fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is very great, but do not let me fall into the hand of man" (v13).** David chooses the judgment that comes most directly from God. Famine would let the rich (including the king) buy food while the poor starved. War would let the king hide behind his army. Plague strikes everyone alike, the palace as well as the village. And David trusts God's mercy (*rachamim*, a tender, deeply felt compassion) more than any human enemy's.
- **"70,000 men of Israel fell" (v14).** The nation David had counted was cut down. The numbers he was proud of became numbers of the dead.
- **"The LORD saw, and he relented from the calamity… 'It is enough; now stay your hand'" (v15).** "Relented" (*nacham*) means God turned from the judgment he had begun. This is not God changing his mind as if surprised. It is God showing the mercy David had appealed to. He stops the angel at the edge of Jerusalem, at the threshing floor of **Ornan the Jebusite**. Samuel calls him Araunah, which may be a Hurrian title or name. The Jebusites were the people who held Jerusalem before David took it (11:4–6). Ornan was one of them who had stayed on, living peacefully under Israelite rule.
- **"Standing between earth and heaven, and in his hand a drawn sword stretched out over Jerusalem" (v16).** Chronicles describes the angel far more vividly than Samuel does. The same picture appears when the angel blocks Balaam's road (Num 22:23, 31) and when the commander of the LORD's army meets Joshua (Josh 5:13). **David and the elders, clothed in sackcloth, fell upon their faces.** Sackcloth was coarse goat-hair cloth worn in mourning and repentance. Only Chronicles mentions it.
- **"But these sheep, what have they done?" (v17).** David the shepherd king, who once risked his life for his father's flock (1 Sam 17:34–35), now pleads for his people. "Let your hand… be against me and against my father's house." He offers himself and his family in place of the nation. It is the right instinct of a true shepherd. But David himself was a sinner, and his life could not ransom the people. God provides another way, in the next verse.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads the two accounts together: Satan provoked David, while the LORD allowed it as discipline on Israel. He insists that counting is not wrong in itself, and that the motive, pride rather than service, made this a sin, with Exodus 30:12 in the background. JFB puts it that God acted "by withdrawing His grace at this time from David." On Joab's words JFB notes that "the people frequently suffer for the misconduct of their rulers." On v6, JFB explains that Levi was not a warrior tribe. Guzik praises David for choosing to face the same danger as his people, not to shield himself. Henry observes that "no mention is made in this book of David's sin in the matter of Uriah," and that this sin is told because it leads to the place of the temple.

#### David Builds an Altar (vv18–30)
- **"The angel of the LORD had commanded Gad to say to David that David should go up and raise an altar" (v18).** God's answer to David's plea is an altar. The way to stop the judgment is not David's death but sacrifice. "Go up," because the threshing floor stood higher than the City of David, on the hill to its north.
- **"Ornan was threshing wheat" (v20).** Wheat was harvested in late spring and early summer. Threshing floors were flat, open areas on high ground, where the wind could blow away the chaff. Oxen dragged a heavy wooden "threshing sledge," studded underneath with sharp stones, over the grain to break the heads. Ornan and his sons also saw the angel, a detail only Chronicles gives, and the sons hid in terror.
- **"Give me the site of the threshing floor… give it to me at its full price" (v22).** The phrase "at its full price" (*bekesef male*, literally "for full silver") is the same phrase Abraham used when he bought the cave of Machpelah from Ephron the Hittite (Gen 23:9). Abraham's purchase gave Israel its first rightful piece of the promised land, a burial place. David's purchase gives Israel its holy place. Neither would accept it as a gift.
- **"I give it all" (v23).** Ornan offers everything: the land, the oxen for sacrifices, the wooden sledges for fuel and the wheat for a grain offering. It was a generous and probably sincere offer, made to a king with an angel's sword overhead.
- **"I will not take for the LORD what is yours, nor offer burnt offerings that cost me nothing" (v24).** This is one of the most quoted lines in David's life. A sacrifice that costs the worshiper nothing is not really his offering. David had sinned, and the offering for that sin must be his own.
- **"David paid Ornan 600 shekels of gold by weight for the site" (v25).** That is about fifteen pounds (7 kg) of gold. 2 Samuel 24:24 says David bought "the threshing floor and the oxen for fifty shekels of silver." Many think the two refer to different purchases: fifty shekels of silver for the threshing floor and oxen that day, and six hundred shekels of gold for the whole "site" (Hebrew *maqom*, "place"), the larger area of the hilltop that would hold the temple and its courts. JFB takes that view. "Place" is also a loaded word in Israel's story: Deuteronomy speaks again and again of "the place that the LORD your God will choose… to put his name" (Deut 12:5).
- **"The LORD answered him with fire from heaven upon the altar of burnt offering" (v26).** Only Chronicles mentions the fire. Fire from heaven marks God's acceptance at the great turning points of Israel's worship: at the first sacrifices of the tabernacle (Lev 9:24), when Elijah faced the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18:38), and when Solomon dedicated the temple on this same spot (2 Chr 7:1). God himself confirms the place.
- **"He put his sword back into its sheath" (v27).** The judgment ends not at David's confession, nor at his offer of himself, but at the sacrifice God accepted.
- **"For the tabernacle of the LORD… were at that time in the high place at Gibeon" (v29).** These closing verses explain why David sacrificed here instead of at the official altar Moses made. Normally all sacrifices were to be offered at the tabernacle (Lev 17:8–9; Deut 12:13–14), which was at Gibeon (16:39–40). But God had commanded this altar through his prophet, and David "was afraid of the sword of the angel" (v30). The Chronicler shows that the move to a new place of worship was not David's whim. It was God's command, sealed with fire.
- **Moriah.** 2 Chronicles 3:1 says Solomon built the temple "on Mount Moriah, where the LORD had appeared to David his father, at the place that David had appointed, on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite." Moriah is where Abraham was told to offer Isaac and where God provided a ram in Isaac's place (Gen 22:2, 13–14). Abraham named it "The LORD will provide," and "on the mount of the LORD it shall be provided." Chronicles is the only book that links the two sites by name. Most scholars accept that the temple mount and Abraham's Moriah are the same hill, as Jewish tradition has always held.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls the command to build an altar "a blessed token of reconciliation," and draws the line to Christ: "it pleased the Lord to bruise him, that through him, God might be to us, not a consuming Fire, but a reconciled God." On vv28–30 he writes, "Here God graciously met me, therefore I will still expect to meet him." JFB suggests David first bought only the oxen and implements for fifty shekels of silver and later bought the whole site. It places the altar "on or close to the spot where Abraham had offered up Isaac" and lists the other times fire fell from heaven. Guzik makes v24 the heart of his application: worship that costs nothing is worth nothing. He also points out that the 70,000 deaths did not atone for the sin. Only a God-appointed sacrifice did.

#### Pointing to Christ
On this hill Abraham was told to offer his son, and God provided a lamb instead. On this hill David offered to die for his people, and God provided an altar instead. On this hill the temple stood, and for a thousand years sacrifices were offered there. Not far away, outside the city walls, God's own Son was offered: the true Shepherd who did not merely offer to die for the sheep but laid down his life for them (John 10:11). David would not offer God what cost him nothing. God gave what cost him everything: "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32). The angel's sword was sheathed when God accepted the sacrifice, and judgment is turned away from all who shelter under Christ's (Rom 5:9).
