*Setting:* "To the choirmaster: according to Jeduthun. A Psalm of David." Jeduthun was one of the three chief musicians David appointed to lead worship, with Asaph and Heman (1 Chr 16:41–42; 25:1–3). His name also heads Psalms 39 and 77. "According to Jeduthun" may mean the psalm was given to his choir, or sung in a style linked to him. No occasion is named. The enemies are men who want to push David down "from his high position" (v4), which the Tyndale notes take as the royal throne, and who flatter him to his face while cursing him in private. That fits the years of Absalom's conspiracy, when Absalom "stole the hearts of the men of Israel" with smiles and kisses at the city gate (2 Sam 15:1–6), though nothing in the psalm requires it. What stands out is the calm. Guzik notes that the psalm asks God for nothing. There is no petition, no lament, no fear. Henry: "This psalm has nothing in it directly either of prayer or praise." It is a psalm of trust from first to last. Its key word is a small Hebrew word, *'ak*, "alone, only, surely," which appears six times (vv1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9).

#### My Soul Waits in Silence (vv1–4)
- **"For God alone my soul waits in silence" (v1).** The Hebrew word order is striking: "Only toward God, silence, my soul." The noun *dumiyyah* means silence, stillness, quiet rest. It is not the silence of having nothing to say. It is the silence of a soul that has stopped arguing, struggling and scheming, and has settled down to wait on God. JFB: "literally, 'is silent,' trusts submissively and confidently as a servant." Henry: the soul "says nothing against what he does, but quietly expects what he will do." The same thought appears in 37:7: "Be still before the LORD and wait patiently for him."
- **"Alone" (v1).** The little word *'ak* can mean "only" or "surely, truly." The King James translates it "Truly" in v1 and "only" in v2. The ESV uses "alone" and "only" throughout, which brings out the point: not God *and* something else. Trapp, cited by Guzik, says trusting God partly is like standing with one foot on a rock and the other on quicksand.
- **"From him comes my salvation" (v1).** "Salvation" (*yeshu'ah*) here means rescue, deliverance, in the broadest sense. It is the word from which the name Jesus (*Yeshua*) comes.
- **"He alone is my rock and my salvation, my fortress" (v2).** Three pictures for God: a rock (stable, unmovable), salvation (rescue), and a fortress (*misgav*, the high stronghold of 59:9). JFB notes how often these titles appear (9:9; 18:2).
- **"I shall not be greatly shaken" (v2).** "Greatly" is honest. David expects to be shaken, but not toppled. Henry: "I may be shocked, but I shall not be sunk." Paul says much the same: "perplexed, but not driven to despair" (2 Cor 4:8). Notice how this changes in v6.
- **"How long will all of you attack a man to batter him?" (v3).** He turns to his enemies. Many against "a man," one person. The verb "batter" is rare, and translations differ. The ESV has "attack… to batter him"; the King James has "imagine mischief against a man." The sense is that they keep piling on.
- **"Like a leaning wall, a tottering fence?" (v3).** Who is the leaning wall? The Hebrew allows either. In the ESV, the wall is the man under attack: they keep pushing at him as if he were a crumbling wall ready to be knocked over. In the King James and JFB, the wall is the enemies themselves: "ye shall be slain all of you: as a bowing wall shall ye be," weak and about to fall. JFB mentions the ESV-type reading and calls his own "a good sense." Either way, the image is a stone wall bulging outward, about to collapse. Guzik notes the translations differ. Henry follows the King James and points to Saul and his men falling on Mount Gilboa.
- **"They only plan to thrust him down from his high position" (v4).** *'Ak* again: this is all they think about. "High position" (*se'et*) means dignity, elevation. JFB: "the elevation to which God had raised him." They are trying to undo what God did.
- **"They take pleasure in falsehood. They bless with their mouths, but inwardly they curse. Selah" (v4).** The worst enemies are the smiling ones. Greeting someone with a blessing was everyday courtesy in Israel (Ruth 2:4). These people give the blessing and mean a curse. Henry: "they compliment David to his face, but… in their hearts they wish him all mischief." Ahithophel and Absalom both did exactly this.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the soul waiting on God is "in the way both of duty and comfort" when it refers all its affairs "to his will and wisdom." He reads "excellency" in v4 two ways: David's royal dignity, and his integrity, since sin "is the only thing that can effectually cast us down from our excellency." Guzik passes on Amyraut's observation that the psalm contains no word of fear or dejection. JFB adds that no injury devised by enemies "shall be permanent."

#### My Hope Is from Him (vv5–8)
- **"For God alone, O my soul, wait in silence" (v5).** The opening line returns, but now as a command to himself. In v1 his soul *was* waiting in silence. In v5 he tells it to. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says David is urging himself back to the stillness he has just described. That is how faith actually works. It has to be renewed. The verb here (*dommi*) is from the same root as the noun in v1.
- **"For my hope is from him" (v5).** "Hope" (*tiqvah*) is the word for a cord or rope (Josh 2:18, Rahab's scarlet cord), and hope is the line that stretches out toward something not yet seen. In v1 his salvation comes from God. In v5 his hope does too. Henry: "His salvation being from God, all his other expectations are from him."
- **"He only is my rock and my salvation, my fortress; I shall not be shaken" (v6).** The refrain of v2 again, with one word dropped: "greatly." In v2 he would not be *greatly* shaken. Now he will not be shaken at all. JFB: "his confidence has increased." Maclaren, cited by Guzik, sees the same growth. Henry: "The more faith is acted the more active it is. It grows by being exercised."
- **"On God rests my salvation and my glory" (v7).** "Glory" (*kavod*) here means honor, standing, reputation, the very thing his enemies were trying to "thrust down" (v4). David's honor does not depend on what people say about him. It rests on God.
- **"My mighty rock, my refuge is God" (v7).** Literally "the rock of my strength," JFB's "strongest support." Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, notes how often David says "my": not just a rock but *my* rock.
- **"Trust in him at all times, O people" (v8).** David turns to everyone around him. What he has learned for himself, he now commends to all of God's people. "At all times" means in good times as well as bad. Henry: "Those that have found the comfort of the ways of God themselves will invite others into those ways."
- **"Pour out your heart before him" (v8).** This is not the silence of v1. It may seem like a contradiction, but it is the other side of the same trust. Silent waiting does not mean bottled-up feelings. It means bringing everything to God, emptying the heart out before him, and then resting. Hannah said at Shiloh, "I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD" (1 Sam 1:15). The image is of water poured out of a jar, every drop. Le Blanc, cited by Guzik, notes that water, unlike oil or wine, leaves nothing behind. Henry hears an echo of the drink offerings poured out before the LORD. Lamentations 2:19 says, "Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord."
- **"God is a refuge for us. Selah" (v8).** "For us" now, not just "for me." The refuge is wide enough for the whole people.
- **Commentators:** Henry defines prayer as "the pouring out of the heart before God. We must lay our grievances before him, offer up our desires to him with all humble freedom, and then entirely refer ourselves to his disposal." JFB: pouring out the heart means to "give full expression to feeling" (1 Sam 1:15; Ps 42:4). Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says David passes on to others the comfort he himself has found.

#### Power and Steadfast Love Belong to God (vv9–12)
- **"Those of low estate are but a breath; those of high estate are a delusion" (v9).** Literally "sons of *adam*" and "sons of *ish*," two Hebrew words for "man." Many translations take them as the common and the great, the poor and the powerful. VanGemeren, cited by Guzik, reads them more simply as all humanity, and Guzik says the difference may just be poetic. "Breath" is *hevel*, the key word of Ecclesiastes ("vanity of vanities," Eccl 1:2), a puff of air, a vapor. "Delusion" (*kazav*) means a lie. The common people cannot help, and the great people promise help and do not deliver. Clarke, cited by Guzik, makes that contrast.
- **"In the balances they go up; they are together lighter than a breath" (v9).** The picture is a merchant's scale. Put all of humanity on one side and a breath of air on the other, and humanity rises. It weighs nothing. Kidner, cited by Guzik, says the point is not that people are worthless but that we have nothing to *hope* from them in place of God. Isaiah says the same: "Stop regarding man in whose nostrils is breath, for of what account is he?" (Isa 2:22).
- **"Put no trust in extortion; set no vain hopes on robbery" (v10).** If people are no foundation, neither is money gained by force. "Extortion" is taking by intimidation, and "robbery" is plain theft, as the Tyndale notes explain. "Set no vain hopes" uses the verb form of *hevel*: do not become empty by trusting in what is empty (Jer 2:5).
- **"If riches increase, set not your heart on them" (v10).** Then comes the harder warning. Even honestly earned wealth is no place to rest one's heart. JFB: "even wealth, increasing lawfully, ought not to engross the heart." Paul seems to echo this: "As for the rich in this present age, charge them not to be haughty, nor to set their hopes on the uncertainty of riches, but on God" (1 Tim 6:17), as Kidner, cited by Guzik, suggests. Jesus warned, "where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matt 6:21). Guzik lists three ways the heart gets set on money: delighting in it, feeling secure because of it, and growing proud of it.
- **"Once God has spoken; twice have I heard this" (v11).** A Hebrew way of giving emphasis, a "one… two" pattern (Job 33:14; 40:5; Prov 6:16). JFB: it gives "emphasis to the sentiment." God has said it clearly, and David has heard it more than once and taken it in.
- **"That power belongs to God, and that to you, O Lord, belongs steadfast love" (vv11–12).** The two things David heard: God is strong, and God is loving. *'Oz* (power, strength) and *chesed* (steadfast, covenant love). Each needs the other. Perowne, cited by Guzik, says power without love would be brutality, and love without power would be weakness. In God both are complete. That is why he alone is worth waiting for. People are a breath, but God is both able and willing.
- **"For you will render to a man according to his work" (v12).** The psalm ends with judgment. God's power and love mean he will set things right. The righteous who wait will not be forgotten, and the liars of v4 will not get away with it. Paul quotes this line almost word for word: God "will render to each one according to his works" (Rom 2:6), and Jesus says the same of his return (Matt 16:27; Rev 22:12). JFB links the clause to "I heard this": David heard that God, by his power, "can show both mercy and justice."
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes that v12 sounds like a threat, but it is also a promise: God rewards faithfulness the world despises. Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, says man neither helps nor rewards, but God does both. Horne, cited by Guzik, reads God's power as his ability to punish sin and his mercy as his willingness to pardon it. JFB: "God's power is tempered by His mercy, which it also sustains."

#### Pointing to Christ
The New Testament quotes the last line of Psalm 62 as a warning, but its heart is an invitation: wait silently for God alone, because power and steadfast love both belong to him. In Jesus they meet perfectly. He had all power, yet "like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isa 53:7). Before his accusers he kept silent (Matt 26:63), entrusting himself to the God who judges justly (1 Pet 2:23). Like David, he was attacked by men who greeted him with a kiss and meant betrayal (Luke 22:48). And in his death and resurrection God's strength and God's love were shown together: the power that raised him from the dead (Eph 1:19–20) is the love that "while we were still sinners" sent him to die for us (Rom 5:8). He is the rock, the salvation and the fortress, and those who rest on him "shall not be shaken."
