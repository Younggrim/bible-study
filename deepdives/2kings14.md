*Setting:* About 796–753 BC. In Judah, Joash has been assassinated by his own officers (12:20–21), and his son Amaziah takes the throne. In Israel, Jehoash son of Jehoahaz has begun to win back cities from Syria, three victories as Elisha foretold (13:25). Syria itself is weakened, battered by Assyrian campaigns, and Assyria will soon turn inward with troubles of its own. That breathing space lets both Hebrew kingdoms grow strong again. This chapter shows two very different uses of the moment. Amaziah wins a war, grows proud and picks a fight with his brother kingdom, and loses badly. In the north, Jeroboam II, an idolatrous king, is used by a merciful God to restore Israel's borders to something like Solomon's. It is the era of the prophets Jonah, Amos and Hosea.

#### Amaziah Reigns in Judah (vv1–22)
- **"He was twenty-five years old when he began to reign, and he reigned twenty-nine years" (v2).** About 796–767 BC. His mother, **Jehoaddin of Jerusalem**, is named as usual for Judah's kings.
- **"He did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, yet not like David his father" (v3).** David is the measuring stick for every king of Judah. Amaziah passes, but only just. Chronicles explains: "yet not with a whole heart" (2 Chr 25:2). **"He did in all things as Joash his father had done"**, which, given how his father ended, is not unmixed praise.
- **"But the high places were not removed" (v4).** The same line as for Joash (12:3). Local altars outside the temple stayed in use.
- **"As soon as the royal power was firmly in his hand, he struck down his servants who had struck down the king his father" (v5).** He waits until he is secure, since the assassins evidently had powerful backing.
- **"But he did not put to death the children of the murderers" (v6).** In the ancient world a traitor's whole family was often wiped out with him, to prevent revenge (compare what Jehu did, 10:11). Amaziah follows the law instead and quotes Deuteronomy 24:16: "each one shall die for his own sin." This is the first time Kings quotes the written "Book of the Law of Moses" as the reason for a king's decision. Ezekiel later preaches the same principle at length (Ezek 18:20).
- **"He struck down ten thousand Edomites in the Valley of Salt and took Sela by storm" (v7).** Edom had broken free under his grandfather Jehoram (8:20–22). The Valley of Salt lay near the south end of the Dead Sea, where David had won a victory too (2 Sam 8:13). "Sela" means "rock." It was a rocky stronghold in Edom's mountains. It is often identified with Petra, but many scholars now prefer a site called es-Sela, a few miles north of Petra. Amaziah renamed it **Joktheel**, probably meaning "subdued by God."
- **What Chronicles adds.** Amaziah first hired 100,000 soldiers from Israel for 100 talents of silver. A man of God warned him, "the LORD is not with Israel," and he sent them home, losing the money. The angry mercenaries raided towns of Judah on their way back. Then, after his victory, he brought home the gods of Edom and worshiped them, and when a prophet rebuked him he told the prophet to be quiet or be killed. The prophet replied, "God has determined to destroy you" (2 Chr 25:6–16). That is the background to the next scene.
- **"Come, let us look one another in the face" (v8).** It sounds like a friendly invitation, but it is a challenge to battle (compare v11, "faced one another"). Amaziah may want revenge for the mercenaries' raids, or he may simply be flushed with victory. He is challenging a king whose army had just beaten Syria three times (13:25).
- **"A thistle on Lebanon sent to a cedar on Lebanon, saying, 'Give your daughter to my son for a wife'" (v9).** Jehoash answers with a fable, like Jotham's fable of the trees in Judges 9:8–15. The cedars of Lebanon were the grandest trees the Israelites knew. A thistle, or thornbush, proposing a marriage alliance with a cedar is absurd. And a wild animal passing by crushes the thistle without even noticing it. The point is plain: you think you are my equal, and you are not.
- **"You have indeed struck down Edom, and your heart has lifted you up" (v10).** Jehoash diagnoses the problem exactly: pride after success. **"Be content with your glory, and stay at home."** It is good advice, even from an idolatrous king. Proverbs says the same: "Pride goes before destruction" (Prov 16:18).
- **"But Amaziah would not listen" (v11).** Chronicles says, "it was of God, in order that he might give them into the hand of their enemies, because they had sought the gods of Edom" (2 Chr 25:20). He had refused to listen to God's prophet, and now he cannot listen to good sense either.
- **"At Beth-shemesh, which belongs to Judah" (v11).** Beth-shemesh lay in the Sorek valley about 15 miles west of Jerusalem, on Judah's border with the Philistine plain. It is where the ark returned from the Philistines (1 Sam 6:12–15). Jehoash marches down into Judah's own territory to fight. **"Every man fled to his home"** (v12): the army breaks and scatters.
- **"Jehoash king of Israel captured Amaziah king of Judah" (v13).** The writer spells out both men's full family lines, as if to underline the humiliation: the son of Jehu's line captures the son of David's line.
- **"Broke down the wall of Jerusalem for four hundred cubits, from the Ephraim Gate to the Corner Gate" (v13).** Four hundred cubits is about 600 feet, roughly 180 meters. The Ephraim Gate faced north, toward Israel, and the Corner Gate was at the northwest. That is the side from which an attack on Jerusalem was easiest. Jehoash leaves the city defenseless on the side facing his own kingdom.
- **"He seized all the gold and silver… also hostages" (v14).** The temple treasures were stripped again, not long after Joash had paid Hazael with them (12:18). Hostages, probably sons of leading families, were taken to Samaria to guarantee Judah's good behavior. Some scholars think Amaziah himself was held captive in Samaria for a time, perhaps until Jehoash died.
- **"Amaziah… lived fifteen years after the death of Jehoash" (v17).** He outlived his conqueror, but his reign never recovered. Many scholars think his son Azariah ruled alongside him as co-regent during these years.
- **"They made a conspiracy against him in Jerusalem, and he fled to Lachish" (v19).** Chronicles says the conspiracy began "from the time when he turned away from the LORD" (2 Chr 25:27). Lachish was Judah's second city, a heavily fortified town about 30 miles southwest of Jerusalem. He was killed there and brought back "on horses," probably in a chariot or on a horse-drawn bier, and buried with his fathers (v20). Like his father Joash, he was murdered by his own people.
- **"All the people of Judah took Azariah, who was sixteen years old, and made him king" (v21).** Azariah ("the LORD has helped") is also called Uzziah ("the LORD is my strength") in Chronicles and Isaiah. Isaiah's great vision came "in the year that King Uzziah died" (Isa 6:1). "All the people of Judah" means the plot was against Amaziah, not against David's line. The people made sure the throne passed to his son.
- **"He built Elath and restored it to Judah" (v22).** Elath, at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, was Judah's port on the Red Sea, near Solomon's old harbor at Ezion-geber (1 Kgs 9:26). Rebuilding it meant control of the trade routes south to Arabia. **"After the king slept with his fathers"** shows it was done after Amaziah's death, finishing what his victory over Edom had begun.
- **Commentators:** Henry praises the delayed justice of v5: "Justice strikes surely by striking slowly, and is often executed most prudently when it is not executed presently." On the fable he notes that pride "is their own heart that does it," and on the defeat, "Challengers commonly prove to be on the losing side." JFB calls the challenge "bold and haughty," thinks the thistle might stand for Amaziah as "a petty prince," and blames his refusal to listen on "judicial blindness and infatuation." JFB also identifies Sela with Petra and translates Joktheel as "conquered by God." Guzik, citing Poole, praises Amaziah's obedience in sparing the children. He draws on Chronicles for the mercenaries and the gods of Edom, and he warns that a leader's foolish pride harms everyone under him. Guzik passes on Dilday's suggestion that the assassins may have been the very children Amaziah spared, but that is a guess the text does not support.

#### Jeroboam II Reigns in Israel (vv23–29)
- **"In the fifteenth year of Amaziah… Jeroboam the son of Joash… began to reign in Samaria, and he reigned forty-one years" (v23).** About 793–753 BC, counting some years as co-regent with his father. He is called Jeroboam II by modern readers to distinguish him from Jeroboam son of Nebat, the founder of the northern kingdom.
- **"He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD. He did not depart from all the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat" (v24).** The two Jeroboams are linked by name and by sin. The golden calves still stand.
- **"He restored the border of Israel from Lebo-hamath as far as the Sea of the Arabah" (v25).** Lebo-hamath ("the entrance of Hamath") was far to the north in the Beqaa Valley of Lebanon, the traditional northern limit of the promised land (Num 34:8; 1 Kgs 8:65). The Sea of the Arabah is the Dead Sea. These were close to the borders of Solomon's kingdom. Amos, preaching in these very years, mocks the people's pride in exactly these conquests (Amos 6:13–14) and warns that the same land "from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of the Arabah" will be oppressed.
- **"According to the word of the LORD… which he spoke by his servant Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet, who was from Gath-hepher" (v25).** This is the Jonah of the book of Jonah, the only time he is mentioned outside it (apart from the Gospels). Gath-hepher was a village in Galilee, a few miles northeast of Nazareth. That is worth noticing in light of the Pharisees' claim that "no prophet arises from Galilee" (John 7:52). Here Jonah is a prophet of good news for Israel. In his book, God sends him with a warning to Nineveh, Assyria's great city, and Jonah runs because he fears God will show mercy to Israel's enemy too (Jonah 4:2).
- **"For the LORD saw that the affliction of Israel was very bitter, for there was none left, bond or free, and there was none to help Israel" (v26).** "None left, bond or free" is a phrase for total helplessness (Deut 32:36). As in 13:4, God acts because he sees his people's suffering.
- **"But the LORD had not said that he would blot out the name of Israel from under heaven" (v27).** God had threatened Israel's end, but he had not yet pronounced it. The time would come (17:18), but not yet. **"So he saved them by the hand of Jeroboam."** An idolatrous king becomes God's instrument of rescue. God's mercy is not a reward for Jeroboam's goodness. It is the overflow of God's own patience (2 Pet 3:9).
- **"How he restored Damascus and Hamath to Judah in Israel" (v28).** The Hebrew is hard. It may mean he brought Damascus and Hamath, which had once been subject to Solomon's united kingdom (2 Sam 8:5–6; 2 Chr 8:3), back under Israel's control. Translators handle the phrase "to Judah in Israel" in different ways, and some suggest a small copying error. The general sense is clear: Jeroboam extended Israel's power far into Syria.
- **The prosperity behind the scenes.** Archaeology confirms this was a wealthy time. The Samaria ostraca, pottery pieces recording shipments of wine and oil to the capital, come from about this era, as do the carved ivories from Samaria's palaces (compare Amos 3:15; 6:4, "beds of ivory"). But Amos and Hosea show what the wealth hid: the poor sold "for a pair of sandals" (Amos 2:6), corrupt courts and empty religion at Bethel (Amos 5:21–24), and a nation that "did not know that it was I who gave her the grain" (Hos 2:8). Within thirty years of Jeroboam's death, the northern kingdom would be gone.
- **"Jeroboam slept with his fathers… and Zechariah his son reigned in his place" (v29).** Zechariah is the fourth generation of Jehu's house (10:30). He will last six months (15:8–10).
- **Commentators:** Henry warns, "We cannot measure men's characters by the length of their lives or by their outward prosperity." He notes that God's using Jonah here, presumably after his flight, shows grace: "A commission amounts to a pardon," though the order of Jonah's ministry is uncertain. Henry also notes that Hosea and Amos began prophesying in this period. JFB says Jeroboam "raised the kingdom to great political power" while keeping Israel's "favorite religious policy," and credits his success to the covenant, which "forbade as yet the overthrow" of the kingdom. JFB does not comment on Jonah or on Hamath. Guzik stresses that God's help was mercy, not merit, and, citing Patterson and Austel, describes the nation's prosperity as covering a rotten spiritual core.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two kings show two sides of the same truth. Amaziah won a victory God gave, let it make him proud, and lost far more than he had gained. Jeroboam II did nothing to deserve God's help, and God saved Israel through him anyway, because "there was none to help Israel." Both point past themselves. Jesus is the King who did not lift up his heart in pride but "humbled himself" (Phil 2:8), and he is the helper for people who have none (Ps 72:12; Rom 5:6). And Jonah of Gath-hepher, the Galilean prophet who announced Israel's restoration, became the sign Jesus himself pointed to: "as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matt 12:40).
