*Setting:* Moses continues his first sermon on the plains of Moab, weeks before his death. Chapter 2 ended with Israel's victory over Sihon king of the Amorites, the first conquest east of the Jordan. Chapter 3 finishes the history: the defeat of Og king of Bashan in the north, the division of the conquered land among Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh, the charge to Joshua, and finally Moses' personal plea to cross the Jordan, and God's refusal. The account of Og parallels Numbers 21:33–35, and the land grant parallels Numbers 32. The chapter ends with one of the most moving scenes in Moses' life.

#### The Defeat of King Og (vv1–11)
- **"We turned and went up the way to Bashan" (v1).** **Bashan** was the fertile high plateau east and northeast of the Sea of Galilee, today's Golan Heights and southern Syria. It was famous for its oaks and its fat cattle (Isa 2:13; Amos 4:1; Ps 22:12). **Edrei** (modern Daraa in Syria) was one of Og's royal cities.
- **"Do not fear him, for I have given him… into your hand" (v2).** This is almost word for word what God told Moses in Numbers 21:34. Og was a far more frightening enemy than Sihon, the last of the giant race (v11), ruling a land of walled cities. God's first word is "Do not fear."
- **"Sixty cities, the whole region of Argob" (v4).** **Argob** was a district within Bashan, possibly a rugged volcanic area of basalt. **"Fortified with high walls, gates, and bars" (v5).** This is the sort of land that had terrified the spies forty years before (1:28). Now it falls entirely. Archaeologists have found many ancient basalt towns in this region, though dating them to this period is debated.
- **"Devoted them to destruction… every city, men, women, and children" (v6).** The same *cherem* judgment as on Sihon (2:34). It belongs to a unique moment of God's judgment on the Amorites, whose iniquity had become "complete" (Gen 15:16), and is not a pattern for other wars (20:10–15). It remains hard to read, and honest readers feel that.
- **"From the Valley of the Arnon to Mount Hermon" (v8).** The whole land east of the Jordan, from Moab's border in the south to the great mountain in the north, about 150 miles.
- **"The Sidonians call Hermon Sirion, while the Amorites call it Senir" (v9).** Hermon, at about 9,200 feet, is the highest mountain in the region, often snow-capped, visible from much of the land. Each people had its own name for it. "Sirion" also appears in the Ugaritic texts from ancient Syria. Psalm 133:3 sings of "the dew of Hermon." Moses' note shows how well-known the mountain was across cultures.
- **"Only Og the king of Bashan was left of the remnant of the Rephaim" (v11).** Og was the last of the old giant race (2:11, 20).
- **"His bed was a bed of iron… Nine cubits was its length, and four cubits its breadth" (v11).** About 13.5 by 6 feet using a common cubit of 18 inches. Iron was rare and valuable at this time, so an iron bed was a royal treasure. Some think the word for "bed" (*'eres*) here means a coffin or sarcophagus, and that "iron" may describe the dark basalt common in Bashan, which looks like iron. Hundreds of ancient stone burial monuments called dolmens stand in the Golan and northern Jordan to this day. Either way, the point is that Og was enormous and his trophy was famous. It was kept as a curiosity in **Rabbah**, the Ammonite capital (modern Amman), where people could still go and see it. If Israel could defeat a king with a bed like that, God was able to give them anyone.
- **Commentators:** JFB estimates from the bed's size that Og was "eleven or twelve feet" tall, though a bed is usually longer than its sleeper and this is guesswork. JFB also gives the reading "coffin" as possible. It calls Argob a district of Bashan with "lofty" fortified towns and describes the campaign as "a war of extermination." On Hermon, JFB explains the other names as meaning "the upraised, glittering" and "the glittering breastplate of ice," meanings that are popular but uncertain. Guzik stresses that this generation did exactly what their parents had refused to do at Kadesh, facing walled cities and a giant king. Henry's comment on this section is on Og's defeat and the hardening of his heart, not the bed. JFB has no separate note on v10.

#### Division of the Land (vv12–22)
- **"To the Reubenites and the Gadites… from Aroer… and half the hill country of Gilead" (v12).** Reuben and Gad receive the southern part, Sihon's former kingdom, from the Arnon north into Gilead (Num 32).
- **"The rest of Gilead, and all Bashan… I gave to the half-tribe of Manasseh" (v13).** Manasseh's clans take the northern part, Og's kingdom. **"All that portion of Bashan is called the land of Rephaim."** The land of the giants now belongs to Israel.
- **"Jair the Manassite took all the region of Argob… Havvoth-jair, as it is to this day" (v14).** **Havvoth-jair** means "the tent-villages of Jair" (Num 32:41). **The Geshurites and Maacathites** were small kingdoms east and north of the Sea of Galilee that Israel never drove out (Josh 13:13). David later married a Geshurite princess, the mother of Absalom (2 Sam 3:3). "To this day" suggests the name was still in use when this was written down.
- **"To Machir I gave Gilead" (v15).** Machir was Manasseh's son, and his clan settled northern Gilead (Num 32:39–40).
- **"From Chinnereth as far as… the Salt Sea, under the slopes of Pisgah" (v17).** The Jordan valley formed the western boundary, from the Sea of Galilee to the Dead Sea. **Pisgah** is the ridge above the Dead Sea's northern end, and its mention here sets up the end of the chapter.
- **"All your men of valor shall cross over armed before your brothers" (v18).** Moses repeats the agreement of Numbers 32. The eastern tribes may settle, but they must fight for the others until "the LORD gives rest to your brothers, as to you" (v20). **"I know that you have much livestock" (v19)** is a wry aside: Moses knows exactly why they wanted this land. "Rest" is a key word in Deuteronomy and Joshua: peace and security in the land God gives (12:9–10; Josh 21:44).
- **"I commanded Joshua at that time, 'Your eyes have seen'" (v21).** Joshua's faith is built on what he has watched God do. Sihon and Og are proof of what is coming: "So will the LORD do to all the kingdoms." **"It is the LORD your God who fights for you" (v22)** is the refrain from 1:30. Moses had urged the last generation not to fear and they did not listen. Now he says the same to Joshua.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes the eastern tribes kept their promise for years, and he stresses that God fights for those who are on his side, not the other way around. JFB has no notes on vv17–24.

#### Moses Forbidden to Cross the Jordan (vv23–29)
- **"I pleaded with the LORD at that time" (v23).** The verb (*chanan* in a reflexive form) means to beg for grace or favor, to plead for mercy. Moses asks not as a right but as a favor. The Jewish weekly Torah reading that begins here is named from this verb: *Va'etchanan*, "And I pleaded."
- **"O Lord GOD, you have only begun to show your servant your greatness" (v24).** After forty years, the plagues, the sea, Sinai and the victories over Sihon and Og, Moses says God has "only begun." He longs to see more. **"What god is there in heaven or on earth who can do such works?"** He is not admitting that other gods exist. He is saying that nothing anyone calls "god" can compare with the LORD.
- **"Please let me go over and see the good land… that good hill country and Lebanon" (v25).** The man who led the people for forty years asks to set foot in the land at the end of the journey. It is a simple, very human request.
- **"The LORD was angry with me because of you" (v26).** Moses says again what he said in 1:37. Elsewhere Scripture names his own sin: at Meribah he struck the rock in anger and spoke rashly, and God said, "you did not believe in me, to uphold me as holy" (Num 20:12; Ps 106:32–33). Both are true. The people provoked him, and he failed. He is not blaming them so much as admitting how their story and his are bound together.
- **"Enough from you; do not speak to me of this matter again" (v26).** "Enough" is *rav lakh*, literally "much for you." It is a deliberate echo. God used the same phrase in 1:6 and 2:3, "you have stayed long enough," when he ended Israel's waiting. And it is the same phrase Korah's rebels threw at Moses, "You have gone too far!" (Num 16:3), and Moses threw back (Num 16:7). Here it is said to Moses himself, firmly but not harshly. God's answer is no, and the matter is closed.
- **"Go up to the top of Pisgah and lift up your eyes" (v27).** God refuses the request but gives a gift. Moses will not enter, but he will see: west, north, south and east, the whole land laid out below him. Chapter 34 records it happening on Mount Nebo, the highest point of Pisgah, where "the LORD showed him all the land."
- **"But charge Joshua, and encourage and strengthen him" (v28).** Moses' last great task is not to cross but to prepare the man who will. "He shall go over at the head of this people." The leader who cannot finish the work must equip the one who will. The book of Deuteronomy is largely the fulfilment of that charge (31:7–8, 23).
- **"So we remained in the valley opposite Beth-peor" (v29).** **Beth-peor** was the shrine of Baal of Peor, where Israel fell into idolatry and immorality (Num 25). It is also where Moses will be buried (34:6). The chapter ends on a quiet note, and the history section closes in the place of Israel's last great sin, which is where the next chapter's warnings begin (4:3).
- **Commentators:** Henry says "God's answer to this prayer had a mixture of mercy and judgment." On "Let it suffice thee," he applies it to every believer denied something they long for: "Let it suffice thee to have God for thy Father, and heaven for thy portion." And on Pisgah: "Though he should not have the possession of it, he should have the prospect of it." JFB calls Moses' prayer a "very earnest wish," suggesting Moses hoped the threat was conditional. Guzik notes that the verb for "pleaded" is strong, which the lexicon supports. He ties the refusal to Moses striking the rock, which in his reading spoiled a picture of Christ, the rock struck once (1 Cor 10:4). He draws the lesson that God sometimes says no even to his greatest servants, and that leaders are judged more strictly (Jas 3:1). His application is that when God closes a door, we can still strengthen those who will walk through it. JFB has no notes on vv27–29.

#### Pointing to Christ
Moses could see the land but could not lead the people in. The law he gave was holy, but it could not bring Israel into rest. That would take Joshua, whose name, in its Greek form, is Jesus (Heb 4:8). Hebrews makes this point directly: Moses was faithful "as a servant," but Christ is faithful "as a son" over God's house (Heb 3:5–6). There is also a quiet answer to Moses' prayer in the Gospels. Centuries later, Moses did stand in the land, on a mountain, talking with Jesus about the "departure" he would accomplish in Jerusalem (Luke 9:30–31). The one who was told "Enough" at Pisgah finally saw the good land, and saw the one who would bring God's people all the way home.
