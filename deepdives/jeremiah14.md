*Setting:* Judah in a severe drought, sometime in the last decades before the fall of Jerusalem, probably in Jehoiakim's reign (609–598 BC). The Bible does not record this drought elsewhere, but in a land with no great rivers, where most water came from winter rain collected in cisterns, a failed rainy season meant hunger within months. The Law had named drought as one of the covenant curses: "the heavens over your head shall be bronze" (Deut 28:23–24; Lev 26:19). Chapters 14–15 are one long exchange. A lament over the drought is followed by the people's prayer, God's refusal, Jeremiah's protest about the false prophets, and a second prayer for mercy. God's final answer comes in 15:1.

#### Famine, Sword, and Pestilence (vv1–12)
- **"Concerning the drought" (v1).** The Hebrew word is plural, literally "the droughts" or "the withholdings" of rain. It may mean a run of dry years, each one leaving people less able to survive the next.
- **"Judah mourns, and her gates languish" (v2).** The city gate was where people met, traded and held court. Now it is empty and listless. **"Her people lament on the ground"** is literally "they are dark to the ground," dressed in black or with faces darkened, sitting in the dust in mourning.
- **"Her nobles send their servants for water; they come to the cisterns; they find no water" (v3).** Even the rich cannot buy water when there is none. Cisterns were pits cut into rock, plastered to hold the winter rain (2:13). The servants come home with empty jars, and the masters **"cover their heads,"** the gesture of shame and grief David made fleeing Absalom (2 Sam 15:30).
- **"Because of the ground that is dismayed" (v4).** The ground itself is cracked, literally "shattered" or "terrified." The farmers stand in their fields with nothing to plow.
- **"Even the doe in the field forsakes her newborn fawn" (v5).** The doe was known for her care of her young. Hunger drives her to abandon the fawn.
- **"The wild donkeys stand on the bare heights; they pant for air like jackals" (v6).** The wild donkey was the toughest animal of the dry country (Job 39:5–8). Even it stands on the hilltops sniffing the wind for any scent of water, its eyes dull with hunger. The picture moves from city to farmland to wilderness: no part of the land escapes.
- **"Though our iniquities testify against us, act, O LORD, for your name's sake" (v7).** The people pray, or Jeremiah prays on their behalf. The prayer has good words. It admits sin ("our backslidings are many") and appeals not to their merit but to God's name, his reputation and character (Ps 25:11; 79:9). This is the right ground for any prayer for mercy.
- **"O you hope of Israel, its savior in time of trouble" (v8).** "Hope of Israel" is a title for God that Jeremiah uses again in 17:13: "O LORD, the hope of Israel… the fountain of living water." Paul later says he is in chains "because of the hope of Israel" (Acts 28:20).
- **"Why should you be like a stranger in the land, like a traveler who turns aside to tarry for a night?" (v8).** A traveler spends one night at an inn and moves on. He has no stake in the place. The prayer complains that God seems to treat his land that way. **"Like a man confused, like a mighty warrior who cannot save?" (v9).** A warrior caught off guard, stunned and helpless. The people almost accuse God of being unable to act.
- **"Yet you, O LORD, are in the midst of us, and we are called by your name; do not leave us" (v9).** They end with the covenant claims: God's presence among them in the temple, and the fact that they bear his name. These are true. But the temple sermon has already shown that they treat them as a guarantee rather than a calling (7:4–11).
- **"They have loved to wander thus; they have not restrained their feet" (v10).** God's answer goes beneath the words. In 2:25 he had told them, "Keep your feet from going unshod," that is, stop running after other gods. They loved the running. **"Therefore the LORD does not accept them."** The prayer sounded right, but the people had not stopped wandering. The words of v7 were confession without turning.
- **"Do not pray for the welfare of this people" (v11).** This is the third time God tells Jeremiah not to pray for them (7:16; 11:14). **"Though they fast, I will not hear their cry"** (v12). Fasting and sacrifices were meant to accompany a turned heart (Joel 2:12–13; Isa 58:3–7). Without that, they become a way of trying to manage God.
- **"By the sword, by famine, and by pestilence" (v12).** This trio of disasters appears about fifteen times in Jeremiah (21:7, 9; 24:10; 29:17). They came together in a siege: the enemy outside, hunger inside, and disease spreading through a packed, starving city. The drought is a foretaste of worse.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that Judah mourns "not for the sin, but for the trouble—for the withholding of the rain, not for the withdrawing of God's favour," and that the nobles who would not be ashamed of their sins were made ashamed of their empty water jars. JFB points out that "drought" is literally "withholdings" of rain (Deut 11:17) and explains v7's "for thy name's sake" as the only plea left "since our sin precludes trust in ourselves." Guzik reads vv7–9 as a model of what true repentance would have sounded like had the nation meant it, and v10 as God's reply to the real, shallow Judah. JFB adds that God "never refuses to hear real prayer"; the problem was that their hearts were "still idolatrous." On v6 JFB's older note offers a long digression about giant serpents, but most commentators, and the ESV, read the animals as jackals.

#### Lying Prophets (vv13–22)
- **"Ah, Lord GOD, behold, the prophets say to them…" (v13).** Jeremiah pleads for the people by blaming their teachers. They have been told, **"You shall not see the sword, nor shall you have famine, but I will give you assured peace in this place."** "Assured peace" is literally "peace of truth," true and lasting peace. The false prophets spoke in God's name, with God's promises, and applied them to people who had broken his covenant.
- **"I did not send them, nor did I command them or speak to them" (v14).** God disowns them three ways: no commission, no command, no word. Their message is **"a lying vision, worthless divination, and the deceit of their own minds."** "Divination" was forbidden outright (Deut 18:10–12). The source of their "visions" was their own hearts. Chapter 23 returns to this at length (23:16–22).
- **"By sword and famine those prophets shall be consumed" (v15).** The punishment fits the lie exactly. They said, "Sword and famine shall not come," so sword and famine will come to them.
- **"The people to whom they prophesy shall be cast out in the streets" (v16).** The hearers are not let off because they were misled. They wanted to be told peace (5:31: "my people love to have it so"). Paul warned of a time when people "will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" (2 Tim 4:3). **"With none to bury them."** Lying unburied was the ultimate disgrace in Israel (Ps 79:2–3).
- **"Let my eyes run down with tears night and day" (v17).** God gives Jeremiah a lament to speak. **"The virgin daughter of my people is shattered with a great wound."** "Virgin daughter" is a tender name for a city, a young woman never before taken by an enemy (Isa 23:12; Lam 1:15). The Tyndale notes take these tears as God's own grief spoken through the prophet. God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezek 18:23).
- **"If I go out into the field… those pierced by the sword! And if I enter the city… the diseases of famine!" (v18).** Outside, the sword. Inside, starvation. This is the siege of v12 seen up close. **"Both prophet and priest ply their trade through the land and have no knowledge."** "Ply their trade" probably means they go about their business as usual, or wander the land like peddlers, without understanding what has happened. Some take the verb to mean they go off into exile without understanding why.
- **"Have you utterly rejected Judah? Does your soul loathe Zion?" (v19).** Despite the command in v11, Jeremiah prays again. The questions echo the end of Lamentations: "unless you have utterly rejected us" (Lam 5:22). **"We looked for peace, but no good came"** repeats 8:15 word for word. The false prophets promised peace, and the people waited for it.
- **"We acknowledge our wickedness, O LORD, and the iniquity of our fathers" (v20).** This confession goes deeper than v7. It owns both present sin and the sins of past generations (Lev 26:40; Dan 9:8; Neh 9:2).
- **"Do not dishonor your glorious throne" (v21).** The "throne" may mean the temple or Jerusalem itself, called "the throne of the LORD" in 3:17. If God's city falls, what will the nations say about him? **"Remember and do not break your covenant with us."** Moses prayed on the same grounds after the golden calf: God's name and God's promises (Exod 32:11–13; Num 14:13–19).
- **"Are there any among the false gods of the nations that can bring rain?" (v22).** The prayer returns to the drought. Baal was supposed to be the storm god who sent the rain. He cannot. **"Or can the heavens give showers?"** Even the sky does not rain on its own. Only the Creator controls it (10:13; 1 Kgs 18; Job 38:25–28).
- **"We set our hope on you, for you do all these things" (v22).** The prayer ends in trust. This is the right confession. Whether the nation truly meant it, God's answer in 15:1 makes plain: too late for this generation to avoid exile. But the words remain a pattern for every later believer who prays out of a dry season.
- **Commentators:** JFB says Jeremiah "urges that much of the guilt of the people is due to the false prophets' influence," and calls the fate of prophets and hearers "retribution in kind." Guzik notes that God had to tell Jeremiah not to pray because Jeremiah kept wanting to, and compares the "sin that leads to death" in 1 John 5:16, a passage whose meaning is itself debated. Guzik also cites Feinberg's point that the people were judged because they were willing to be deceived. JFB reads "virgin daughter" as a city "heretofore never under foreign yoke." Henry opens the chapter by observing that the prayers in it are still called "the word of the Lord," since "every acceptable prayer is that which God puts into our hearts."

#### Pointing to Christ
The people prayed, "Why should you be like a stranger in the land, like a traveler who stays for a night?" In Jesus God did come and dwell among his people, not as a passing traveler but as one who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and he will never leave his own (Matt 28:20). He was the true prophet, sent and commanded by the Father, who never said "peace" where there was no peace, yet he wept over the city that rejected him (Luke 19:41–42). And to every dry and thirsty place he still calls, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). The hope of Israel, the Savior in time of trouble, has a name.
