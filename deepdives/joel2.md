*Setting:* Chapter 1 described a locust plague and drought that stripped Judah bare and stopped the daily offerings in the temple. Joel called the priests and people to fast and cry out, and he warned that "the day of the LORD is near" (1:15). Chapter 2 turns up the intensity. It opens with an alarm and a terrifying army, then gives one of the Old Testament's great calls to repentance, then turns on the word "then" (v18) to God's answer: pity, restored harvests and finally the promise of his Spirit poured out on all his people. Peter quoted the last part on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2:16–21), and Paul quoted its last verse in Romans 10:13. **A note on numbering:** in Hebrew Bibles, 2:28–32 is counted as chapter 3, and English chapter 3 as chapter 4. The words are the same.

#### The Day of the LORD (vv1–11)
- **"Blow a trumpet in Zion; sound an alarm on my holy mountain!" (v1).** The trumpet is the *shofar*, a ram's horn. A watchman on the wall blew it when an enemy was sighted (Ezek 33:2–6; Hos 5:8), and priests blew trumpets to summon the nation (Num 10:1–10). Here it is God's own alarm. "Let all the inhabitants of the land tremble."
- **"A day of darkness and gloom, a day of clouds and thick darkness!" (v2).** Some people in Israel expected the day of the LORD to be a day of victory for Israel. The prophets reversed that: for an unfaithful people it would be "darkness, and not light" (Amos 5:18–20; Zeph 1:15, which uses almost the same words). The words echo Sinai, where God came down in "thick darkness" (Exod 19:16; Deut 4:11).
- **"Like blackness there is spread upon the mountains a great and powerful people" (v2).** The Hebrew word for "blackness" can also be read as "dawn," which is how the KJV takes it ("as the morning spread upon the mountains"). Either way it is a vast thing moving over the hills. JFB suggests the light reflected off millions of locust wings. "Their like has never been before" repeats 1:2.
- **"The land is like the garden of Eden before them, but behind them a desolate wilderness" (v3).** This is Eden in reverse. The prophets promised that God would one day make the wilderness "like Eden" (Isa 51:3; Ezek 36:35). Here judgment turns Eden into wilderness.
- **Locusts, or a human army? (vv1–11).** Readers have long disagreed, and the Tyndale notes lay out three views. (1) It is the same locust plague as chapter 1, now described as a vast army. Locusts look like tiny horses (v4; in Italian a locust is *cavalletta*, "little horse"), sound like chariots (v5), climb walls and enter windows (vv7–9). (2) It is a second, future locust plague worse than the first. (3) It is a human army, pictured with the imagery of locusts, coming on the day of the LORD. JFB and Guzik both take it as a human army, JFB pointing to the Assyrians as the first fulfillment. The ESV's present-tense verbs fit a vivid prophetic picture of something coming. The plainest reading is that Joel uses the locusts he has seen as a preview of a far greater army God could send. In any case, v11 makes clear whose army it is.
- **"Like war horses they run… with the rumbling of chariots" (vv4–5).** Revelation 9:7–9 borrows these very images for the locusts from the abyss: "like horses prepared for battle… the noise of their wings was like the noise of many chariots." A large swarm really does make a roar.
- **"They march each on his way; they do not swerve from their paths" (vv7–8).** This is the discipline of a perfect army. Proverbs 30:27 noted the same about locusts: "they have no king, yet all of them march in rank." Nothing stops them, not walls, not weapons, not houses. "They enter through the windows like a thief" (v9). Ancient windows were openings with lattices, not glass.
- **"The earth quakes… the sun and the moon are darkened, and the stars withdraw their shining" (v10).** A thick swarm literally darkens the sky, but the language goes beyond that. Shaking earth and darkened heavens are the language of God appearing in judgment (Isa 13:9–13; Exod 19:18). Jesus uses this same language for the end of the age (Mark 13:24–25).
- **"The LORD utters his voice before his army" (v11).** Here is the shock of the passage. This army is God's army, and he leads it. The voice of the LORD is often thunder (Ps 29:3–9). "He who executes his word is powerful." **"For the day of the LORD is great and very awesome; who can endure it?"** The section ends with a question that has only one answer: no one can, unless God shows mercy. Malachi 3:2 asks the same: "who can endure the day of his coming?"
- **Commentators:** JFB reads the army as "not literal, but figurative locusts," with the Assyrian host as the first fulfillment and the final enemies of God's people as the last. Guzik also thinks Joel describes a real human army, and suggests it may never have come because Judah repented, the way Nineveh was spared in Jonah. He admits that identifying it is hard. Henry's concise notes take the description as what "would attend the devastations of locusts." The Tyndale notes present all the views without choosing between them. On v11, JFB mentions that the Arabs called God "Lord of the locusts."

#### Return to the LORD (vv12–17)
- **"Yet even now," declares the LORD (v12).** After "who can endure it?" comes the most hopeful "yet" in the book. The Tyndale notes point out that "declares the LORD," a common formula in the prophets, appears only here in Joel. God speaks directly in the moment of greatest danger: it is not too late.
- **"Return to me with all your heart" (v12).** "Return" (*shuv*) is the Old Testament's word for repentance, turning around and going back. "With all your heart" means the whole inner person: mind, will and affections. Fasting, weeping and mourning are the outward signs that go with it. Joel never lists the people's sins. The call is simply to come back to God.
- **"Rend your hearts and not your garments" (v13).** Tearing one's clothes was the standard outward sign of grief or repentance (Gen 37:34; 2 Kings 19:1). God is not banning the outward sign. He is saying that the sign is worthless without the reality (Ps 51:17; Isa 58:5–7). This is one of the most quoted lines in the prophets, and it is still the heart of Ash Wednesday services in many churches.
- **"For he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love" (v13).** This is a quotation of the name God proclaimed to Moses on Sinai after the golden calf (Exod 34:6–7), the most repeated description of God in the Old Testament (Num 14:18; Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 103:8; 145:8). "Steadfast love" is *hesed*, God's loyal covenant love. Joel adds a phrase that Jonah also uses (Jonah 4:2): "and he relents over disaster." The reason to return is not just fear. It is who God is.
- **"Who knows whether he will not turn and relent?" (v14).** This is humble hope, not presumption. Repentance does not force God's hand, and Joel does not claim to know the outcome. The king of Nineveh used the same words (Jonah 3:9). What God might "leave behind him" is a blessing, specifically "a grain offering and a drink offering." The first thing Joel hopes for is restored worship, the very offerings that were cut off in 1:9, 13.
- **"Gather the children, even nursing infants. Let the bridegroom leave his room" (v16).** Everyone comes, with no exceptions. A newly married man was normally excused from war and public duty for a year (Deut 24:5). Not this time. Even the wedding is interrupted. "His room" and "her chamber" refer to the bridal chamber.
- **"Between the vestibule and the altar let the priests… weep" (v17).** This was the open court between the temple porch and the great bronze altar of sacrifice, the place where priests stood to minister. It was near where Zechariah was later murdered (2 Chr 24:21; Matt 23:35). Ezekiel saw idolaters in this same space with their backs to the temple (Ezek 8:16). Here the priests face it and weep.
- **"Spare your people, O LORD, and make not your heritage a reproach… Why should they say among the peoples, 'Where is their God?'" (v17).** The priests' prayer appeals to two things: that Israel is God's "heritage," his own possession, and that God's own reputation among the nations is at stake (compare Moses in Exod 32:11–12; Ps 79:10; 115:2). It asks for mercy on God's own terms, for the sake of his own name.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "rending the garments would be vain, except their hearts were rent by abasement and self-abhorrence." JFB puts it plainly: "Let there be the inward sorrow of heart, and not the mere outward manifestation of it." Guzik stresses that God's kindness, not fear alone, is what moves people to return. He passes on a story from Spurgeon about a woman who freely called herself a sinner but could not name one commandment she had broken, a confession with no real content. He also notes that the priests must repent first, alongside the people.

#### The LORD Had Pity (vv18–27)
- **"Then the LORD became jealous for his land and had pity on his people" (v18).** This is the turning point of the whole book. The verbs can be read as past ("then the LORD became jealous"), which suggests the people did repent and God responded, or as future ("will be jealous"), as some older translations have it. The ESV reads them as past. "Jealous" here is God's zeal for what belongs to him, like a husband's protective love for his wife. It is not petty envy.
- **"I am sending to you grain, wine, and oil" (v19).** Each loss in chapter 1 is reversed: grain, wine and oil (1:10), no more reproach (2:17).
- **"I will remove the northerner far from you" (v20).** Locusts normally came into Israel from the south or east, so "the northerner" may point beyond locusts to the human enemies who always came down from the north: Assyria and Babylon (Jer 1:14–15; Ezek 38:6, 15). JFB takes it this way. If it means locusts, the picture matches what happens to swarms: they are blown into the desert and into the seas, "the eastern sea" (the Dead Sea) and "the western sea" (the Mediterranean), and their rotting bodies stink. Observers have recorded exactly this. **"For he has done great things"** is ironic: the invader exalted itself.
- **"Fear not, O land… Fear not, you beasts of the field… Be glad, O children of Zion" (vv21–23).** Three "fear nots" mirror the three groups that suffered in chapter 1: the ground that mourned (1:10), the animals that groaned (1:18, 20) and the people whose joy dried up (1:12). Now "the LORD has done great things" (v21), the same words used of the invader in v20. God's great deeds outdo the enemy's.
- **"He has given the early rain for your vindication" (v23).** Israel's farming depended on two rainy seasons: the "early" rain in autumn (October–November), which softened the ground for plowing and sowing, and the "latter" rain in spring (March–April), which filled out the crops before harvest (Deut 11:14). The Hebrew word for "early rain" here, *moreh*, can also mean "teacher," so this phrase could be read "the teacher for righteousness." The community of the Dead Sea Scrolls called its leader "the Teacher of Righteousness," a title that may draw on this verse and Hosea 10:12. JFB says that rendering "is wrong," and most translations agree that rain fits the context. "For your vindication" (literally "for righteousness") means rain given in the right measure, as a sign that the relationship with God is restored.
- **"I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten" (v25).** This much-loved promise uses the four locust names from 1:4 in a different order. "Restore" (*shillam*) means to repay or make good. The years themselves could not be lived again, but God would repay what they had cost. Spurgeon, as Guzik notes, said that the locusts ate not the years but the fruit of the years, and God can give back the harvest. **"My great army, which I sent among you"** confirms that the plague was God's discipline, not chance.
- **"You shall eat in plenty and be satisfied, and praise the name of the LORD your God" (v26).** The goal of the restoration is not just full stomachs but worship. Deuteronomy 8:10 says the same: "you shall eat and be full, and you shall bless the LORD your God."
- **"You shall know that I am in the midst of Israel… and there is none else" (v27).** The deepest gift is God's presence. "Know" in the Bible means more than learning a fact: it means experiencing it. "My people shall never again be put to shame" is repeated twice (vv26–27), the double ending of the restoration section.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "God is ready to succour his people; and he waits to be gracious," and that "His promises are real answers to the prayers of faith." JFB takes "the north" as Assyria or Babylon, "the type and forerunner of all Israel's foes," and notes that the locust names in v25 come in reverse order from 1:4. Guzik explains the two rainy seasons and passes on Spurgeon's point about the fruit of the years. He also warns against the "Latter Rain" movement of the mid-1900s, which took its name from v23 and taught a special end-time outpouring for an elite group, and he regards several of its doctrines as unbiblical.

#### The LORD Will Pour Out His Spirit (vv28–32)
- **"And it shall come to pass afterward" (v28).** After the material restoration comes something greater. When Peter quoted this verse at Pentecost, he rendered "afterward" as "in the last days" (Acts 2:17). The New Testament treats the time from Jesus' resurrection to his return as "the last days" (Heb 1:2; 1 John 2:18).
- **"I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (v28).** "Pour out" (*shaphak*) pictures water poured lavishly, not sprinkled. In the Old Testament the Spirit came on chosen individuals for particular tasks: judges (Judg 3:10; 15:14), kings (1 Sam 10:10; 16:13), prophets and the occasional priest (2 Chr 24:20). Moses had longed for something more: "Would that all the LORD's people were prophets, that the LORD would put his Spirit on them!" (Num 11:29). Joel promises exactly that. "All flesh" in context means all of God's people, regardless of age, sex or class. At Pentecost Peter extends it to "all who are far off, everyone whom the Lord our God calls to himself" (Acts 2:39), and the rest of Acts shows Gentiles receiving the same Spirit (Acts 10:44–47).
- **"Your sons and your daughters… your old men… your young men… even on the male and female servants" (vv28–29).** The list deliberately breaks down every barrier of the ancient world: men and women, old and young, free and slave. Paul would later write, "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28). Prophecy, dreams and visions were the ways God spoke to prophets (Num 12:6). Now God's whole people would know him directly (Jer 31:33–34).
- **"Wonders in the heavens and on the earth, blood and fire and columns of smoke" (v30).** The language recalls the exodus: blood (the Nile turned to blood), fire and smoke (the pillar of fire and cloud, and Sinai on fire, Exod 19:18). "The sun shall be turned to darkness, and the moon to blood" (v31) is the language of cosmic upheaval used for God's judgment (Isa 13:10; Rev 6:12). A total lunar eclipse turns the moon a deep red.
- **How Pentecost fulfills Joel.** Peter said plainly, "this is what was uttered through the prophet Joel" (Acts 2:16). The Spirit was poured out, and men and women from many nations spoke God's praises. But the sun did not literally turn dark that morning. Christians read this in a few ways. Many see Pentecost as the beginning of the fulfillment, with the cosmic signs still ahead, before Christ's return (Mark 13:24–26). Others note that the sky did go dark at noon when Jesus died, seven weeks earlier (Luke 23:44–45), and read the signs as fulfilled in the cross and in the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. Most agree that Pentecost opened "the last days," and that the "great and awesome day of the LORD" still lies ahead.
- **"Everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved" (v32).** "Calling on the name of the LORD" is the Bible's phrase for worshiping and trusting God, from the earliest generations (Gen 4:26; 12:8). Peter ended his quotation here and told the crowd how to call: "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 2:38). Paul quotes this verse to show that salvation is for Jew and Gentile alike: "the same Lord is Lord of all… For 'everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved'" (Rom 10:12–13). In Joel, "the LORD" is Yahweh. In Romans the context shows that the "Lord" called on is Jesus (Rom 10:9), one of the New Testament's clearest ways of saying that Jesus is the LORD.
- **"In Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be those who escape, as the LORD has said" (v32).** "As the LORD has said" may point to Obadiah 17, which says almost the same, or to an earlier word of God. **"Among the survivors shall be those whom the LORD calls."** There is a beautiful balance in this verse. People call on the LORD, and the LORD calls people. Human calling and divine calling meet. Peter's sermon ends with the same balance: the promise is for "everyone whom the Lord our God calls to himself" (Acts 2:39).
- **Commentators:** JFB says v28 was "Fulfilled in earnest (Ac 2:17) on Pentecost," with more to follow, and reads the signs of v30 as chiefly symbols of political upheaval, with the fall of Jerusalem as "a type and earnest" of the final judgment. It explains "call" in v32 as a "metaphor from an invitation to a feast" and says the remnant is saved "according to the election of grace." Guzik treats Pentecost as a real but not yet complete fulfillment and notes that Peter's call to repent mirrors Joel's. Henry writes that "the Holy Spirit was poured out" at Pentecost, and that "Calling on God supposes knowledge of him, faith in him, desire toward him, dependence on him." The Tyndale notes connect the promise to Moses' wish in Numbers 11:29.

#### Pointing to Christ
Joel 2 moves from darkness to mercy to Spirit. The day of the LORD that no one can endure is met by the God who is "gracious and merciful," and by a call to return. The New Testament shows how that mercy reached us. Jesus endured the darkness of the day of the LORD on the cross, when the sun failed at noon (Luke 23:44–45). He rose, ascended and, as Peter said, "having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this that you yourselves are seeing and hearing" (Acts 2:33). The one who pours out the Spirit is Jesus. And the name on which "everyone who calls" is saved is his name (Acts 4:12; Rom 10:9–13).
