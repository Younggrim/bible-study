*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably around 460–430 BC, close to the time of Ezra and Nehemiah. The exiles had been home from Babylon for nearly a century. The temple was rebuilt and sacrifices were offered again (1:7–10), and a Persian governor ruled the province (1:8). But the great hopes raised by Haggai and Zechariah had not come true in the way the people expected. There was no king, no glory filling the temple and no obvious blessing. Many had grown cynical. The sins Malachi condemns are the same ones Nehemiah found when he came back to Jerusalem (Neh 13): careless priests, unpaid tithes, marriages to women who worshiped other gods and a cooling toward the covenant. Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament in the Christian order, and his voice is followed by about four hundred years of prophetic silence until John the Baptist. The book is built as six disputes. In each one God makes a statement, the people push back with a question ("How have you loved us?"), and God answers. Chapter 1 contains the first dispute, about God's love, and most of the second, about worthless sacrifices.

#### The Oracle of the Word of the LORD (v1)
- **"The oracle" (v1).** The Hebrew word *massa'* can mean "burden," the load a donkey carries (Exod 23:5; Neh 13:15), and it is also the standard heading for a prophetic message (Isa 13:1; Nah 1:1; Hab 1:1). The older translations render it "burden." The lexicon lists the two senses separately, so it may not be a pun at all. But many have noted that a message from God weighs heavy on those who receive it.
- **"To Israel" (v1).** Malachi speaks to the people of Judah, the returned exiles. Calling them "Israel" claims that this small group is still God's covenant people, the heirs of all twelve tribes.
- **"By Malachi" (v1).** The name means "my messenger." It is the same word God uses in 3:1: "Behold, I send my messenger." Because of this, some have wondered whether Malachi was a title rather than a personal name. The Greek Old Testament translates the verse "by the hand of his messenger," and one Jewish tradition identified the prophet with Ezra. Most scholars accept it as his real name, since every other prophetic book names its prophet. Nothing else is known about him.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the message came "by the hand of Malachi," as if delivered in writing, which gave it greater certainty. He reads "burden" as a word of weight, heavy to those who reject it. JFB glosses "burden" as "heavy sentence." Guzik dates Malachi to about a century after the return, around the time of Nehemiah or just after his book closes, since the temple stands, a governor rules and the sins match those Nehemiah confronted.

#### The Lord's Love for Israel (vv2–5)
- **"I have loved you," says the LORD (v2).** The book begins not with a rebuke but with a declaration of love. Everything that follows, every charge and warning, rests on this. The Hebrew verb is in a form that can cover past love continuing into the present: "I have loved you, and I still do."
- **"But you say, 'How have you loved us?'" (v2).** This is the first of the book's pushback questions (compare 1:6, 7; 2:14, 17; 3:7, 8, 13). The people do not deny God's love outright. They ask for proof. After years of poverty, foreign rule and unfulfilled hopes, God's love no longer felt real.
- **"Is not Esau Jacob's brother?… Yet I have loved Jacob but Esau I have hated" (vv2–3).** God answers by pointing to history. Jacob and Esau were twins (Gen 25:21–26). Esau was the firstborn and had every natural claim to the birthright, yet God chose the younger (Gen 25:23). Jacob's descendants became Israel, and Esau's became Edom, the nation south and east of the Dead Sea. Paul quotes these verses in Romans 9:13 to show that God's choice rests on his own free mercy, made "though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad" (Rom 9:11).
- **What does "hated" mean?** "Love" and "hate" here are covenant words. In the language of treaties and family law, to "love" means to choose and commit, and to "hate" means not to choose, to set aside (compare Gen 29:30–31, where Leah is "hated" in the sense of loved less; Deut 21:15–17). Jesus uses the same idiom: anyone who does not "hate" father and mother cannot be his disciple (Luke 14:26; compare Matt 10:37, "loves… more than me"). The point is not that God felt personal malice toward Esau. Esau was blessed with land and wealth (Gen 33:9; 36). The point is that the covenant promises came through Jacob, not Esau. Christians have debated how far this passage speaks of individuals and eternal destiny and how far of nations and their roles in history. In Malachi it is clearly speaking of two nations.
- **"I have laid waste his hill country and left his heritage to jackals of the desert" (v3).** Edom's land was rugged hill country around Petra and Bozrah. During the sixth and fifth centuries BC Edom was overrun, perhaps by Babylon under Nabonidus, and then by Arab tribes. By about 300 BC the Nabataeans held the region. Many Edomites moved west into the southern part of Judah, an area later called Idumea. Herod the Great was an Idumean.
- **Edom's history with Israel.** The brothers' rivalry continued between their nations. Edom refused Israel passage in the wilderness (Num 20:14–21), and when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem, Edom cheered and joined in (Ps 137:7; Obad 10–14; Ezek 35:5). Obadiah's whole book is a sentence of judgment on Edom.
- **"They may build, but I will tear down" (v4).** Edom vowed to rebuild, but God says its ruins will stay ruins. Judah had also been laid waste, but God rebuilt it. That difference was the proof of love the people had asked for. Edom would be called "the wicked country," literally "the territory of wickedness."
- **"Great is the LORD beyond the border of Israel!" (v5).** When they see Edom's fate, they will recognize that the LORD rules not only Israel but the nations around it. The book's horizon reaches beyond Israel from the start (compare 1:11, 14).
- **Commentators:** Henry says God first assures Israel of his love before he rebukes them, and that Edom's cities "are laid waste, and never rebuilt," while Jacob's are rebuilt. He writes: "Esau was justly hated, but Jacob freely loved." JFB says the people's question shows "insensibility to God's love," and that "God's love is often least acknowledged where it is most manifested." He reads "hated" as "not positively, but relatively," citing Luke 14:26. Guzik notes the book is built around a series of such doubting questions. He agrees that "hate" means passed over for the covenant blessing, not cursed, and he passes on Spurgeon's remark that his real wonder was not that God hated Esau but that God could love Jacob. He adds that the passage contrasts two nations, with Edom judged for its own sins.

#### The Priests' Polluted Offerings (vv6–14)
- **"A son honors his father, and a servant his master" (v6).** The second dispute begins. Everyone in Israel's world knew that sons owe fathers honor and servants owe masters respect (Exod 20:12). God is Israel's father (Exod 4:22; Isa 63:16; 64:8) and its master. "Where is my honor?… where is my fear?"
- **"O priests, who despise my name" (v6).** The priests, who should have led the people in worship, are the worst offenders. "Despise" is the word used of Esau, who "despised his birthright" (Gen 25:34). The priests are treating their sacred calling the way Esau treated his.
- **"How have we despised your name?" (v6).** The priests do not see the problem. They are going through the motions and are unaware that the motions have become an insult.
- **"By offering polluted food upon my altar" (v7).** "Food" is literally "bread," a general word for the offerings, which were called God's "food" (Lev 3:11; 21:6). "The LORD's table" is the altar (Ezek 41:22; 44:16). To treat the table as something that "may be despised" was to despise the one whose table it was.
- **"When you offer blind animals in sacrifice, is that not evil?" (v8).** The Law said plainly that blind, lame, sick or blemished animals could not be offered (Lev 22:17–25; Deut 15:21). The priests were accepting the animals the farmers did not want, the ones that were worth nothing at market.
- **"Present that to your governor; will he accept you?" (v8).** The "governor" (*pechah*) was the Persian-appointed official over Judah (Hag 1:1; Neh 5:14). No one would dare bring a sick sheep as a gift to him. They gave God what they would be ashamed to give a man. Nehemiah, as governor, refused even the food allowance owed him (Neh 5:14–18), which makes the contrast sharper.
- **"Entreat the favor of God, that he may be gracious to us" (v9).** This is sharp irony. Let the priests pray all they like. With offerings like these, why would God answer?
- **"Oh that there were one among you who would shut the doors" (v10).** God would rather the temple be closed than see this worship continue. The doors may be the temple doors or the gates of the inner court. "That you might not kindle fire on my altar in vain": an empty ritual is worse than none. Isaiah had said the same: "Bring no more vain offerings" (Isa 1:13).
- **"I have no pleasure in you… and I will not accept an offering from your hand" (v10).** "Offering" is *minchah*, a word used for gifts or tribute and, more narrowly, for the grain offering. God refuses the gift because of the giver.
- **"From the rising of the sun to its setting my name will be great among the nations" (v11).** This is one of the most striking verses in the book. The Hebrew has no verb, so it can be read "is great" or "will be great." The ESV takes it as future. Most Christian interpreters see a prophecy of the gospel going to the Gentiles. Very early, the church read "in every place… a pure offering" as fulfilled in Christian worship. The *Didache*, a church manual from around AD 100, applies it to the Lord's Supper, and JFB allows that reading as possible. Some read it as present, referring to Jews scattered across the Persian Empire who still prayed toward Jerusalem. A few take it to mean God accepts sincere pagan worship, but that does not fit Malachi's insistence on God's name and covenant. Either way, the contrast is pointed: the nations will honor the name that Israel's own priests despise.
- **"Incense… and a pure offering" (v11).** Incense pictured prayer rising to God (Ps 141:2; Rev 5:8; 8:3–4). "Pure offering" is *minchah tehorah*, the clean gift that the priests in Jerusalem were failing to bring.
- **"But you profane it" (v12).** To profane is to treat something holy as common. The priests' attitude says the altar and its food are worth nothing.
- **"'What a weariness this is,' and you snort at it" (v13).** The priests sighed over their duties and sniffed at them in contempt. Jewish scribal tradition lists this verse among a small number of places where early copyists are said to have softened a phrase out of reverence. The original may have read "you snort at *me*," and it was changed to "at it." Either way, the contempt is directed at God.
- **"What has been taken by violence or is lame or sick" (v13).** "Taken by violence" may mean stolen, or an animal mauled by a wild beast. The Law forbade eating such an animal (Exod 22:31), let alone offering it to God.
- **"Cursed be the cheat who has a male in his flock, and vows it, and yet sacrifices… what is blemished" (v14).** The worst is the person who promises God a healthy male, the best animal, and then substitutes a defective one. He has the good one and keeps it for himself. The same sin lay behind Ananias and Sapphira's deception (Acts 5:1–11).
- **"For I am a great King, says the LORD of hosts" (v14).** "Great King" was the title of the emperors of Assyria and Persia, the "king of kings" (Ezra 7:12). God claims the title for himself (Ps 47:2; 95:3). If the people would honor a Persian governor, how much more the King of the universe? "My name will be feared among the nations" repeats the theme of v11.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes, "It is a very evil thing, whether men think so or no, to offer the blind and the lame," and notes that worshipers would "scorn to offer to their governor" what they offered God. On v10 he says that those "who are mercenary, and would never do the work but for the wages" are not acceptable servants. On v13: "their own wicked hearts made it a weariness." On v14: "Those who think to put a cheat upon God will prove, in the end, to have put a damning cheat upon their own souls." JFB says that "a cheap religion, costing little, is rejected by God" and "better no sacrifices than vain ones." He reads v11 as a sign of the church's universal reach, with incense as prayer. He also says greed, not poverty, explains the poor offerings. Guzik notes that worship can grow cheap gradually without the worshiper noticing, and contrasts David's refusal to offer God what cost him nothing (2 Sam 24:24). He passes on Joyce Baldwin's view that v11 does not mean the nations already worship God under other names, but that they will come to know the God of Scripture.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter opens with God's free love, choosing Jacob before he had done anything, and closes with priests offering God what cost them nothing. Both sides point to Christ. God's electing love reaches its height in the Father's gift of his Son, "not that we have loved God but that he loved us" (1 John 4:10). In place of blind and lame animals, Christ offered himself "without blemish" to God (Heb 9:14; 1 Pet 1:19), the perfect sacrifice no priest of Malachi's day could bring. And because of that sacrifice, the promise of v11 has come true: across the nations, "from the rising of the sun to its setting," people now call on the name of the LORD through Jesus (Rom 10:12–13; Rev 7:9–10).
