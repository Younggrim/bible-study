*Setting:* About AD 30, in the days just before Passover. Jesus has come down the Jordan valley with crowds of pilgrims and has just healed a blind beggar outside Jericho (18:35–43). Chapter 19 brings the long journey that began in 9:51 to its end. In Jericho he finds one more lost man, a rich tax collector. On the road up he tells a parable about a king who goes away and comes back. Then he rides down the Mount of Olives to the cheers of his disciples, weeps over the city that will reject him, and enters the temple to clear it. From here until his arrest, he teaches in the temple every day.

#### Jesus and Zacchaeus (vv1–10)
- **"He entered Jericho and was passing through" (v1).** Jericho was a rich oasis town of palm and balsam groves, on a main trade route across the Jordan. Herod the Great had built a winter palace there, and his son Archelaus rebuilt it. Customs on goods crossing from Perea would have been collected here, which made it a good post for a tax official.
- **"A man named Zacchaeus... a chief tax collector and was rich" (v2).** His name comes from a Hebrew word meaning "pure" or "innocent," an irony everyone in town would have enjoyed. "Chief tax collector" (*architelōnēs*, used only here in the New Testament) means he oversaw other collectors, taking a cut from their takings as well as his own. Luke has just said how hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom (18:24–27). Here is a rich man, and a hated one, about to enter.
- **"He was seeking to see who Jesus was" (v3).** His motive may have begun as curiosity. But "seeking" is a word that matters in this story, as v10 will show.
- **"Small in stature... climbed up into a sycamore tree" (vv3–4).** The crowd would not let a hated tax collector through, and he could not see over them. The sycamore here is the sycamore-fig, a broad tree with low, spreading branches that is easy to climb. A wealthy official running ahead and climbing a tree in his robes was undignified, and he did it anyway.
- **"Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for I must stay at your house today" (v5).** Jesus stops under the tree, looks up and calls him by name. No one introduced them. "Must" (*dei*) is Luke's word for God's plan. This meeting was not an accident. And Jesus invites himself, which a guest normally would not do. Of all the homes in Jericho, he chooses the one no respectable person would enter.
- **"He hurried and came down and received him joyfully" (v6).** He obeys the words exactly: "hurry... come down." "Joyfully" links him to the joy of chapter 15 over the lost being found.
- **"They all grumbled" (v7).** The same word as 15:2. This time it is "all," not only the Pharisees. "To be the guest" means to lodge, to stay the night, which was even closer fellowship than eating together. Jesus has just healed a blind beggar to the crowd's praise (18:43), and now the same crowd is offended.
- **"Zacchaeus stood and said to the Lord" (v8).** He stands, probably at the meal, to make a public statement. Luke calls Jesus "the Lord" in the narration, and Zacchaeus does too.
- **"The half of my goods I give to the poor" (v8).** The verbs are present tense, "I give... I restore." Most read this as a resolution made on the spot. A few read it as his existing habit, which he is defending against the crowd's charge. The first fits the story better: something has changed in him, and the change shows up in his money.
- **"If I have defrauded anyone of anything, I restore it fourfold" (v8).** "If" here means "since" or "whatever." He admits he has. "Defrauded" is the word John the Baptist used when he told tax collectors to collect no more than their due (3:13–14). The law required a cheat who confessed to repay what he took plus a fifth (Lev 6:5; Num 5:7). Fourfold was the penalty for stealing a sheep (Exod 22:1), and David said the man in Nathan's story must pay fourfold (2 Sam 12:6). Zacchaeus treats his own gains as outright theft and goes far beyond what the law demands.
- **The rich ruler and Zacchaeus.** The ruler asked what he must do, kept all his money and went away sad (18:18–23). Zacchaeus asks nothing, gives away half and more, and is full of joy. Jesus did not even tell him to give. "What is impossible with man is possible with God" (18:27) has just happened in front of the crowd.
- **"Today salvation has come to this house" (v9).** "Today" recalls the angels at his birth (2:11), his first sermon at Nazareth (4:21) and the cross (23:43). Salvation has come because the Savior has come into the house. "This house" includes his household.
- **"Since he also is a son of Abraham" (v9).** The crowd treated him as if he had forfeited his place in Israel. Jesus says he is a true son of Abraham, by descent and now by faith (Gal 3:7). Earlier Jesus called a crippled woman a "daughter of Abraham" (13:16).
- **"For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost" (v10).** Many see this as the key verse of Luke's whole Gospel. It echoes God's promise as shepherd: "I will seek the lost" (Ezek 34:16). Zacchaeus was seeking to see Jesus (v3), but the deeper truth is that Jesus was seeking him.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "He that has a mind to know Christ, shall be known of him," and Zacchaeus "does not look to be justified by his works, as the Pharisee," but shows the sincerity of his faith by them. JFB thinks curiosity was his "only motive" at first, overruled "for more than he sought," and says Jesus invites himself in "royal" style, the way a king announces he will stay at a subject's house. JFB says fourfold matched the Roman law, while the Jewish law required the principal plus a fifth. Guzik also contrasts Zacchaeus with the rich ruler and notes that love, not guilt, is what made him so generous.

#### The Parable of the Ten Minas (vv11–27)
- **"Because they supposed that the kingdom of God was to appear immediately" (v11).** Luke again states the reason before the story. Jesus is within a day's walk of Jerusalem, crowds are calling him Son of David (18:38), and salvation has just come to Jericho. Many expected him to enter the city and set up God's kingdom on the spot (compare Acts 1:6).
- **"A nobleman went into a far country to receive for himself a kingdom" (v12).** Everyone listening would think of Herod's family. Herod the Great went to Rome in 40 BC to be made king of Judea. When he died in 4 BC, his son Archelaus went to Rome to have his kingship confirmed by Augustus. Archelaus's palace was right there in Jericho.
- **"His citizens hated him and sent a delegation after him" (v14).** This happened too. Josephus records that a delegation of Jews went to Rome to plead against Archelaus, saying they did not want him as king. Augustus gave him only part of the kingdom and a lesser title. Archelaus had already put down protests with great bloodshed, and his rule was so harsh that Rome removed him in AD 6. Jesus borrows this well-known story to describe himself: he too will go away to receive a kingdom and come back.
- **"Ten minas... 'Engage in business until I come'" (v13).** A mina was worth 100 drachmas, about four months' wages for a laborer. It was a modest sum for a king to hand out, which is the point. It is a test with "a very little" (v17). Each servant receives the same amount. "Engage in business" means to trade, to put the money to work.
- **A different parable from the talents.** Matthew's parable of the talents (Matt 25:14–30) is similar but distinct. There the master gives different amounts to three servants (five, two and one talents, each talent worth about sixty minas), and it is told on the Mount of Olives a few days later. Here every servant gets the same, and the parable adds the rebellious citizens. Jesus likely used the same theme more than once.
- **"Your mina has made ten minas more" (v16).** The servant says "your mina," not "I." The gain belongs to the master's money.
- **"Well done, good servant!... authority over ten cities" (v17).** The reward for faithfulness with a little is responsibility over much (16:10). Faithful service now prepares for greater service in the kingdom to come.
- **"Your mina has made five minas" (vv18–19).** Less, but still faithful, and still rewarded in proportion.
- **"Here is your mina, which I kept laid away in a handkerchief" (v20).** The word is the cloth used to wipe the face or to wrap around the head or neck. Rabbinic law said that a person who buried entrusted money was not liable if it was stolen, but one who wrapped it in a cloth was responsible, since that was not a safe way to keep it. This servant took the least safe option and did nothing with it.
- **"I was afraid of you, because you are a severe man" (v21).** "Severe" (*austēros*, from which English gets "austere") means harsh or strict. He blames his master's character for his own failure.
- **"I will condemn you with your own words" (v22).** The master does not agree that he is severe. He says that even if the servant believed it, he should at least have put the money with the bankers. "Bank" is literally "table," the moneychanger's counter. The problem was not fear but a lack of trust and love.
- **"Take the mina from him" (vv24–26).** The bystanders protest that the first servant already has ten. The principle (8:18): whoever uses what God gives will receive more, and whoever does not will lose even what he seemed to have.
- **"As for these enemies of mine... slaughter them before me" (v27).** The story ends with the citizens who rejected the king. Ancient kings returning to power often executed rebels (1 Kgs 2:13–46). This is a sobering picture of final judgment on those who refuse Christ's reign, and many also see a warning of Jerusalem's fall in AD 70, which Jesus foretells in vv43–44. The parable has two audiences: servants who must be faithful while the king is away, and citizens who must decide whether to have him as king at all.
- **Commentators:** JFB lists four differences between this parable and the talents. Here all receive the same pound, so the lesson is "different degrees of improvement of the same opportunities." Henry says the one pound given to each "seems to point out the gift of the gospel, which is the same to all who hear it," while the talents represent different gifts and abilities. JFB says "a far country" was "said to put down the notion that He was just on His way to set up His kingdom." Guzik makes the delay of the kingdom the main point and says the reward for faithful service is more service.

#### The Triumphal Entry (vv28–40)
- **"He went on ahead, going up to Jerusalem" (v28).** He leads the way, as in Mark 10:32. The road from Jericho climbs steeply through bare wilderness, some 3,000 feet in about 18 miles, the road of the Good Samaritan story (10:30).
- **"Bethphage and Bethany, at the mount that is called Olivet" (v29).** Bethany, about two miles east of Jerusalem, was the home of Mary, Martha and Lazarus (John 11:1, 18). Bethphage, "house of unripe figs," was a village close by, nearer the city. The Mount of Olives is the ridge east of Jerusalem. Zechariah foretold that the LORD would stand on it on the day he comes as king (Zech 14:4).
- **"A colt tied, on which no one has ever yet sat" (v30).** Matthew quotes Zechariah 9:9: "Behold, your king is coming to you... humble, and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey." An animal never used before was fit for a sacred purpose (Num 19:2; 1 Sam 6:7). A king riding to war came on a horse. A king coming in peace came on a donkey, as David's son Solomon rode David's mule to his coronation (1 Kgs 1:33–40).
- **"The Lord has need of it" (vv31–34).** Whether Jesus had arranged it in advance or knew it by divine insight, Luke shows that everything happened "just as he had told them." Kings had the right to call on their subjects' animals. Here the true King asks, and the owners let it go.
- **"They spread their cloaks on the road" (v36).** This is how Israel greeted Jehu when he was proclaimed king (2 Kgs 9:13). Luke does not mention the palm branches the other Gospels record, or the word "Hosanna," perhaps because his Gentile readers would not know it.
- **"Already on the way down the Mount of Olives" (v37).** At the top of the ridge the whole city comes into view, with the temple gleaming white and gold across the Kidron Valley. "The whole multitude of his disciples" means his followers, not the whole city, praising God "for all the mighty works that they had seen."
- **"Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord!" (v38).** The words come from Psalm 118:26, one of the psalms pilgrims sang going up to the feasts. Only Luke adds "the King." "Peace in heaven and glory in the highest!" echoes the angels at Bethlehem (2:14). There it was "peace on earth." Here it is "peace in heaven." As the next scene shows, earth, and Jerusalem in particular, is about to reject the peace he brings.
- **"Teacher, rebuke your disciples" (v39).** The Pharisees hear the crowd calling Jesus king and want him to stop it. It could be dangerous with Roman troops in the city for Passover, and it is blasphemy if he is not who they say.
- **"If these were silent, the very stones would cry out" (v40).** Jesus accepts the praise. Creation itself would praise its king if people refused. Habakkuk pictured stones crying out against injustice (Hab 2:11), and the psalms call all creation to praise (Ps 96:11–13; 98:7–9). There may also be a hint ahead, since in a few verses Jesus says Jerusalem's stones will be torn down (v44).
- **Commentators:** JFB says Jesus had always discouraged public demonstrations until now, but "on this one occasion" he accepts the acclaim as fitting for the King entering his city, so much so that it "would have been wrung out of the stones rather than be withheld." JFB suggests the colt's owner was perhaps a disciple. Henry: "Christ's triumphs, and his disciples' joyful praises, vex proud Pharisees." Guzik stresses that a donkey, not a war horse, signals a king of peace, and that praise should be specific, for what God has actually done.

#### Jesus Weeps over Jerusalem (vv41–44)
- **"When he drew near and saw the city, he wept over it" (v41).** The verb means to weep aloud, to sob. It is a different word from the quiet tears at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35). Moments after the crowds cheer him as king, Jesus is in tears, not for himself, but for the city that will kill him.
- **"Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace!" (v42).** "Jerusalem" was understood to contain the Hebrew word for peace, *shalom* (Ps 122:6–8). The city of peace does not recognize the King of peace. "On this day" means the day of his arrival.
- **"But now they are hidden from your eyes" (v42).** The same language used of the disciples not understanding his death (18:34). Here it carries the weight of judgment. Persistent refusal brings blindness.
- **"Your enemies will set up a barricade around you" (v43).** The word *charax* means a stake or a palisade of stakes. In AD 70 the Roman general Titus did exactly this. Josephus describes a siege wall about four and a half miles long built around the city, cutting off food and escape. Famine followed, then the city was stormed.
- **"Tear you down to the ground, you and your children within you" (v44).** The language echoes Old Testament judgments (Ps 137:9; Hos 10:14). Josephus says the Romans demolished the city and the temple so thoroughly that visitors could hardly believe it had been inhabited.
- **"Because you did not know the time of your visitation" (v44).** A "visitation" (*episkopē*) is a time when God comes to his people, to rescue or to judge. Zechariah praised God that he "has visited and redeemed his people" (1:68), and the crowd at Nain said, "God has visited his people!" (7:16). Jerusalem missed the day God came to it in person.
- **One view of the timing.** Guzik, like a number of dispensational writers following Robert Anderson, argues that Daniel 9:25 foretold this very day, counting 483 years of 360 days from a decree to rebuild Jerusalem. Others question the calculation, and many interpreters read Daniel's "weeks" differently. The point in Luke is plain either way: this was the moment God appointed, and the city missed it.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The Son of God did not weep vain and causeless tears, nor for a light matter, nor for himself." He adds a warning: "though Jesus wept over Jerusalem, he executed awful vengeance upon it." JFB notes the contrast with the joy just before, saying Jesus "yielded Himself alike freely to both," and cites Josephus on the Roman wall "built in three days." Guzik draws out the meaning of Jerusalem's name and the horrors of the siege.

#### Jesus Cleanses the Temple (vv45–48)
- **"He entered the temple and began to drive out those who sold" (v45).** In the vast outer court, the Court of the Gentiles, merchants sold animals for sacrifice and moneychangers exchanged foreign coins for the Tyrian shekels used for the temple tax. Pilgrims needed these services, but the trade had moved into the one area where Gentiles could pray, and prices were controlled by the high priestly families. Luke tells this much more briefly than Mark (Mark 11:15–17). John records a similar act at the start of Jesus' ministry (John 2:13–22), and many think Jesus did it twice.
- **"My house shall be a house of prayer" (v46).** From Isaiah 56:7, where God promises that foreigners who love him will be brought to his holy mountain. Mark includes the rest of the line, "for all the nations." Luke leaves it out, perhaps because by the time he wrote, the temple had been destroyed, and the prayer of the nations was happening elsewhere.
- **"But you have made it a den of robbers" (v46).** From Jeremiah 7:11. In Jeremiah's day people sinned all week and then ran to the temple as if it were a hideout where they were safe. Jeremiah warned that God would destroy that temple, and he did, in 586 BC. Jesus' quotation carries the same warning.
- **"He was teaching daily in the temple" (v47).** Having cleared the court, he occupies it. Malachi foretold that "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple" (Mal 3:1).
- **"The chief priests and the scribes and the principal men of the people were seeking to destroy him" (v47).** In Jerusalem the opposition now includes the priestly aristocracy who ran the temple, as well as leading laymen of the council. Jesus' action struck at their authority and their income.
- **"All the people were hanging on his words" (v48).** The verb pictures someone clinging to or hanging from something. The crowds protect him for now. The leaders will have to find another way, and in chapter 22 they will.
- **Commentators:** JFB counts this as a second cleansing, the first being in John 2, and notes that the gentler "house of trade" used then has become "den of thieves" now, men "banded together for plunder." Henry prays that Christ will "cleanse our hearts by his Spirit, from all that defiles." Guzik describes the merchants and priests overcharging worshipers in the Court of the Gentiles, and he applies the scene to Christ cleansing the heart of the believer.

#### Pointing to Christ
In one chapter Jesus goes into the house of a sinner and into the house of God, and both need him. He finds the lost man in a tree and tells him salvation has come today. He rides in as the humble king of Zechariah 9, receives praise that the stones themselves would give, and then weeps for the city that will crucify him. The parable shows that he will go away to receive his kingdom and come back. Before that, he will go to the cross, and the peace "in heaven" (v38) will be made there.
