*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." After three songs of Zion (46–48), Psalm 49 changes tone completely. It is a wisdom psalm, closer to Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes than to a hymn. Matthew Henry puts it well: "This psalm is a sermon." It takes on a hard question every generation faces: why should God's people fear or envy the rich and powerful, especially when those people use their wealth against them? The answer is death. Death levels everyone, and money cannot buy a way out of it. But the psalm also contains one of the clearest hopes of life beyond death in the Old Testament: "But God will ransom my soul from the power of Sheol, for he will receive me" (v15). No date or author beyond the Korahite singers is given. The psalm has an introduction (vv1–4) and two main parts (vv5–12 and vv13–20), each closed by nearly the same refrain (vv12, 20).

#### Hear This, All Peoples (vv1–4)
- **"Hear this, all peoples! Give ear, all inhabitants of the world" (v1).** Like Psalm 47, this psalm addresses the whole world, not only Israel. Wisdom is for everyone, because death is everyone's problem. "World" (*cheled*) is an unusual word that can mean the world of passing time, this short-lived age (JFB: "duration of life"). Psalm 39:5 uses it for the brief span of a life.
- **"Both low and high, rich and poor together!" (v2).** "Low and high" is literally "sons of Adam and sons of man," two Hebrew words for humanity, *adam* and *ish*. Many take the first as ordinary people and the second as people of rank. The psalm speaks to both: the poor need comfort against the rich, and the rich need warning.
- **"My mouth shall speak wisdom… understanding" (v3).** The speaker sounds like a teacher in Proverbs (Prov 1:1–6). Both "wisdom" and "understanding" are plural in Hebrew, which often means "deep" or "full" wisdom.
- **"I will incline my ear to a proverb" (v4).** Before teaching, the wise man listens. He turns his ear to receive wisdom from God before he speaks it. "Proverb" (*mashal*) means a comparison or saying, and the psalm's main comparison comes in v12: man is "like the beasts."
- **"I will solve my riddle to the music of the lyre" (v4).** "Riddle" (*chidah*) is a puzzle or hard question, the word used of Samson's riddle (Judg 14:12) and the queen of Sheba's hard questions for Solomon (1 Kgs 10:1). The riddle here is the old puzzle of why the wicked prosper. The teacher sings his answer.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the psalmist means "to awaken worldly people out of their security" and to comfort the godly in a day of distress. Guzik notes that "riddle" means something hard to grasp, not secret knowledge, and cites Kidner on how the call goes out to all of humanity, not just Israel. JFB notes that "parable" and "proverb" can render the same Hebrew word.

#### What Money Cannot Buy (vv5–12)
- **"Why should I fear in times of trouble" (v5).** The question is the theme of the psalm. The trouble is real. The teacher is surrounded by people who have money and power and use it against him.
- **"When the iniquity of those who cheat me surrounds me" (v5).** Literally "the iniquity of my heels." The word for heel (*'aqev*) is the root of Jacob's name, the one who grabbed his brother's heel and supplanted him (Gen 25:26; 27:36). JFB renders it "my supplanters," people who trip others up to get ahead. They follow at his heels, waiting for a chance to bring him down.
- **"Those who trust in their wealth and boast of the abundance of their riches" (v6).** The problem is not wealth itself but trusting it and boasting in it. Abraham and Job were rich. What the Bible condemns is the heart that leans on money (52:7; Prov 11:28; 1 Tim 6:17).
- **"Truly no man can ransom another" (v7).** This is the heart of the first part. "Ransom" (*padah*) is the word for buying someone back: buying a firstborn son back from God (Exod 13:13), or buying a slave out of bondage (Exod 21:8). The lexicon confirms the meaning: to ransom for a price, from violence and death. The Hebrew line is short and punchy, and its first word can be read two ways. The King James reads it as "brother": "None of them can by any means redeem his brother." The ESV reads a near-identical word meaning "surely" ("Truly no man"). Either way, the point holds. No one can buy anyone out of death, not even his own brother.
- **"Or give to God the price of his life" (v7).** "Price" (*kopher*) is a ransom payment. The law of Moses allowed a man to pay a *kopher* to save his life in some cases (Exod 21:30), and every Israelite paid a half-shekel "ransom for his life" at the census (Exod 30:12). But when it comes to death itself, there is no price high enough. God is not for sale.
- **"The ransom of their life is costly and can never suffice" (v8).** Life is too valuable to be bought with money. Jesus asked the same question: "What can a man give in return for his soul?" (Mark 8:37). Peter answers it: "You were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19).
- **"That he should live on forever and never see the pit" (v9).** "The pit" is the grave, and beyond it the realm of the dead. JFB notes that "pit" here shows that "soul" in v8 means "life." The rich man's money cannot buy him one extra day.
- **"Even the wise die; the fool and the stupid alike must perish" (v10).** Death comes to the clever and the foolish, the hard-working and the lazy. Ecclesiastes says the same: "How the wise dies just like the fool!" (Eccl 2:16).
- **"And leave their wealth to others" (v10).** They cannot take it with them, and they do not even get to choose who gets it (Eccl 2:18–19; Luke 12:20: "the things you have prepared, whose will they be?").
- **"Their graves are their homes forever" (v11).** A small but nice textual point. The Hebrew text reads "their inward thought (*qirbam*) is that their houses are forever." The Septuagint, Syriac and Aramaic versions read "their graves (*qibram*) are their houses forever," which needs only two letters swapped. The ESV follows the versions and gives the Hebrew in a footnote. Both readings are pointed: they *think* their homes will last forever, and in fact their *grave* is the only home they keep forever.
- **"Though they called lands by their own names" (v11).** People name estates, cities and buildings after themselves to be remembered (Gen 4:17; 2 Sam 18:18, where Absalom set up a pillar so his name would not be forgotten). The irony is sharp: their names are on the land, but they are under it.
- **"Man in his pomp will not remain; he is like the beasts that perish" (v12).** This is the refrain. "Pomp" (*yeqar*) means honor, splendor, preciousness. "Remain" (*yalin*) literally means "lodge overnight." JFB: "lodgeth not," that is, does not stay till morning. The rich man in all his glory is a guest in this world for one night. In death, nothing separates him from the animals. The comparison is not that people *are* animals (Gen 1:26–27 says otherwise) but that, without God, they *end* like them.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the folly of trusting wealth: it cannot save one's friends from death, cannot save oneself, and cannot secure lasting happiness in this world or the next. On v7 he reads "none of them can by any means redeem his brother," with the King James. Guzik points to the rich fool in Luke 12 and passes on Boice's story of Voltaire on his deathbed, offering his doctor half of what he owned for six more weeks of life, and Spurgeon's comment that naming lands after oneself is like writing on water. JFB notes that "iniquity of my heels" means "my supplanters," and that "lodgeth not" pictures a sudden death, like beasts taken without warning.

#### But God Will Ransom My Soul (vv13–20)
- **"This is the path of those who have foolish confidence" (v13).** "Path" is their way of life. The word for "foolish confidence" (*kesel*) can mean both "confidence" and "folly." Their confidence *is* their folly.
- **"Yet after them people approve of their boasts. Selah" (v13).** Even after they die, others admire them and copy their philosophy. The ESV footnote offers another reading: "and of those after them who approve of their boasts," which makes the followers share the same path and the same end. Guzik and VanGemeren take it that way. Folly is easily inherited.
- **"Like sheep they are appointed for Sheol" (v14).** Sheep are driven where the shepherd leads, without knowing where they are going. JFB: "unwittingly." The proud rich are herded toward the grave like a flock to the slaughter. "Sheol" is the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead, often simply "the grave."
- **"Death shall be their shepherd" (v14).** A chilling picture, and the exact opposite of Psalm 23. Those who trust the LORD can say, "the LORD is my shepherd." Those who trust their wealth will find that death is theirs. "Shepherd" can also mean "graze on," so the older translations had "death shall feed on them" (KJV), death feeding on them as sheep feed on grass. Both senses are in the verb (*ra'ah*).
- **"And the upright shall rule over them in the morning" (v14).** "In the morning" is the moment after the long night, when things are put right. Many readers, including Henry, hear a hint of resurrection morning here. The tables will be turned: those who oppressed the upright will find the upright ruling over them (Dan 7:22, 27; Mal 4:3). JFB takes "in the morning" more cautiously as "suddenly, or in their turn." The psalm does not spell it out, but it points to a day when God's justice is done.
- **"Their form shall be consumed in Sheol, with no place to dwell" (v14).** The body they pampered wastes away in the grave. The Hebrew of this line is very difficult and translations vary, but the sense is clear: nothing of their glory survives.
- **"But God will ransom my soul from the power of Sheol" (v15).** This is the turning point of the psalm. Kidner, cited by Guzik, calls it one of the great peaks of Old Testament hope. The same verb "ransom" (*padah*) from v7 returns. No man can pay the ransom for another's life, but God can and will. "The power" is literally "the hand" of Sheol (JFB), death's grip. God pries the teacher out of death's hand. Hosea uses the same words: "Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol?" (Hos 13:14), and Paul takes up that verse in his great chapter on the resurrection (1 Cor 15:55).
- **"For he will receive me" (v15).** "Receive" (*laqach*) is literally "take." It is the verb used when Enoch "was not, for God took him" (Gen 5:24), and when the LORD "took" Elijah up to heaven (2 Kgs 2:3, 5, 10). Psalm 73:24 uses it the same way: "afterward you will receive me to glory." The psalmist does not explain how. He only knows that death will not have the last word, because God will take him to himself. The pause that follows, "Selah," is well placed.
- **"Be not afraid when a man becomes rich" (v16).** Now the teaching is applied, and it answers the question of v5. Do not fear the rich, and do not envy them. Psalm 37 gives the same counsel (37:1, 7).
- **"For when he dies he will carry nothing away; his glory will not go down after him" (v17).** "Glory" is his wealth and honor, all the trappings of success. It stays behind. Job said it first: "Naked I came from my mother's womb, and naked shall I return" (Job 1:21), and Paul repeats it: "we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world" (1 Tim 6:7).
- **"Though, while he lives, he counts himself blessed" (v18).** Literally "he blessed his soul" (JFB), he congratulated himself. Jesus' rich fool says the same: "Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry" (Luke 12:19). "And though you get praise when you do well for yourself": the world always applauds success. JFB: the flatterers feed the rich fool's self-satisfaction, but "he has had his portion" (compare Luke 16:25).
- **"His soul will go to the generation of his fathers, who will never again see light" (v19).** He joins his ancestors in the dark. Contrast the upright, who rule "in the morning" (v14).
- **"Man in his pomp yet without understanding is like the beasts that perish" (v20).** The refrain returns with one small change. In v12 it said man in his pomp "will not remain" (*yalin*). Here it says "yet without understanding" (*yavin*). In Hebrew the two words differ by one letter, a deliberate wordplay. JFB notices the change makes the folly explicit. The problem is not wealth but wealth without understanding, a life that never grasped the wisdom of this psalm. A rich person who understands that riches cannot save, and trusts God instead, is not like the beasts.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads v14 as death feeding on them, "the second death," and the morning as the morning of the resurrection, when "the upright shall have dominion over them." On v15 he writes: "The God of life, that was its Creator at first, can and will be its Redeemer at last," and "He redeems their souls, that he may receive them." Guzik ties "receive" to Enoch, and sets the two refrains side by side: for the rich who trust wealth, this life is the best they will ever have, but for the upright it is the worst. He passes on Spurgeon's blunt line that the banker and the beggar decay alike in the grave. JFB reads "like sheep" as "unwittingly" and notes that v20's "understandeth not" sharpens v12.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 49 sets the problem plainly: "no man can ransom another… the ransom of their life is costly and can never suffice." Then it says, "But God will ransom my soul." The New Testament shows how. Jesus said that "the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The price no fortune could pay was paid "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Pet 1:18–19). And having paid it, he broke the power of Sheol: "I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades" (Rev 1:18). The rich fool of Jesus' parable lived out the first half of this psalm (Luke 12:16–21). Those who trust Christ are given the second half: the Good Shepherd in place of the shepherd Death, and the promise of the morning, when he will "receive" them to himself, "that where I am you may be also" (John 14:3).
