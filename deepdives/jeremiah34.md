*Setting:* The Book of Consolation (chapters 30–33) is over, and the book turns back to the grim present: the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem, 588–587 BC. Chapters 34–39 lead step by step to the city's fall. This chapter holds two scenes from the siege. In the first, Jeremiah gives King Zedekiah a hard word mixed with an unexpected mercy. In the second, which happened a little later, the people of Jerusalem make a solemn covenant to free their Hebrew slaves, and then, when the Babylonian army pulls back for a while, they take the slaves back. It is a sharp picture of a people who turn to God when they are frightened and turn away the moment the pressure lifts. Chapter 35 follows with the opposite picture, a family that kept its word for two hundred years.

#### Zedekiah to Die in Babylon (vv1–22)
- **"When Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and all his army and all the kingdoms of the earth under his dominion and all the peoples were fighting against Jerusalem" (v1).** Babylon's army included troops drawn from its subject nations, so it looked like the whole world had come against one small city. This is early in the siege, while other Judean towns were still holding out (v7).
- **"Go and speak to Zedekiah king of Judah" (v2).** Zedekiah was a son of Josiah, put on the throne by Nebuchadnezzar in 597 BC after his nephew Jehoiachin was taken to Babylon (2 Kgs 24:17). He swore loyalty to Babylon and then rebelled, trusting Egypt (2 Kgs 24:20; Ezek 17:15–18). Jeremiah's message repeats what he says throughout these chapters: **"I am giving this city into the hand of the king of Babylon, and he shall burn it with fire."**
- **"You shall see the king of Babylon eye to eye and speak with him face to face. And you shall go to Babylon" (v3).** As in 32:4. Zedekiah would have to answer personally to the king whose oath he had broken. It happened at Riblah in Syria, where Nebuchadnezzar passed sentence on him (39:5).
- **"Yet hear the word of the LORD, O Zedekiah… 'You shall not die by the sword. You shall die in peace'" (vv4–5).** A surprising word of mercy inside the judgment. Zedekiah would not be executed. He would die a natural death. That is what happened, though in grim circumstances. His sons were killed before his eyes, he was blinded and taken to Babylon in chains, and he was kept in prison "till the day of his death" (39:6–7; 52:10–11). "In peace" here means not by violence, not a life without suffering. Some interpreters think the fuller mercy was offered on the condition that he obey God's word and surrender (38:17–18), and that Zedekiah's refusal turned what could have been an honorable captivity into a bitter one.
- **"As spices were burned for your fathers… so people shall burn spices for you and lament for you, saying, 'Alas, lord!'" (v5).** Israelites did not cremate their dead. The "burning" was a great fire of spices lit in a king's honor at his burial, as for King Asa (2 Chr 16:14). Jehoram, a wicked king, was denied it (2 Chr 21:19). Zedekiah would be mourned as a king. Compare the curse on his brother Jehoiakim: no one would lament him, saying, "Ah, lord!" (22:18). Whether that mourning took place among the exiles in Babylon is not recorded.
- **"Lachish and Azekah, for these were the only fortified cities of Judah that remained" (v7).** Lachish, about 30 miles southwest of Jerusalem, was Judah's second city. Azekah lay about 11 miles north of it. Both guarded the routes from the coastal plain into the hill country, and both had been fortified by Rehoboam (2 Chr 11:9). In 1935 archaeologists digging the gate area at Lachish found the "Lachish Letters," notes written in ink on broken pottery by a Judean officer to his commander during this very campaign. One of them ends: "we are watching for the signals of Lachish… for we cannot see Azekah." Most scholars read it to mean that Azekah's signal fire had gone out, because the town had fallen. It is a remarkable window onto the days described in this verse.
- **"After King Zedekiah had made a covenant with all the people in Jerusalem to make a proclamation of liberty to them" (v8).** The second scene. "Liberty" is *deror*, the word used for the year of jubilee, when "you shall proclaim liberty throughout the land" (Lev 25:10). Isaiah used it for the Servant's ministry: "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Isa 61:1), and Jesus read that passage in Nazareth (Luke 4:18–19).
- **"That everyone should set free his Hebrew slaves, male and female, so that no one should enslave a Jew, his brother" (v9).** Slavery among Israelites was usually debt slavery. A poor man might sell himself or a child into service to pay what he owed. The Law limited it. A Hebrew servant was to serve six years and go free in the seventh, and he was not to be sent away empty-handed (Exod 21:2; Deut 15:12–15). An Israelite was not to be treated "as a slave" but "as a hired worker," because "they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt" (Lev 25:39–42). Note the word "brother." The Law saw the servant as family.
- **Why release the slaves then?** The text does not say why Zedekiah made this covenant. Several reasons have been suggested, and more than one may be true. It may have been a sincere act of repentance in a crisis, an attempt to win God's favor so he would save the city. It may also have been practical. During a siege there were no fields to work outside the walls, masters had to feed their servants from shrinking supplies, and freed men could be added to the defense. Verse 15 says that, whatever else was going on, the release itself was right: **"You recently repented and did what was right in my eyes."**
- **"And they obeyed… They obeyed and set them free" (v10).** The repetition stresses that they really did it. All the officials and people signed on.
- **"But afterward they turned around and took back the male and female slaves they had set free" (v11).** What changed is explained in v21. The Babylonian army **"has withdrawn from you."** When an Egyptian army under Pharaoh Hophra marched toward Judah, the Babylonians lifted the siege to meet it (37:5, 11). Jerusalem breathed again, and the masters reclaimed their servants. The covenant had lasted only as long as the danger.
- **"I myself made a covenant with your fathers when I brought them out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (v13).** God's argument starts with Israel's own story. They had been slaves, and God had freed them. A people redeemed from slavery should never hold their own brothers in permanent slavery (Deut 15:15).
- **"At the end of seven years each of you must set free the fellow Hebrew who has been sold to you and has served you six years" (v14).** A quotation of Deuteronomy 15:1, 12. "At the end of seven years" means in the seventh year, after six years' service. **"But your fathers did not listen to me."** The law had long been ignored. Zedekiah's covenant was a recovery of something old, which makes its reversal worse.
- **"You made a covenant before me in the house that is called by my name" (v15).** The oath was taken in the temple, in God's presence. **"But then you turned around and profaned my name" (v16).** "Profaned" means treated as common or empty. To swear by God and then break the oath made his name look worthless (Exod 20:7; Lev 19:12). Note the play on "turn" (*shub*): they "turned" in repentance (v15) and then "turned around" again (v16). **"Whom you had set free according to their desire."** Literally "free for their own soul," free to do as they wished.
- **"You have not obeyed me by proclaiming liberty… behold, I proclaim to you liberty to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine" (v17).** A bitter wordplay. They refused to release their brothers, so God would release them, set them loose from his protection, to the three great killers of a siege. **"I will make you a horror to all the kingdoms of the earth."** The phrase from the covenant curses (Deut 28:25; Jer 15:4; 24:9).
- **"I will make them like the calf that they cut in two and passed between its parts" (v18).** This explains how the covenant had been made. In the ancient Near East, the parties to a solemn treaty would cut an animal in half and walk between the pieces. The ritual was a self-curse: "May this happen to me if I break my word." An Aramaic treaty found at Sefire in Syria, from the 8th century BC, spells it out: as a calf is cut in two, so may the treaty-breaker be cut in two. This is why the Hebrew idiom for making a covenant is literally to "cut" a covenant (*karat berit*). God himself took part in such a ceremony with Abraham. Abraham laid out the halves of the animals, and then "a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces" (Gen 15:9–18). There God alone passed through, binding himself to his promise.
- **"The men who transgressed my covenant" (v18).** The Hebrew carries a wordplay that English hides. "Transgressed" and "passed between" are the same verb, *abar*, to cross over. They "crossed over" between the pieces, and then they "crossed over" the covenant. They walked through the curse and then broke the oath.
- **"The officials of Judah, the officials of Jerusalem, the eunuchs, the priests, and all the people of the land" (v19).** Every class took the oath, and every class broke it. "Eunuchs" probably means court officials generally.
- **"Their dead bodies shall be food for the birds of the air and the beasts of the earth" (v20).** To lie unburied was the worst shame in Israel's world (7:33; 16:4; Deut 28:26). The curse they spoke over themselves would come true.
- **"The army of the king of Babylon which has withdrawn from you" (v21).** The pause in the siege was only a pause. **"I will command… and will bring them back to this city. And they will fight against it and take it and burn it with fire" (v22).** The Babylonians would come back because God would command them. They did, and Egypt's army went home without a fight (37:7–8).
- **Commentators:** On vv4–5 Henry writes, "A man may die in a prison and yet die in peace." JFB takes the "burning" in v5 as an honor of "aromatic spices," not cremation, a point Guzik also makes, citing Feinberg. Guzik suggests the promise of a peaceful death may have depended on Zedekiah's obedience. He also cites Clarke's report of a Jewish tradition that Zedekiah died in Babylon, mocked, and was then buried with honor, which is a tradition, not part of the text. Guzik and Henry both doubt the depth of the people's repentance. Guzik weighs panic, piety and self-interest and leans toward self-interest, since the masters could not use their servants during the siege. Henry says that taking them back "was jesting with God almighty, as if he could be imposed upon by fallacious promises," and draws a gospel lesson from the release law: "we must release as we are released, forgive as we are forgiven." On v17 Henry writes that liberty to sin "is but a liberty to the sorest judgments," and JFB calls it "retribution answering to the offense." Guzik contrasts it with the freedom Christ gives, which is never taken back. All three explain the calf ritual from Genesis 15, and JFB states its meaning plainly: those who passed between the pieces implied they should be "cut in two" if they broke faith.

#### Pointing to Christ
Judah's leaders walked between the pieces of a calf, swore before God, and broke their word within weeks. Long before, God had walked between the pieces alone while Abraham slept, binding himself to keep his promise whatever it cost. In Jesus that cost was paid. He bore the covenant curse that his people deserved, "becoming a curse for us" (Gal 3:13), and he has proclaimed a liberty that is never taken back. He stood in Nazareth and read, "He has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Luke 4:18). "So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36). Freed people, in turn, are called to free others and forgive as they have been forgiven.
