*Setting:* God is still giving Moses the laws of offering from the tent of meeting at Sinai. The first three offerings (chs 1–3) were mostly voluntary, gifts a worshiper chose to bring. Chapter 4 begins something new. A fresh introduction, "And the LORD spoke to Moses," marks the start of the required offerings, brought when a person had actually broken one of God's commands. The sin offering is the first of them. The chapter is laid out like a ladder from the top down: the high priest, the whole nation, a tribal leader and finally an ordinary person.

#### Laws for Sin Offerings (vv1–35)
- **"If anyone sins unintentionally" (v2).** The word is *shegagah*, "error, inadvertence." BDB notes that it is set against sinning "with a high hand" (Num 15:30), that is, defiantly. It covers sins done in ignorance, by mistake or through carelessness, the kind a person only discovers afterward (vv13–14, 23, 28). Numbers 15:30–31 says that for deliberate, defiant rebellion against God's word there was no sacrifice to bring. Later chapters show the line is not quite so sharp: some knowing sins can be confessed and atoned for (5:1, 5; 6:1–7).
- **"A sin offering" (v3).** The Hebrew word, *hatta't*, is simply the ordinary word for "sin." The same word means both the wrong and the offering that deals with it. Many scholars today call it the "purification offering," because its blood is put on the altar and the sanctuary to cleanse them from the pollution sin caused. The Tyndale notes connect the root with "missing the mark." Both ideas are present: sin defiles the person and God's house, and the blood cleanses both.
- **"If it is the anointed priest who sins, thus bringing guilt on the people" (v3).** "The anointed priest" means the high priest (compare 8:12). "Anointed" is *mashiah*, the word that later gives us "Messiah." His sin is the most serious because he stands for the whole nation before God. When the high priest goes wrong, everyone is affected. The text assumes the high priest could sin. Matthew Henry draws the obvious conclusion: God "never had any infallible priest in his church upon earth."
- **The blood carried inside (vv5–7).** For the high priest's sin, the blood was not only put on the outer altar. It was carried into the Holy Place, sprinkled **"seven times… in front of the veil,"** and smeared on **"the horns of the altar of fragrant incense."** The more responsible the sinner, the further into the sanctuary his sin reached, and the further in the blood had to go. The incense altar stood right before the veil, where the priest prayed for the people. His sin had stained the place of intercession.
- **"All the rest of the blood… he shall pour out at the base of the altar" (v7).** Blood belonged to God (17:11). Whatever was not used was not thrown away carelessly. It was returned to the altar.
- **"Outside the camp to a clean place, to the ash heap" (vv11–12).** The fat was burned on the altar as with the peace offering (vv8–10), but the rest of the bull was not eaten. It was carried out and burned completely outside the camp. The Tyndale notes suggest that a priest could not eat the offering for his own sin and so profit from it. The place was "clean," not a garbage dump, since what had been offered was still holy.
- **"If the whole congregation of Israel sins unintentionally" (vv13–21).** A whole nation can sin without realizing it. When the wrong comes to light, the same bull and the same ritual are required as for the high priest. **"The elders of the congregation shall lay their hands on the head of the bull"** (v15). The elders, family and tribal heads, stood in for the people. The section ends with the first of this chapter's great promises: **"the priest shall make atonement for them, and they shall be forgiven"** (v20).
- **"When a leader sins" (vv22–26).** "Leader" is *nasi'*, a chief of a tribe or clan (Num 1:16). He brings **"a goat, a male without blemish"** (v23), less than a bull but more than a commoner's offering. Now the blood goes only on the horns of the bronze altar in the courtyard (v25). The meat of this offering was eaten by the priests (6:26).
- **"If anyone of the common people sins" (vv27–35).** An ordinary Israelite brings a female goat or lamb. The same pattern repeats: hand on the head, the animal killed, blood on the horns, the fat burned. Those who could not afford even that could bring birds or a little flour (5:7–13). The cost was scaled to a person's position and means, but no one was too great or too small to need it. Henry puts it well: "the greatest are not above, the meanest are not below Divine justice."
- **"He shall be forgiven" (vv20, 26, 31, 35).** Four times. The passive voice is important. The priest makes atonement, but only God forgives. The word is *salah*, used in the Old Testament only of God's forgiving. These laws are not only about rules. They are God's assurance that a sinner who has confessed and brought what he asked can know he is forgiven.
- **Commentators:** All three see the sliding scale as a lesson in responsibility, with more required of those who lead (compare Jas 3:1). Guzik reads the blood on the incense altar as a sign that sin hinders prayer, and he stresses that this offering was for people whose hearts were turned toward God, not for the defiant. Henry: in the burning outside the camp, "the load of sin was supposed then to be borne by the guiltless animal." He also urges daily prayer for God to show hidden faults (Ps 19:12). JFB thinks these offerings gave "a formal deliverance from a secular penalty" and that true pardon came only through Christ. The Tyndale notes take the repeated "they shall be forgiven" more at face value, as real forgiveness given by God through the means he appointed, which Hebrews says pointed forward to Christ (Heb 10:1–4). JFB has no notes on vv4–10.

#### Pointing to Christ
Hebrews picks up the burning outside the camp directly: "the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places by the high priest as a sacrifice for sin are burned outside the camp. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Heb 13:11–12). Christ is both the sinless high priest who needed no offering for himself (Heb 7:26–27) and the offering. Paul says God "made him to be sin who knew no sin" (2 Cor 5:21), and since the Hebrew *hatta't* means both "sin" and "sin offering," many think Paul has this chapter in mind. And John gives the same assurance this chapter repeats four times: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9).
