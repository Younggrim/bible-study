*Setting:* Psalm 43 has no title, which is rare in Book Two of the Psalter, and it ends with the same refrain that runs through Psalm 42 (42:5, 11; 43:5). Many Hebrew manuscripts join the two as one psalm, and most scholars think they were written as one song in three stanzas, each closing with "Why are you cast down, O my soul?" Others, including JFB, keep them separate: "there is no good reason to suppose this a part of the preceding, though the scope is the same." Henry suggests the same trouble came back and the psalmist turned to the same remedy, and Spurgeon, cited by Guzik, thinks it a companion hymn. Either way, it should be read straight after Psalm 42. The singer, probably a Levite of the Sons of Korah, is still far from Jerusalem and still taunted by enemies. But something has shifted. Psalm 42 looked back with longing at the processions to God's house. Psalm 43 looks forward and asks God to lead him back. It is the shortest step from darkness toward the altar.

#### Vindicate Me, O God (vv1–2)
- **"Vindicate me, O God, and defend my cause" (v1).** "Vindicate" is literally "judge me," a law-court word. He asks God to hear his case and declare him in the right. "Defend my cause" is the language of a lawyer pleading for a client (35:1; Mic 7:9). He does not take his own revenge or argue with his accusers. He takes the case to the highest court.
- **"Against an ungodly people" (v1).** Literally "a nation not *chasid*," a people without loyal love. The godly are *chasidim*, "saints," the faithful ones (37:28). These people are the opposite. It may mean a foreign nation, if the psalmist is in exile, or a faithless group within Israel. JFB: they are "neither in character or condition objects of God's favor."
- **"From the deceitful and unjust man deliver me!" (v1).** The singular may point to one leader of the opposition, or simply stand for the type. Deceit is what makes such people dangerous. They hide injustice behind smooth words. Guzik notes that this is exactly why the psalmist turns to God: he cannot fight deceit with his own strength.
- **"For you are the God in whom I take refuge" (v2).** Literally "the God of my stronghold," as JFB notes. A stronghold is a fortress on high ground where people ran when an enemy came (18:2).
- **"Why have you rejected me?" (v2).** In 42:9 he asked "Why have you forgotten me?" Now it is stronger: "rejected," cast off. JFB: rejected "in scorn." This question recurs in the Psalms of Book Two and Three (44:9, 23; 60:1; 74:1; 88:14). Scripture allows God's people to ask it.
- **"Why do I go about mourning because of the oppression of the enemy?" (v2).** Nearly word for word from 42:9. "Mourning" again is the dark, unwashed look of a mourner (38:6). The repetition is one of the strongest signs that the two psalms belong together.
- **Faith and complaint together.** In one verse he calls God his stronghold and asks why God has rejected him. He does not resolve the tension by giving up either. That is what lament looks like in the Bible.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees vv1–2 as David's appeal to God "concerning the injuries that were done him by his enemies," and notes that, like Christ in Gethsemane, the psalmist "prayed a second and third time" in the same words. Guzik stresses that the psalmist brings his sense of injustice to God's throne instead of defending himself, and he passes on Poole's description of deceitful men as hateful to God and to good people. JFB glosses "judge" as "vindicate."

#### Send Out Your Light and Your Truth (vv3–4)
- **"Send out your light and your truth; let them lead me" (v3).** This is the turning point of Psalms 42–43. Instead of only remembering or complaining, he asks God for something specific. Light and truth are pictured as two messengers, sent out from God's presence like servants, to take him by the hand and guide him home. JFB calls them "personified messengers." The same kind of picture appears in 23:6, where goodness and mercy "follow" the believer, and in 57:3, where God sends out "his steadfast love and his faithfulness."
- **"Your light" (v3).** God's light is his favor and presence, the light of his face (4:6; 27:1, "The LORD is my light and my salvation"). In 42:5 salvation was pictured as God's face turned toward him.
- **"Your truth" (v3).** The Hebrew *'emet* means faithfulness as well as truth: God's reliability, keeping his promises. JFB: shown in God "fulfilling promises." Light and truth together are God's goodness and his word, his presence and his promise. Some have connected them with the Urim and Thummim on the high priest's breastplate (Exod 28:30), whose names are often explained as "lights and perfections," but that is only a suggestion. Psalm 119:105 gives the plainer sense: "Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path."
- **"Let them bring me to your holy hill and to your dwelling!" (v3).** The "holy hill" is Mount Zion in Jerusalem, where the ark was kept and later the temple stood (2:6; 15:1). "Dwelling" is plural in Hebrew, "dwellings," which JFB explains as an allusion to "the various courts" of the sanctuary. Compare the little hill Mizar in 42:6 with God's holy hill here.
- **"Then I will go to the altar of God" (v4).** The order matters: light and truth lead him to God's house, then to the altar, then to God himself. The altar was where sacrifices were offered. Here it is probably a thank offering for deliverance (Lev 7:12), the meal of fellowship with God. G. Campbell Morgan, cited by Guzik, says the way to God is always the way of the altar.
- **"To God my exceeding joy" (v4).** Literally "to God, the gladness of my joy," or "the joy of my rejoicing." Two words for joy are piled up. The goal is not the altar or the temple building or even the restored festivals of 42:4. It is God himself. Augustine's line fits: "our heart is restless until it rests in you." The psalm that began with a thirsty deer ends at the spring.
- **"And I will praise you with the lyre, O God, my God" (v4).** The lyre (*kinnor*) was a small stringed instrument, the one David played for Saul (1 Sam 16:23). As a Levite singer the psalmist would take his instrument back up. "O God, my God" answers the taunt "Where is your God?" (42:3, 10). He is mine.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv3–4 as a prayer that God would "restore to him the free enjoyment of public ordinances again," with a promise to use them well. Guzik notes that the psalmist asks to be *led* by God's light and truth, not just to admire them, and passes on Spurgeon's point that the goal is communion with God, not using truth for show. Guzik links the altar to the cross and to Hebrews 13:10–15, where Christians offer "a sacrifice of praise." JFB calls the altar "the chief place of worship" and notes that the harp makes praise prominent in his offering.

#### Hope in God (v5)
- **"Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me?" (v5).** The refrain comes a third time, exactly as in 42:11. Nothing outward has changed. He is still far from Zion and still taunted. But he has asked God to lead him, and he has pictured himself at the altar. The refrain now sounds less like a struggle and more like a settled answer.
- **"Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God" (v5).** The final words of the whole song are "my God." Morgan, cited by Guzik, observes that the darkness is still there, but now the shining path is visible through it. Maclaren, also cited by Guzik, says faith may struggle long, but it has the last word. JFB gives no separate note on this verse, since it repeats 42:11.
- **Why repeat it?** Feelings return, so the answer to them has to be repeated. Peter tells believers in suffering to set their hope fully on grace (1 Pet 1:13), and Paul says "hope does not put us to shame" (Rom 5:5). The refrain is a pattern for honest believers: name the feeling, question it, and tell your soul to hope in God.

#### Pointing to Christ
The psalmist asked God to send out his light and his truth to lead him to God's dwelling. God answered more fully than he could have imagined. He sent his Son, who said, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12) and "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6). In Jesus, God's light and truth did not just guide a pilgrim to a building on a hill. They came and lived among us (John 1:14). And Jesus himself is the altar's sacrifice, so that through him believers can "draw near" with confidence (Heb 10:19–22) and come, at last, "to God my exceeding joy."
