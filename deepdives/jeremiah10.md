*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably early in the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), still within the poems that follow Jeremiah's temple sermon (chs 7–10). Chapter 9 ended by placing Judah in a list with Egypt, Edom, Ammon and Moab, uncircumcised in heart like the nations around her. Chapter 10 presses the point: if Judah is living like the nations, she is also worshiping like them. The first half is a poem mocking idols and praising the living God, much like Isaiah 40 and 44. Then the tone turns sharply. A siege is coming, Jerusalem is pictured as a mother whose tent has been torn down, and Jeremiah ends with a prayer for mercy for his people and justice on their destroyers.

#### Idols and the Living God (vv1–25)
- **One ESV section, three movements.** The ESV keeps the whole chapter under one heading, but it falls into three parts: the living God set against idols (vv1–16), the coming exile and Jerusalem's lament (vv17–22), and Jeremiah's prayer (vv23–25).
- **"Hear the word… O house of Israel" (v1).** "Israel" here means the whole covenant people, now represented by Judah, the part that remained. To "hear" in Hebrew means to listen and obey. Hearing that changes nothing is not hearing at all.
- **"Learn not the way of the nations" (v2).** The verb is the ordinary word for being taught, the root of the later word for a disciple. The danger is not one dramatic act of apostasy but slowly being schooled by the culture around them. God's people are to be different on purpose (Lev 18:3; Deut 12:29–31).
- **"Nor be dismayed at the signs of the heavens" (v2).** Babylon especially was famous for astrology. Eclipses, comets and unusual movements of the planets were read as omens, and whole kingdoms halted business over a bad sign. Genesis 1:14 says God made the lights for signs and seasons, but they serve him. They do not rule people. Those who fear the Maker of the stars need not fear the stars.
- **"The customs of the peoples are vanity" (v3).** "Vanity" is *hevel*, breath or vapor (the ESV footnote gives "vapor" or "mist"), the key word of Ecclesiastes. Idolatry looks solid, but it is as empty as a puff of breath on a cold morning.
- **"A tree from the forest is cut down" (vv3–4).** The satire follows the idol through the workshop. A craftsman fells a tree, shapes it with an axe, covers it with silver and gold, and nails it down "so that it cannot move." The god must be fastened in place so it will not fall over. Isaiah mocks the same process at length (Isa 40:18–20; 44:9–20; 46:6–7).
- **Is this about Christmas trees?** Some readers have taken vv3–4 as a ban on Christmas trees. The passage describes carving a tree into an idol, shaping it "with an axe" and overlaying it with metal, and v5 calls the result an idol that must be carried. It is about worshiping a made god, not about decorating a home. A believer who is convinced otherwise should follow conscience (Rom 14:5, 23), but the text itself is aimed at idolatry.
- **"Like scarecrows in a cucumber field" (v5).** The Hebrew *tomer* can mean a palm tree (so the KJV, "upright as the palm tree") or a post. Most modern versions take it as a scarecrow, a pole dressed up to frighten birds in a melon patch (compare Isa 1:8). It looks like a person, but it cannot speak, cannot walk and has to be carried in procession (Ps 115:5–7; Isa 46:1, 7). Only birds that know no better are scared of it.
- **"They cannot do evil, neither is it in them to do good" (v5).** There is nothing to fear from an idol and nothing to hope for. Isaiah 41:23 taunts the idols: "do good, or do harm," anything at all.
- **"There is none like you, O LORD" (v6).** The poem now alternates: verses about idols (vv2–5, 8–9, 11, 14–15) and verses about God (vv6–7, 10, 12–13, 16). The Hebrew of v6 is emphatic, literally "there is nothing at all like you." Israel had sung the same at the sea (Exod 15:11).
- **"Who would not fear you, O King of the nations?" (v7).** The LORD is not a local god of a small hill country. He is King over the very nations whose idols Judah is copying. Revelation picks up this line in the song of the Lamb: "Who will not fear, O Lord, and glorify your name?" (Rev 15:3–4).
- **"The instruction of idols is but wood!" (v8).** A compressed line, literally something like "the teaching of vanities, it is wood." Whatever an idol teaches is as lifeless as the material it is made of. People become like what they worship (Ps 115:8), and the "wise ones of the nations" (v7) become "stupid and foolish."
- **"Beaten silver… from Tarshish, and gold from Uphaz" (v9).** Tarshish was at the far western edge of the known world, probably Tartessus in southern Spain, famous for its metals. Uphaz is unknown. Some ancient versions read Ophir, the gold-producing region of Arabia or East Africa, and some scholars think the word may simply mean "refined gold." **"Violet and purple"** were the costliest dyes in the ancient world, the colors of royalty. The idol is dressed like a king, but it is only the "work of skilled men."
- **"But the LORD is the true God; he is the living God and the everlasting King" (v10).** Three titles answer the idols point for point. "True" is *ʾemet*, truth, reality, faithfulness: God is the real thing, where idols are breath. "Living" stands against wood that has "no breath" (v14). "Everlasting King" stands against images that rot and are carried off. Paul echoes this when he says the Thessalonians "turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God" (1 Thess 1:9).
- **Verse 11 is in Aramaic.** The ESV footnote notes this. It is the only Aramaic verse in Jeremiah; Aramaic was the international language of the Babylonian and Persian empires (Ezra 4–7 and Daniel 2–7 are also in Aramaic). It may have been a well-known saying, given in the language Judah's captors spoke, so that exiles would have a ready answer for their neighbors: "The gods who did not make the heavens and the earth shall perish." Some scholars think it was a marginal note copied into the text, but every ancient version contains it, and it fits the poem well. There is even a wordplay in Aramaic between "made" (*ʿabadu*) and "perish" (*yebadu*).
- **"It is he who made the earth by his power" (vv12–13).** The real test of a god is creation. Power, wisdom and understanding together made and still run the world (Prov 3:19–20). The thunder, rain, mist, lightning and wind of v13 were exactly what Baal, the Canaanite storm god, was supposed to control. The LORD claims them all as his: "he brings forth the wind from his storehouses." Psalm 135:7 uses almost the same words.
- **"Every goldsmith is put to shame by his idols" (v14).** The craftsman is embarrassed by his own product, because "there is no breath in them." The word for breath, *ruach*, can also mean spirit. No spirit lives in the image.
- **"A work of delusion" (v15).** Literally "a work of mockeries," something worth only ridicule. **"At the time of their punishment they shall perish."** When God judges the nations, their gods go down with them.
- **"Not like these is he who is the portion of Jacob" (v16).** "Portion" is *cheleq*, a share of land handed out by lot. When the land was divided, the Levites received no territory, because the LORD himself was their portion (Num 18:20). The psalmists claim the same: "The LORD is my chosen portion" (Ps 16:5; 73:26). And the relationship runs both ways: "Israel is the tribe of his inheritance." God's people belong to him as he belongs to them (Deut 32:9).
- **This poem appears twice.** Verses 12–16 are repeated almost word for word in 51:15–19, in the oracle against Babylon. The same confession that rebukes Judah's idolatry will be sung over the fall of the empire whose gods Judah feared.
- **"Gather up your bundle from the ground, O you who dwell under siege!" (v17).** The scene changes abruptly. Jerusalem is addressed as a woman sitting in a besieged city, told to pick up her little bundle of belongings and get ready to leave. This is the pack a refugee or captive could carry.
- **"I am slinging out the inhabitants of the land" (v18).** The verb is used for hurling a stone from a sling (1 Sam 17:49; 25:29). God himself will throw his people out of the land, quickly and far. **"That they may feel it"** is literally "that they may find," perhaps meaning they will finally find out by experience that the warnings were true.
- **When was this fulfilled?** The Tyndale notes connect it with the first Babylonian invasion in 605 BC but note that deportations followed in 597, 586 and 582 BC (52:28–30). Archaeology confirms how thorough the destruction was: many towns in Judah were destroyed in the early sixth century BC and not rebuilt for generations.
- **"Woe is me because of my hurt!" (v19).** Now Jerusalem, or Jeremiah speaking for her, cries out. The "I" is both the prophet and the nation, and Jeremiah feels the wound as his own. **"Truly this is an affliction, and I must bear it."** Readers differ on the tone. It may be the beginning of honest acceptance that the suffering is deserved (Mic 7:9, "I will bear the indignation of the LORD because I have sinned against him"), or simply resignation.
- **"My tent is destroyed, and all my cords are broken" (v20).** Jerusalem is pictured as a mother living in a tent, the way Israel's ancestors lived. The cords that held it up are snapped, and her children, who would have helped her pitch it again, are gone. This is one of Jeremiah's favorite pictures of the ruined land (4:20). Isaiah uses the reverse picture for restoration: "enlarge the place of your tent… lengthen your cords" (Isa 54:2).
- **"The shepherds are stupid and do not inquire of the LORD" (v21).** "Shepherds" means Judah's leaders, both kings and religious leaders (2:8; 23:1–2). "Stupid" is the same word used for idolaters in vv8 and 14: the leaders have become as brainless as the idols. Their great failure is prayerlessness. They never asked God what to do, so the flock is scattered.
- **"A voice, a rumor! Behold, it comes!" (v22).** The news of the army "out of the north country" (1:14–15; 4:6; 6:22) is already on the road. The cities will become "a lair of jackals," the same phrase as 9:11.
- **"I know, O LORD, that the way of man is not in himself" (v23).** Jeremiah turns from the people to God. People cannot steer their own course to a good end (Prov 16:9; 20:24). This may also mean that Babylon's march is not finally in Babylon's hands: God is directing it (Isa 10:5–7). And it is the right answer to idolatry, which is at heart an attempt to manage life without God.
- **"Correct me, O LORD, but in justice; not in your anger" (v24).** Jeremiah does not ask God to cancel the discipline. He asks for measured correction, the way a father corrects a child, not destruction. God answers this prayer later: "I will discipline you in just measure… I will not make a full end of you" (30:11; 46:28). "Justice" here (*mishpat*) means right measure, fitting the punishment to its purpose.
- **"Pour out your wrath on the nations that know you not" (v25).** This verse is nearly identical to Psalm 79:6–7, a lament over the destruction of Jerusalem. Either Jeremiah drew on a known psalm or the psalm drew on him. The prayer asks God to keep his own distinction between his children, whom he disciplines, and the brutal empires who "devoured Jacob." God did judge Babylon, as chapters 50–51 foretell.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the whole chapter as preparation for exile, a warning against "learn[ing] the way of the heathen" both for those already carried to Babylon and those left behind, and he notes the idol is "but the stock of a tree, fitter to make a gate-post of than any thing else." Guzik draws on Kidner's suggestion that idolatry tempted people because it was impressive to look at and because it meant keeping in step with the majority. He devotes some space to the Christmas tree question and concludes, as above, that the text is about idols. JFB explains "true God" in v10 as God being "truth in the reality of His essence, as opposed to the 'vanity'" of idols, and takes v11's Aramaic as a ready-made reply for exiles to give their captors. On v19 JFB at first reads "I must bear it" as "sullen impenitence," then prefers to see in it Judah's eventual acknowledgment of guilt, and the two readings show how open the line is. Guzik, following Morgan, links v23 to the idols: making a god is an attempt to direct one's own steps. JFB adds that Psalm 79's author probably repeated Jeremiah rather than the reverse, but scholars are divided on that.

#### Pointing to Christ
Idols must be carried. The living God carries his people (Isa 46:3–4). The emptiness of idols is finally answered not by an argument but by a person: "the true God and eternal life" (1 John 5:20), the Word through whom all things were made (John 1:3), who "became flesh" so that God could be seen without an image made by hands. Jeremiah prayed for correction in justice and not in anger. At the cross God's anger against sin fell on Christ, so that for those who belong to him every discipline is a Father's correction for their good (Heb 12:5–11). And the One who is the "portion of Jacob" is offered to every nation that once bowed to wood and stone, so that people can turn "from idols to serve the living and true God" (1 Thess 1:9).
