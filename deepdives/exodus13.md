*Setting:* The morning after the first Passover. Egypt's firstborn are dead, Pharaoh has ordered Israel out, and the people have marched from Rameses to Succoth with unleavened dough on their shoulders and Egyptian silver and gold in their packs (12:29–41). Chapter 13 pauses on the road to answer one question: how will Israel remember what God has just done? God claims every firstborn as his own, the Feast of Unleavened Bread is set as a yearly reminder, and fathers are told what to say when their children ask. Then the march resumes, not by the short road but the long one, with Joseph's bones in the baggage and the LORD himself going ahead in a pillar of cloud and fire.

#### Consecration of the Firstborn (vv1–2)
- **"Consecrate to me all the firstborn" (v2).** "Consecrate" (*qadash*) means to set apart as holy, to move something from ordinary use into God's possession. God has just struck Egypt's firstborn and spared Israel's. The spared ones now belong to him.
- **"Whatever is the first to open the womb… is mine."** The claim covers people and animals. It rests on God's own word at the burning bush: "Israel is my firstborn son" (4:22). The firstborn was the strength and hope of a family (Gen 49:3), so giving it to God said that the whole family's future was his.
- **Why it comes first.** These two verses open the chapter and are filled out in vv11–16. In between, Moses speaks about the feast. Both are ways of remembering the same night.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "God, who is the first and best, should have the first and best." JFB explains "sanctify" as to "set apart from a common to a sacred use." Guzik gives three reasons for the claim: Israel is God's firstborn, the firstborn was the best, and it keeps the night of redemption in memory.

#### The Feast of Unleavened Bread (vv3–16)
- **"Remember this day… out of the house of slavery" (v3).** "House of slavery" becomes a standard name for Egypt, and it opens the Ten Commandments (20:2). **"By a strong hand the LORD brought you out."** The phrase "strong hand" comes four times in this chapter (vv3, 9, 14, 16). Israel did not escape. God brought them out.
- **"Today, in the month of Abib, you are going out" (v4).** Abib means "fresh ears of grain," the spring month of new barley, about March–April.
- **"The land of the Canaanites, the Hittites, the Amorites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites" (v5).** God names the land before they have left Goshen, as he did at the bush (3:8). **"A land flowing with milk and honey"** is a picture of rich pasture for herds and wild flowers for bees, a land full of life after the stripped fields of Egypt. The feast is to be kept "when the LORD brings you" there, so it looks forward as well as back.
- **"Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread" (v6–7).** These verses repeat 12:15–20, now spoken by Moses to the people. **"No leaven shall be seen with you in all your territory."** Removing leaven became a careful household search in later Jewish practice. The point was a clean break: no trace of Egypt carried into the new life.
- **"It is because of what the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt" (v8).** The father speaks in the first person: "for *me*." Later generations who never saw Egypt were to say the same words. The Passover *seder* still quotes this verse: in every generation each person should see himself as if he came out of Egypt.
- **"As a sign on your hand and as a memorial between your eyes" (v9).** The hand stands for what we do and the eyes for what we look at and think about. Remembering the exodus should shape both. **"That the law of the LORD may be in your mouth."** The aim is talk as well as memory.
- **Phylacteries.** The word for "frontlets" in v16, *totaphot*, means bands worn on the forehead. Later Jewish practice took these verses, with Deuteronomy 6:8 and 11:18, literally and began wearing *tefillin* (phylacteries): small leather boxes holding Scripture passages, including this one, bound on the arm and forehead at prayer. The lexicon calls the command "originally figurative for perpetual remembrance," and many Christian interpreters agree. Jesus did not condemn the practice itself, only making them broad for show (Matt 23:5).
- **"You shall set apart to the LORD all that first opens the womb" (v12).** The law takes effect in the land (v11). Clean animals like lambs and calves were sacrificed (Num 18:17).
- **"Every firstborn of a donkey you shall redeem with a lamb, or… break its neck" (v13).** A donkey was "unclean," so it could not be sacrificed, but it was valuable. The owner could buy it back by offering a lamb in its place. If he would not pay, the animal had to die anyway; he could not simply keep what belonged to God. **"Redeem"** (*padah*) means to buy back by paying a price.
- **"Every firstborn of man among your sons you shall redeem."** Israel's God never accepts human sacrifice, unlike some of Canaan's gods (Lev 18:21; Deut 12:31). Firstborn sons were bought back. Later, the Levites were taken in place of Israel's firstborn (Num 3:11–13, 40–51), and a payment of five shekels was set (Num 18:16). Mary and Joseph brought Jesus to the temple "as it is written in the Law of the Lord, 'Every male who first opens the womb shall be called holy to the Lord'" (Luke 2:22–23).
- **"When in time to come your son asks you, 'What does this mean?'" (v14).** As at 12:26, God expects children to ask questions and parents to answer with the story. The answer explains the strange custom: **"the LORD killed all the firstborn in the land of Egypt… Therefore I sacrifice… but all the firstborn of my sons I redeem" (v15).** Every redeemed firstborn son was a living reminder that a death had happened in his place.
- **"Pharaoh stubbornly refused" (v15).** Literally "Pharaoh made himself hard," using the word for a hard or stiff neck (*qashah*). The same word will describe Israel itself as "stiff-necked" (32:9).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Remember it by a good token, as the most remarkable day of your lives," and teaching children the story "is a debt we owe to the honour of God, and to the benefit of our children's souls." JFB calls the sign on the hand "only a figurative mode of expression" and doubts the phylacteries go back this far. He notes that unclean animals like the donkey, "being unfit for sacrifice, were to be redeemed." Guzik reads the feast's purity as the right response to deliverance by blood: those saved by the Lamb are to walk clean. He also points out that the feast began and ended with a celebration, so purity with God is not joyless. The Tyndale notes stress that the redeemed firstborn picture all of God's people, who deserved death and are bought back at the price of the Son's life.

#### Pillars of Cloud and Fire (vv17–22)
- **"God did not lead them by way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near" (v17).** The shortest route to Canaan ran along the Mediterranean coast, a main highway later called the Way of the Sea. Egypt guarded it with a chain of forts, and Egyptian records describe these strongholds on the road to Gaza. Strictly, the Philistines settled there in force a little later, so the name may be the one familiar to later readers. Either way, it was a road lined with soldiers.
- **"Lest the people change their minds when they see war and return to Egypt."** God knows his people. They have never fought anyone, and he will not test them beyond what they can bear (1 Cor 10:13). Their fear in 14:11–12 and their longing for Egypt in Numbers 14:4 show how real this danger was. The longer way was the kinder way.
- **"Around by the way of the wilderness toward the Red Sea" (v18).** The "Red Sea" is *yam suph*, literally "Sea of Reeds." Its exact location is debated. Suggestions include one of the lakes along the line of today's Suez Canal, such as the Bitter Lakes or Lake Timsah, or the northern tip of the Gulf of Suez. The Bible uses *yam suph* for the Gulf of Aqaba too (1 Kgs 9:26), so it can name more than one body of water.
- **"Equipped for battle."** The word *chamushim* is rare. It is used of Israel's fighting men crossing the Jordan ready for war (Josh 1:14; 4:12). Some older interpreters linked it with *chamesh*, "five," and read "five in a rank," as in the KJV margin, but the lexicon says its root is unknown and gives "in battle array." Israel looked like an army, organized and ready. The Tyndale notes point out that the readiness was only outward; they had not yet seen God fight for them.
- **"Moses took the bones of Joseph with him" (v19).** Joseph died in Egypt, perhaps four centuries earlier, and made his brothers swear: **"God will surely visit you, and you shall carry up my bones with you from here"** (Gen 50:24–25). His coffin waited in Egypt as a standing promise that Israel would leave. Hebrews 11:22 counts this as faith: "By faith Joseph… gave directions concerning his bones." The bones were finally buried at Shechem (Josh 24:32). "Visit" (*paqad*) means God coming in to act for his people, the word used when he "visited" them in 4:31.
- **"They moved on from Succoth and encamped at Etham, on the edge of the wilderness" (v20).** Etham's location is unknown. "The edge of the wilderness" places it where the farmland of the Delta gives way to desert.
- **"The LORD went before them by day in a pillar of cloud… and by night in a pillar of fire" (v21).** "Pillar" (*ammud*) means something that stands, a column. The same presence is called "the angel of God" in 14:19, and it will fill the tabernacle (40:34–38). The cloud gave guidance and shade in a hot desert (Ps 105:39); the fire gave light and warmth at night. Some have pointed to the custom of caravans and armies carrying braziers of smoke and fire at their head, but this column is no human signal. God leads in person.
- **"That they might travel by day and by night."** With Egypt behind them, they could move whenever God moved.
- **"Did not depart from before the people" (v22).** The pillar stayed for forty years, even when Israel rebelled (Neh 9:19). God's guidance did not depend on their faithfulness.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the coast road "would have brought them after a few days' march to the land of the Philistines," and Israel was "not as yet fit for war, much less for war with the Philistines." He pictures them marching "like an army with banners," and notes the pillar "in the bright day appeared cloudy, and in the dark night appeared fiery." JFB reads "harnessed" as "girded, equipped for a long journey," mentions the "five in a rank" margin reading, and calls the pillar "a visible token of His presence, the Shekinah, in a majestic cloud." Guzik draws the lesson that the easy-looking road can hide dangers God sees, notes that Joseph's coffin was a silent witness to God's promise for centuries, and warns that Israel doubted even with a pillar of fire overhead.

#### Pointing to Christ
Every firstborn son in Israel was bought back, a living reminder that someone else had died so he could live. Jesus was presented in the temple under this very law (Luke 2:22–23), but he did not stay redeemed. He came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45), and he is called "the firstborn among many brothers" (Rom 8:29), whose death buys back all who belong to him. "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (1 Cor 6:19–20). And the God who went before Israel in fire and cloud, choosing the long road because he knew their weakness, came among us in person: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). Jesus says, "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness" (John 8:12).
