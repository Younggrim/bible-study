*Setting:* Psalm 94 has no title in the Hebrew text, and its author and date are unknown. The Greek translation (the Septuagint) heads it "A Psalm of David, for the fourth day of the week," and the Mishnah (about AD 200) records that the Levites sang it in the temple each Wednesday. It sits inside the group of "The LORD reigns" psalms (93–100), and at first it seems out of place: Psalm 93 proclaims God's unshakable throne, and Psalm 94 cries out because injustice is running wild. But the two belong together. If the LORD is King, then he is also "judge of the earth" (v2), and his people may ask him to act like it. The oppressors here seem to be people inside Israel who hold power and use the law to crush the weak (vv5–6, 20–21), not a foreign army. The psalm moves from a cry for justice, to a rebuke of the oppressors, to a blessing on the faithful, and then to the psalmist's own testimony that God held him up. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's stanza breaks.

#### God of Vengeance, Shine Forth (vv1–7)
- **"O LORD, God of vengeance" (v1).** The Hebrew is *El neqamot*, "God of vengeances," and the phrase is repeated for emphasis. To modern ears "vengeance" sounds like a grudge or a temper. In the Old Testament *naqam* is the just repayment a rightful ruler gives when wrong has been done and no one else will set it right. Moses' song has God say, "Vengeance is mine, and recompense" (Deut 32:35), the verse Paul quotes when he tells believers not to take revenge themselves (Rom 12:19). The psalmist does not plan revenge. He hands the case to the only Judge with the right and the power to settle it.
- **"Shine forth!" (v1).** The verb (*hophia*) is used when God appears in blazing glory: "he shone forth from Mount Paran" (Deut 33:2), "out of Zion… God shines forth" (Ps 50:2). It is a prayer for God to step out of hiddenness and show himself, as he did at Sinai.
- **"Rise up, O judge of the earth" (v2).** God is not only Israel's judge but the whole world's, the title Abraham used when he pleaded for Sodom: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25). "Rise up" pictures a judge standing to give sentence. **"Repay to the proud what they deserve"** asks for justice that fits the crime, no more and no less.
- **"How long shall the wicked exult?" (v3).** "How long" is the classic cry of lament (Ps 13:1–2; 90:13; Hab 1:2). It assumes God will act. The only question is when. Revelation gives the same cry to the martyrs under the altar (Rev 6:10).
- **"They pour out their arrogant words" (v4).** Their speech bubbles out like a spring that will not stop. **"All the evildoers boast"** may be read "act proudly" or "talk big." The wicked are first described by their mouths.
- **"They crush your people, O LORD, and afflict your heritage" (v5).** Twice the psalmist reminds God whose people these are. "Heritage" (*nachalah*) is God's own inheritance, the people he chose as his family estate (Deut 9:29; 32:9). To attack them is to trespass on his property.
- **"They kill the widow and the sojourner, and murder the fatherless" (v6).** These three groups had no male protector or family land, so they were the easiest to exploit. God's law named them again and again as under his special care (Exod 22:21–24; Deut 10:18; 24:17; 27:19), and the prophets condemned leaders who preyed on them (Isa 1:23; 10:1–2; Jer 7:6). Whether "kill" is literal murder or ruin that destroys a life, the psalm treats it as bloodshed.
- **"The LORD does not see; the God of Jacob does not perceive" (v7).** This is the root of their cruelty. They do not deny that God exists; they assume he is not watching or does not care (compare Ps 10:11; 73:11; Ezek 8:12). Calling him "the God of Jacob" may be a sneer: the God who supposedly looks after his people does not seem to be looking.
- **Commentators:** Henry writes that the persecutors "have the prayers of God's people against them," and that God's throne is "the last refuge" of the oppressed. He draws the lesson that "we must not avenge ourselves" because it is "daring presumption to usurp his prerogative." He calls such prayers "prophecies, which speak terror to all the sons of violence." JFB defines God's vengeance as "His judicial infliction of righteous punishment," and says "as justice to the weak is a sign of the best government, their oppression is a sign of the worst." Guzik makes the same distinction between personal revenge and God's just repayment, and links "shine forth" to God's glory: justice on sin is part of his glory, not a loss of control.

#### Understand, O Dullest of the People (vv8–11)
- **"Understand, O dullest of the people!" (v8).** "Dullest" (*bo'arim*, KJV "brutish") is the word Psalm 92:6 used for the "stupid man," someone with no more spiritual sense than an animal. The surprise is that the psalmist argues with them at all. He still hopes they might become wise.
- **"He who planted the ear, does he not hear? He who formed the eye, does he not see?" (v9).** This is one of the Bible's simplest and strongest arguments. Hearing and seeing come from God. The one who gave them cannot lack what he gave. The verb "planted" pictures the ear set into the head like a tree in the ground, and "formed" (*yatsar*) is the potter's word from Genesis 2:7, where God formed the man from dust. The psalmist does not say God has ears and eyes. He says God hears and sees, so the argument does not make God a body.
- **"He who disciplines the nations, does he not rebuke?" (v10).** "Disciplines" (*yasar*) can mean to instruct, to warn or to correct. The Tyndale notes point out that the same word covers all three. If God corrects whole nations, he will not let these individuals off. **"He who teaches man knowledge"** is left hanging, and v11 finishes the thought: the Teacher of all knowledge certainly knows.
- **"The LORD—knows the thoughts of man, that they are but a breath" (v11).** "Breath" (*hevel*) is the word Ecclesiastes uses for "vanity": a vapor, a puff, gone as soon as it appears. The oppressors think their plans are clever and hidden. God knows them, and knows they are nothing. Paul quotes this verse in 1 Corinthians 3:20 to deflate human cleverness: "The Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are futile." He changes "man" to "the wise," fitting his point that worldly wisdom is folly with God.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of the scoffers, "Nabal is his name and folly is with him," yet observes that they are "fairly reasoned with, for his conviction and conversion," since "while there is life there is hope." He notes that the ear "is planted in the head, as a tree in the ground," and reasons, "If all knowledge is from God, no doubt all knowledge is in God." He adds that "the same word signifies to chastise and to instruct." JFB says God's "creative power and omniscience" are the evidence of his government. Guzik calls the logic of v9 inescapable once God is accepted as Creator, and notes that the psalm does not say God has an ear, only that he hears.

#### Blessed Is the Man Whom You Discipline (vv12–15)
- **"Blessed is the man whom you discipline, O LORD" (v12).** The same verb from v10 (*yasar*) returns, but the tone changes. God's correction that threatens the wicked is, for his people, a father's training. "Blessed" (*ashrei*) is the word that opens the Psalter (Ps 1:1): truly happy, well off. Proverbs 3:11–12 and Hebrews 12:5–11 say the same: the Lord disciplines the one he loves.
- **"And whom you teach out of your law" (v13).** The discipline is not only hard circumstances but instruction from God's *torah*, his teaching. The wicked say God does not see (v7). The faithful are being taught by him all along. Henry notes that the persecutors are, without knowing it, the rod in God's hand (compare Isa 10:5–7).
- **"To give him rest from days of trouble, until a pit is dug for the wicked" (v13).** "Rest" here is quietness of heart, being calm inside while evil still has the upper hand outside. The picture of the pit is from hunting: a pit was dug to trap animals. The wicked are already, unseen, digging their own trap (Ps 7:15; 9:15).
- **"For the LORD will not forsake his people; he will not abandon his heritage" (v14).** This answers v5. The oppressors crush God's "heritage," but God will never let it go. Samuel used almost these words when Israel feared God would abandon them: "the LORD will not forsake his people, for his great name's sake" (1 Sam 12:22). Paul draws on the same promise: "God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew" (Rom 11:1–2).
- **"For justice will return to the righteous" (v15).** The Hebrew is literally "judgment will return to righteousness." Right now justice and righteousness have been pulled apart, because the courts serve the powerful. One day they will be rejoined. **"All the upright in heart will follow it"** means they will gladly line up behind justice when it returns.
- **Commentators:** Henry says "he looks above the instruments of trouble, and eyes the hand of God, which gives it another name," and that "the afflictions of the saints are fatherly chastenings, designed for their instruction." JFB says God "favors though He chastens" the faithful, and will "teach and preserve them till the prosperous wicked are overthrown," because of "His abiding love." Guzik quotes the observation that chastening without teaching makes no one blessed, and takes v14 as the unshakable assurance at the heart of the psalm.

#### The LORD Has Become My Stronghold (vv16–23)
- **"Who rises up for me against the wicked? Who stands up for me against evildoers?" (v16).** Now the voice becomes personal: "for me." He looks around for a defender, the way a person in court looks for a witness to stand up on their behalf, and finds no one. The answer comes in v17: only the LORD. JFB says these questions "imply that none other than God will help."
- **"My soul would soon have lived in the land of silence" (v17).** "Silence" (*dumah*) is a name for the grave, where no voice praises God (Ps 115:17). He was nearly dead.
- **"When I thought, 'My foot slips,' your steadfast love, O LORD, held me up" (v18).** The moment he felt himself going down, God's *hesed*, his loyal covenant love, caught him. The image recalls Psalm 73:2 ("my feet had almost stumbled… my steps had nearly slipped"), another psalm about the prosperity of the wicked. The Tyndale notes contrast his slipping foot with the world God holds firm, which "shall never be moved" (93:1).
- **"When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul" (v19).** "Cares" (*sar'appim*) are anxious, tangled thoughts, the same word David uses in Psalm 139:23: "try me and know my thoughts." It is a picture of a mind that cannot stop turning. God's "consolations" (*tanchumim*) do not just calm him. They "cheer" or "delight" his soul, the word for a child at play (Isa 11:8).
- **"Can wicked rulers be allied with you, those who frame injustice by statute?" (v20).** Literally "Can the throne of destruction be joined with you?" The oppressors hold office and use the law itself to do wrong. "Frame" (*yotser*) is the same verb as "formed" in v9: God forms the eye, and they form injustice. Isaiah condemns the same thing: "Woe to those who decree iniquitous decrees" (Isa 10:1). The question expects a firm no. God never partners with corrupt government, even when it claims his authority.
- **"They band together against the life of the righteous and condemn the innocent to death" (v21).** The language is of a court, with innocent blood condemned by legal process. Henry points to Daniel, whose enemies passed a law against prayer and then used it to throw him to the lions (Dan 6:7–16). Naboth was killed the same way, through a rigged trial (1 Kgs 21:8–13).
- **"But the LORD has become my stronghold, and my God the rock of my refuge" (v22).** "Stronghold" (*misgav*) is a high, secure place out of an enemy's reach. "Rock of my refuge" is a cliff to shelter in (Ps 18:2). **"Has become"** means this is not theory. He has found it so.
- **"He will bring back on them their iniquity" (v23).** The wicked will reap what they sowed. Their own evil will return on their heads. **"The LORD our God will wipe them out."** The verb (*tsamat*, "put an end to, silence") is said twice. The psalm that began asking God to repay (v2) ends sure that he will. Notice the shift from "my God" (v22) to "our God" (v23): the private testimony becomes the whole community's confidence again.
- **Commentators:** Henry says of v20, "Iniquity is not the better, but much the worse, for being enacted by law; nor will it excuse those that practise it to say that they did but do as they were bidden." He compares v16 with Paul before Nero, when "no man stood by him" (2 Tim 4:16). JFB reads "my thoughts" as "anxious cares," "dwelt in silence" as the grave, and "throne" as "power, rulers," and notes that the wicked are cut off "while they are engaged in evil doing." Guzik notes that the psalm ends where it began, with God's just repayment, and that the punishment fits the crime.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 94 describes a world where the powerful pervert the law and condemn the innocent, and where no one stands up for the righteous. That happened most fully at the cross. Jesus was condemned by rulers who banded together against him (Acts 4:25–27), by a trial that framed injustice under cover of law, and he was left alone as his friends fled. He did not take revenge: "when he suffered, he did not threaten, but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23). God answered by raising him, and he is now the one through whom God "will judge the world in righteousness" (Acts 17:31). The psalm's cry "How long?" will be answered when he comes. Meanwhile his people can say, as the psalmist did, that his steadfast love holds up a slipping foot, and that his consolations cheer an anxious heart (2 Cor 1:5).
