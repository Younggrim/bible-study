*Setting:* Judah, around 609–605 BC, in the early reign of King Jehoiakim, with Babylon (the Chaldeans) rising fast. In chapter 1 Habakkuk asked why God let Judah's violence go unpunished, and was stunned by the answer: God was raising up the brutal Chaldeans to judge Judah. In chapter 2 he took his stand on the watchtower and received God's reply. The vision will come at its appointed time, "the righteous shall live by his faith" (2:4), Babylon itself will fall under five woes, and "the LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before him" (2:20). Chapter 3 is what that silence turns into: a psalm. The prophet who began with "How long?" ends with a song meant for the temple choir.

#### Habakkuk's Prayer (vv1–16)
- **"A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet" (v1).** The chapter has a heading like a psalm, and it closes like one: "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments" (v19). It has three *Selah* marks too (vv3, 9, 13), a word found otherwise only in the Psalms. This is a prayer written down for Israel to sing in worship. That suggests Habakkuk may have had some link with the temple musicians, though the book never says so.
- **"According to Shigionoth" (v1).** A musical direction whose meaning is uncertain. The singular, *shiggayon*, heads Psalm 7. The lexicon links it to a root meaning "to wander" or "to reel," which may point to a wild, passionate song with quick changes of rhythm. The Greek translation simply reads it as "song." Readers do not need to know the tune to follow the words, but the hint of strong emotion fits a prayer that swings from terror to joy.
- **"O LORD, I have heard the report of you" (v2).** The "report" is the account of God's past deeds: the exodus, Sinai, the conquest, the songs Israel had sung for centuries. It may also include the news God just gave him in chapters 1–2. Either way, Habakkuk has heard enough to be afraid. **"Your work, O LORD, do I fear"** is the right response to a God who acts in history, and the same "work" God promised in 1:5, a work Habakkuk "would not believe if told."
- **"In the midst of the years revive it" (v2).** "Revive" is *chayah*, "make it live," "keep it alive." The prophet asks God to do now, in his own lifetime, what he did long ago. "In the midst of the years" means in the middle of history, not only at its start or end. Christians have long used this verse to pray for revival, and the plea fits: it asks God to act again, not for something new and strange.
- **"In wrath remember mercy" (v2).** The most famous line of the prayer. Habakkuk does not ask God to cancel the coming judgment. He has accepted it (1:12). He asks that when God's anger falls, his compassion would not be forgotten. "Wrath" here is *rogez*, a word for turmoil and agitation. It comes back as a verb in vv7 and 16: the land of Midian "did tremble," and the prophet's body "trembles." "Mercy" is *racham*, related to the word for womb, the tender love of a parent. Exodus 34:6–7 and Micah 7:18 hold the same two things together.
- **"God came from Teman, and the Holy One from Mount Paran" (v3).** Here the prayer becomes a vision of God marching to war. Teman was a city and region of Edom, south-east of Judah, and Mount Paran lay in the wilderness between Edom and Sinai. Both point to the south, the direction from which God came to his people at Sinai. Moses' blessing uses the same picture: "The LORD came from Sinai… he shone forth from Mount Paran" (Deut 33:2), and so does Deborah's song (Judg 5:4–5). The name for God here is the rare poetic *Eloah*. "Holy One" picks up 1:12, "my Holy One."
- **"His brightness was like the light; rays flashed from his hand" (v4).** "Rays" is literally "horns," the Hebrew word *qeren*. Horns were a picture of strength, and the same word is used when Moses' face "shone" after meeting God (Exod 34:29–30). The KJV's "horns coming out of his hand" led to medieval statues of Moses with horns. The sense is beams of light. **"There he veiled his power"**: even this blinding glory is only the edge of God's might. Most of it stays hidden.
- **"Before him went pestilence, and plague followed at his heels" (v5).** Plague and pestilence are pictured as soldiers in God's army, one marching in front and one behind. The plagues of Egypt and the wilderness judgments stand behind the line (Exod 9:15; Num 16:46–50). Canaanite religion knew a plague god named Resheph, which is also the Hebrew word here for "plague." The Tyndale notes point out that Habakkuk borrows such pictures to show that the forces pagans called gods are only servants of the LORD.
- **"He stood and measured the earth… the eternal mountains were scattered" (v6).** God surveys the earth like a ruler marking out his land, and the oldest, most stable things in creation give way. The "everlasting hills" sink, but **"his were the everlasting ways"**. Only God is truly ancient and unshaken.
- **"The tents of Cushan… the curtains of the land of Midian" (v7).** Cushan appears only here. The parallel with Midian suggests a desert people of the same south-eastern region, perhaps a tribe linked to Moses' Midianite or "Cushite" wife (Num 12:1). Some connect it with Cushan-rishathaim, the oppressor whom God defeated through Othniel (Judg 3:8–10). The nomads' tents shake as God passes, as the nations trembled at the exodus (Exod 15:14–16).
- **"Was your wrath against the rivers, O LORD?" (v8).** A rhetorical question. God parted the Red Sea and stopped the Jordan (Exod 14; Josh 3), but he was not angry at water. The waters were in the way of his people's rescue. Ancient Near Eastern myths told of storm gods battling Sea and River as rival gods. Habakkuk takes over the imagery to show that for Israel's God the sea is no rival, only part of creation that obeys. **"Your chariot of salvation"** makes God the true charioteer, in contrast with Pharaoh's chariots that sank (Exod 15:4).
- **"You stripped the sheath from your bow, calling for many arrows" (v9).** The ESV footnote admits that the Hebrew of this line is uncertain. Word for word it reads something like "oaths of rods of word," and scholars have counted dozens of proposed readings. The KJV has "according to the oaths of the tribes." The ESV's "calling for many arrows" fits the battle picture. **"You split the earth with rivers"** may recall water from the rock (Exod 17:6; Ps 78:15–16).
- **"The mountains saw you and writhed… the deep gave forth its voice" (v10).** Creation responds as if alive. Psalm 77:16–19, which is very close to this passage, says the same: "When the waters saw you, O God… they were afraid." The deep lifting "its hands on high" may be waves rising, or a gesture of surrender.
- **"The sun and moon stood still in their place" (v11).** A clear echo of Joshua's battle at Gibeon, when "the sun stood still, and the moon stopped" (Josh 10:12–13), and the LORD threw great hailstones on the enemy. God's "arrows" and "glittering spear" are probably lightning, which outshines sun and moon.
- **"You threshed the nations in anger" (v12).** Threshing meant beating grain to separate it from the husk. God tramples the hostile nations like grain on the threshing floor (Mic 4:13).
- **"You went out for the salvation of your people, for the salvation of your anointed" (v13).** Here is the reason for all the violence. God's war is a rescue. "Salvation" is *yesha*, from the root that gives the name *Yeshua*, Jesus. "Your anointed" (*mashiach*) could mean the king of Israel, Moses or Joshua as God's chosen leaders, or the people as a whole (the line runs parallel with "your people"). Christians have long seen it pointing on to the Messiah, the Anointed One, for whose sake God saves his people.
- **"You crushed the head of the house of the wicked, laying him bare from thigh to neck" (v13).** The picture is of a building torn down from roof to foundation, or of an enemy chief struck down and stripped. The ESV notes again that the Hebrew is uncertain. "Crushing the head" echoes the first promise of the Bible, that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent's head (Gen 3:15).
- **"You pierced with his own arrows the heads of his warriors" (v14).** The enemy's weapons are turned on himself, as when God made Israel's enemies fight one another (Judg 7:22; 2 Chr 20:23). They came "like a whirlwind to scatter me," and the prophet suddenly speaks for the whole people. They were **"rejoicing as if to devour the poor in secret"**, like a robber gloating over a helpless victim. The words recall Babylon's greed in 1:14–17.
- **"You trampled the sea with your horses" (v15).** The section closes where it began, with God's horses and the sea (v8). The march through the Red Sea is the frame for the whole vision.
- **"I hear, and my body trembles… my legs tremble beneath me" (v16).** Habakkuk returns to "I have heard" from v2. Now he describes the effect on his body: his insides churn, his lips quiver, his bones feel like they are rotting, his legs will not hold him. Seeing God's power for real is not a pleasant feeling. Isaiah (6:5), Daniel (10:8) and John (Rev 1:17) all react the same way. The verb "tremble" is *ragaz*, the root of the "wrath" in v2. The prophet asked God to remember mercy in his turmoil, and now he is in turmoil himself.
- **"Yet I will quietly wait for the day of trouble" (v16).** "Quietly wait" is *nuach*, "to rest, settle down, be at peace." The same word appears in Job 3:26 beside *rogez*: Job had "no rest" because turmoil came. Habakkuk chooses rest in spite of turmoil. He does this not because the danger has passed, but because he has seen that God will deal with Babylon, "upon people who invade us." This is what waiting for the vision looks like (2:3): not knowing when, but trusting who.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls vv3–15 a recollection of God's appearances for his people from the exodus to the conquest, and draws the lesson that past mercies are a ground for present hope. On v2 he writes, "He does not say, Remember our merit, but, Lord, remember thy own mercy." Guzik takes v2 as a prayer for revival and stresses that revival is God's work, not something people can produce. All one can do is plead for it, and plead for mercy, because the people have not earned it. Henry and JFB both connect v11 with Joshua at Gibeon, and JFB reads "the light of thine arrows" as hail mixed with lightning. On v13 JFB takes "thine anointed" as the Messiah, with Moses, Joshua and David as types of him. Henry names Joshua, and sees in him a picture of Jesus the Mediator. Guzik reads it directly of Jesus. JFB suggests Cushan "harmonizes with" Midian, perhaps one people under two names, and Henry mentions the link with Cushan-rishathaim. On v16 Henry turns the verse into a saying: "I tremble in myself, that I might rest in the day of trouble."

#### Habakkuk Rejoices in the LORD (vv17–19)
- **"Though the fig tree should not blossom… and there be no herd in the stalls" (v17).** Six losses in a row: figs, grapes, olives, grain, sheep and cattle. That was the whole economy of a farming people, the food, the oil for lamps, the wine for feasts and the animals for milk and sacrifice. Invasion brought exactly this. Armies stripped the land, cut down trees and carried off flocks (Deut 28:30–33; Joel 1:10–12). Habakkuk is not imagining a bad harvest. He is describing what Babylon will do to Judah.
- **"Yet I will rejoice in the LORD" (v18).** The turn rests on one word, "yet." Habakkuk does not pretend the losses are small. He names every one. He finds a joy that does not depend on them. Two verbs make the point strongly: "rejoice" (*alaz*, to exult) and "take joy" (*gil*, to spin around in gladness). This is not quiet resignation but delight, and its object is a person: "in the LORD… in the God of my salvation."
- **"The God of my salvation" (v18).** The word *yesha* again, from v13. God's great saving acts in the past are now personal. The God who saved "his people" and "his anointed" is "my" salvation. The book that began with "I cry to you 'Violence!' and you will not save" (1:2) ends with God as "my salvation."
- **"GOD, the Lord, is my strength" (v19).** The capital letters in the ESV mark the divine name, here *YHWH Adonai*, "the LORD, my Lord." Strength for Habakkuk is not grain in the barn but God himself.
- **"He makes my feet like the deer's; he makes me tread on my high places" (v19).** The line is almost word for word from David's song in 2 Samuel 22:34 and Psalm 18:33. A hind, a female deer, can run and leap on steep mountain paths without falling. "High places" here are not pagan shrines, as so often elsewhere, but the heights of the land, a picture of security and freedom (Deut 32:13; 33:29). The man whose legs trembled in v16 now stands on the heights. He is given the steadiness to walk through the hard country ahead, not lifted out of it.
- **"To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments" (v19).** The ESV footnote says the Hebrew is literally "my stringed instruments." This direction normally stands at the start of a psalm (Pss 4, 6, 54). Placed at the end, it makes the prophet's private struggle into the congregation's song. Judah would sing this through the siege and the exile.
- **The book's shape.** Habakkuk moves from complaint (1:2–4) to bewilderment (1:12–17) to waiting (2:1) to worship (ch 3). The circumstances never change in the book. Babylon still comes. What changes is the prophet. This is how "the righteous shall live by his faith" (2:4) works out in real life: faith takes hold of God when the fig tree is bare.
- **Commentators:** Henry puts the whole section in a phrase: "when all is gone his God is not gone." He reads v19 as strength "to run the way of his commands and outrun my troubles," and JFB repeats the thought almost word for word. JFB adds that the high places are called "mine," a sign of safety and freedom given back to God's people. Guzik insists the passage is not positive thinking that ignores loss. Habakkuk sees the ruin clearly and still finds God greater than his losses. Guzik also passes on the well-known story that Benjamin Franklin read vv17–19 aloud to a group in Paris who mocked the Bible, and they praised it as fine poetry until he told them where it came from. The story is often repeated, though it is hard to confirm. The Tyndale notes put it plainly: even if God never gave material blessing again, he would still be worthy of trust and praise.

#### Pointing to Christ
Habakkuk asked God to remember mercy in wrath, and the cross is where that prayer is answered in full. There God's judgment on sin fell, and his mercy was never more plain. The divine warrior who went out "for the salvation of your anointed" and crushed the head of the wicked is fully seen in Jesus, the Anointed One, who defeated the serpent by being crushed himself (Gen 3:15; Col 2:15) and who will come again as the warrior of Revelation 19. Paul and Hebrews both build on Habakkuk's "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Rom 1:17; Heb 10:38). Chapter 3 shows what that faith sounds like. It can lose everything else and still say, "I will take joy in the God of my salvation," because in Christ that salvation cannot be taken away.
