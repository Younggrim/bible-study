*Setting:* This is the last chapter of Isaiah and the end of God's answer (chapters 65–66) to the long prayer of 63:15–64:12, where the people pleaded among the ruins of Jerusalem and its burned temple. Chapter 65 divided God's people into two groups, rebels who chase idols and "servants" who seek him, and ended with new heavens and a new earth. Chapter 66 holds those two groups side by side one last time. The setting is most likely the years after the return from Babylon (from 538 BC), when the exiles were rebuilding and arguing about the temple, and some mixed pagan rites with temple worship. The book ends where it began, with God calling heaven and earth to hear (1:2), true and false worship (1:11–17), Zion restored (1:26–27), and rebels burning with no one to quench them (1:31).

#### The Humble and Contrite in Spirit (vv1–6)
- **"Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool" (v1).** A king's footstool was the low stool where he rested his feet. The whole earth is only that to God. Solomon said the same at the dedication of the first temple: "heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27). Jesus used this verse to forbid careless oaths: do not swear "by heaven, for it is the throne of God, or by the earth, for it is his footstool" (Matt 5:34–35).
- **"What is the house that you would build for me, and what is the place of my rest?" (v1).** This is not a ban on building the temple. The prophets Haggai and Zechariah urged the returned exiles to rebuild it (Hag 1:2–8). The point is that a building cannot hold or tame God, and a people who think it can have misunderstood him. Stephen quoted vv1–2 in his last speech, telling the Jerusalem council that "the Most High does not dwell in houses made by hands" (Acts 7:48–50), as the Tyndale notes observe. Henry sees here a hint of "the dismantling of the temple by the planting of the Christian church."
- **"All these things my hand has made" (v2).** You cannot give God a house when he made the material. David prayed the same truth: "all things come from you, and of your own have we given you" (1 Chr 29:14).
- **"But this is the one to whom I will look: he who is humble and contrite in spirit and trembles at my word" (v2).** Here is where God *does* choose to dwell. "Humble" (*ʿani*) means poor or afflicted, the same word as "the poor" who receive good news in 61:1. "Contrite" (*nekeh*) means stricken or crippled. The same word describes Mephibosheth, "crippled in his feet" (2 Sam 4:4; 9:3). Guzik draws the same picture from Motyer: one lamed by awareness of sin. The lexicon supports it. Isaiah 57:15 said God dwells "in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit." David: "a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Ps 51:17). Jesus began the Sermon on the Mount, "Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matt 5:3).
- **"And trembles at my word" (v2).** Not terror but deep reverence that takes God's word seriously and is moved by it. Ezra gathered "everyone who trembled at the words of the God of Israel" (Ezra 9:4; 10:3). JFB calls the contrite heart God's "spiritual temple."
- **"He who slaughters an ox is like one who kills a man" (v3).** Four pairs follow: a lawful sacrifice beside something monstrous. An ox offering beside murder. A lamb beside breaking a dog's neck (dogs were unclean, and some pagans sacrificed them). A grain offering beside pig's blood. Incense beside blessing an idol. The ESV adds the word "like" to make the comparison, and most translations do the same, but the Hebrew sets the phrases side by side abruptly: "slaughtering an ox, striking a man." Some take this as describing people who actually do both, offering at the temple and joining in pagan rites. Either way, the Tyndale notes say their sin made their lawful sacrifices no different from pagan ones. Isaiah opened the book the same way: "Bring no more vain offerings" (1:13). JFB: "God loathes even the sacrifices of the wicked."
- **"These have chosen their own ways… I also will choose harsh treatment for them" (vv3–4).** A pointed play on "choose." They chose their ways; God chooses their punishment. "Bring their fears upon them" means the very things they dreaded will happen. **"When I called, no one answered."** This repeats 65:12 almost word for word. Guzik links the giving-over to 2 Thessalonians 2:11, where God sends a strong delusion on those who refused the truth.
- **"Hear the word of the LORD, you who tremble at his word" (v5).** Now God turns to the humble of v2 with comfort. **"Your brothers who hate you and cast you out for my name's sake."** The faithful are being shunned by their own countrymen, people of the same nation and the same temple. Jesus warned his disciples, "They will put you out of the synagogues" (John 16:2), and "Blessed are you when people hate you and when they exclude you… on account of the Son of Man!" (Luke 6:22). JFB applies the mocking to Christ's crucifixion.
- **"'Let the LORD be glorified, that we may see your joy'" (v5).** This is sarcasm: "Go on, let your God show himself, then we'll see you smile." Compare the taunts at the cross: "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now" (Matt 27:43). **"But it is they who shall be put to shame."**
- **"The sound of an uproar from the city! A sound from the temple! The sound of the LORD" (v6).** Three times "sound" (*qol*, also "voice"). The noise is God himself at work, judging from his own temple. The Tyndale notes compare Ezekiel 9, where God's executioners begin judgment "at my sanctuary" (Ezek 9:6). Peter writes, "it is time for judgment to begin at the household of God" (1 Pet 4:17).
- **Commentators:** Henry sees vv1–4 as showing the "contempt God puts upon ceremonial services in comparison with moral duties," and as hinting the temple's end. JFB and the Tyndale notes both tie v1 to Solomon's prayer and Stephen's speech. Guzik explains "contrite" from a root meaning lamed or disabled, which the lexicon supports, and on vv3–4 warns that God may give people the delusion they insist on. JFB reads v5 of believers excluded as unclean by their own people and connects it to the Pharisees' treatment of Jesus' followers.

#### Rejoice with Jerusalem (vv7–14)
- **"Before she was in labor she gave birth; before her pain came upon her she delivered a son" (v7).** Zion is a mother again, as in 49:20–21 and 54:1 ("Sing, O barren one"). Normally labor comes first and then birth. Here the birth comes before the pain, so sudden and easy that no one can explain it. The Tyndale notes connect "a son" to the child of 9:6. Revelation 12:1–5 pictures a woman, Israel, giving birth to a male child who will rule the nations, Christ. Most read Isaiah's "son" here as the restored people, which JFB calls a "collective" image; some see a hint of the Messiah born from Israel.
- **"Shall a land be born in one day? Shall a nation be brought forth in one moment?" (v8).** Nations usually take centuries to form. JFB says Zion "starts into maturity at once." Christian readers often recall Pentecost, when three thousand were added to the church in one day (Acts 2:41). Others point to the modern state of Israel, declared on a single day in May 1948, as a possible echo; that is a reading of history, not something the text names.
- **"Shall I bring to the point of birth and not cause to bring forth?" (v9).** The answer to Hezekiah's despair years before: "children have come to the point of birth, and there is no strength to bring them forth" (37:3). God never starts what he will not finish (Phil 1:6). The Tyndale notes say the LORD promised he would surely recreate his holy nation.
- **"Rejoice with Jerusalem… all you who mourn over her" (v10).** The mourners of 61:2–3 and the prayer of 63–64 are told to rejoice. Jesus: "Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted" (Matt 5:4).
- **"That you may nurse and be satisfied from her consoling breast" (v11).** A nursing baby is fed, soothed and content all at once. Jerusalem is pictured as a mother who has plenty for all her children. Peter uses the same picture: "Like newborn infants, long for the pure spiritual milk" (1 Pet 2:2).
- **"I will extend peace to her like a river" (v12).** Peace (*shalom*) means wholeness and well-being, not just the absence of war. In 48:18 God sighed, "Oh that you had paid attention to my commandments! Then your peace would have been like a river." What Israel lost by disobedience, God now gives by grace. **"The glory of the nations like an overflowing stream."** The wealth of nations streaming to Zion (60:5, 11).
- **"You shall be carried upon her hip, and bounced upon her knees" (v12).** A homely, lovely picture: the small child on the hip, bounced on the knee. JFB notes the pronouns are unclear, so it may be Jerusalem carrying her children or the nations carrying Israel's children home (as in 49:22, 60:4).
- **"As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you" (v13).** One of the clearest places where God compares himself to a mother (see also 49:15; 42:14). God is not female, and Scripture calls him Father (63:16; 64:8). But his tenderness includes everything good in a mother's comfort. "Comfort" here is the keynote word of the book's second half: "Comfort, comfort my people" (40:1).
- **"Your bones shall flourish like the grass" (v14).** Bones stand for the whole person, especially strength and health (58:11; Prov 3:8). Grass earlier pictured how quickly human life withers (40:6–8); now it pictures fresh green life. **"The hand of the LORD shall be known to his servants, and he shall show his indignation against his enemies."** The two groups again: the same hand that comforts also judges.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads this section as God rescuing his people from oppressors, comforting the persecuted, and bringing in the Gentiles. Guzik takes the painless birth as a picture of the easy, sweeping victory God gives his people, and draws on Spurgeon for the tenderness of a mother's comfort. JFB reads the birth "beyond all expectation" of the Jewish people restored and compares the overflowing stream to the Nile's yearly flood that watered Egypt's fields.

#### Final Judgment and Glory of the Lord (vv15–24)
- **"For behold, the LORD will come in fire, and his chariots like the whirlwind" (v15).** God comes as a divine warrior in a storm of fire (Ps 18:7–15; Hab 3:8). Elijah was taken up by "chariots of fire… by a whirlwind" (2 Kings 2:11). Paul describes Christ's return as "the Lord Jesus… revealed from heaven with his mighty angels in flaming fire, inflicting vengeance" (2 Thess 1:7–8).
- **"By fire will the LORD enter into judgment, and by his sword, with all flesh" (v16).** "All flesh" means all humanity. In 40:5 "all flesh shall see" God's glory. Here "all flesh" faces his judgment, and in v23 "all flesh" worships. The Tyndale notes connect this judgment to the renewal of the earth (65:17; 2 Pet 3:10–13).
- **"Those who sanctify and purify themselves to go into the gardens" (v17).** The garden rites of 65:3 again. These people went through purification rituals, not for God but for idol worship. **"Following one in the midst"** probably means following a leader or priest in the center of the group performing the rite, or possibly an idol set in the middle. **"Eating pig's flesh and the abomination and mice."** All unclean animals under the law (Lev 11:7, 29). Some ancient cults did eat mice and pigs in ritual meals. JFB applies the verse to "all mere formalists."
- **"For I know their works and their thoughts" (v18).** God sees the secret rites and the hearts behind them. **"The time is coming to gather all nations and tongues. And they shall come and shall see my glory."** "Tongues" means languages, recalling Babel, where God scattered the nations by confusing their languages (Gen 11:7–9). Here he gathers them. At Pentecost people "from every nation under heaven" heard the gospel each "in his own language" (Acts 2:5–8).
- **"I will set a sign among them" (v19).** The sign is not explained. The Tyndale notes take it as the proclamation of God's glory itself. Others think of the deliverance that leaves "survivors," or the signal banner of 11:12 and 62:10. Many Christians see the cross, lifted up to draw all people (John 12:32), which is a Christian reading rather than the plain sense.
- **"I will send survivors to the nations" (v19).** Israelites who survive God's judgment become missionaries. Apart from Jonah's reluctant trip to Nineveh, this is one of the very few places in the Old Testament where God *sends* his people out to the nations to declare his glory, rather than waiting for the nations to come to Zion. (JFB reads it the other way: Gentiles who see the glory go home and tell others.) The first missionaries of the church were exactly that, a Jewish remnant sent to the Gentiles (Acts 1:8; 13:47).
- **"To Tarshish, Pul, and Lud, who draw the bow, to Tubal and Javan, to the coastlands far away" (v19).** The edges of the known world in every direction. Tarshish was in the far west, probably Tartessus in Spain (Jonah 1:3). Pul is unknown; many scholars correct it to Put, in Libya, which the Greek translation has. Lud was in Africa or western Asia Minor, famous for archers (Jer 46:9). Tubal was in Asia Minor (eastern Turkey), and Javan is Greece, the Ionians. Most of these names appear in the table of nations in Genesis 10. JFB gives similar identifications. Guzik passes on Bultema's link of Tubal to Russia, which few scholars hold. **"That have not heard my fame."** Paul made it his ambition "to preach the gospel, not where Christ has already been named" (Rom 15:20), and he planned to go to Spain (Rom 15:24).
- **"They shall bring all your brothers from all the nations as an offering to the LORD" (v20).** The nations bring scattered Israelites home, on every kind of transport: horses, chariots, covered litters, mules, camels. Paul uses this very idea: his "priestly service of the gospel of God, so that the offering of the Gentiles may be acceptable" (Rom 15:16). **"Just as the Israelites bring their grain offering in a clean vessel."** The returnees are a holy offering, and the carriers serve as priests bringing it.
- **"And some of them also I will take for priests and for Levites" (v21).** Who are "them"? It could be the returning Israelites, from tribes other than Levi. But most interpreters, including JFB and the Tyndale notes, take it as the Gentiles, which is the shock of the verse. Under the law only Aaron's descendants could be priests, and outsiders who came near were to die (Num 3:10). Now God takes priests from the nations. Isaiah 56:6–7 already promised foreigners joy in God's house of prayer "for all peoples." The New Testament says all believers, Jew and Gentile, are "a holy priesthood" (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6). The Tyndale notes see here the removal of the dividing line between Israel and the nations (Eph 2:14).
- **"As the new heavens and the new earth that I make shall remain before me… so shall your offspring and your name remain" (v22).** 65:17 returns. God's people will last as long as the new creation, which is forever. The Tyndale notes tie this to God's everlasting covenant with Abraham (Gen 17:7).
- **"From new moon to new moon, and from Sabbath to Sabbath, all flesh shall come to worship before me" (v23).** Isaiah began with God saying, "New moon and Sabbath… I cannot endure iniquity and solemn assembly" (1:13). The book ends with new moons and Sabbaths filled with true worship from every nation. Guzik notes the contrast. JFB takes it to imply the Sabbath remains binding; many other readers take the festival language as a picture of continual worship, as Hebrews speaks of the Sabbath rest that remains (Heb 4:9). Revelation pictures every nation worshiping before the throne (Rev 7:9; 15:4).
- **"They shall go out and look on the dead bodies of the men who have rebelled against me" (v24).** The worshipers leave the city and see the fate of the rebels outside it. "Rebelled" is *pashaʿ*, the same word as the book's opening charge: "Children have I reared… but they have rebelled against me" (1:2). Outside Jerusalem's walls lay the Valley of Hinnom, where children had been burned to Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31–32). It later became a place of refuse and fire, and its name, *Ge-Hinnom*, became Gehenna, the word Jesus used for hell.
- **"For their worm shall not die, their fire shall not be quenched" (v24).** Worms and fire both consume dead bodies. Here neither ever finishes its work. Jesus quotes this verse word for word about hell: "where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched" (Mark 9:48). JFB calls it an image of hell drawn from the Valley of Hinnom. Christians have long agreed that this describes final, irreversible judgment. Most have read it as conscious, unending punishment (Matt 25:46; Rev 14:11), while some evangelicals read the unquenchable fire as a judgment whose result, destruction, is permanent. **"An abhorrence to all flesh."** The word (*deraʾon*) appears only here and in Daniel 12:2, where some rise "to shame and everlasting contempt."
- **Why end here?** Isaiah's last word is a warning. In Jewish synagogue reading, the custom is to repeat v23 after v24 so the reading does not end on judgment. The book itself refuses to soften it. The great vision of new creation stands beside the reality of those who chose rebellion. The Tyndale notes observe that the book begins and ends with the condemnation of rebels (1:2–4), and Guzik sums up the choice as worship or the worm.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv19–24 as the church firmly established, its "constant attendance on God and triumph over its enemies," and says the end of the prophecy looks "as far forward as to the latter days, to the last day, to the days of eternity." Guzik sees partial fulfilment of vv18–21 in the church's missions and fuller fulfilment at Christ's return (Matt 24:30; Rev 5:9–10). JFB and the Tyndale notes both read the priests and Levites of v21 as Gentiles. JFB differs from most on v19, reading the Gentiles rather than Jewish survivors as those who go out. On v24 JFB adds that rejoicing in God's justice against the wicked is consistent with love for the godly.

#### Pointing to Christ
Isaiah ends with two pictures held together. In one, God dwells with the humble and contrite, comforts his people like a mother, and sends messengers to the far coastlands until every nation worships him. In the other, rebels lie outside the city under fire that does not go out. Jesus brings both into focus. He is the true temple where God meets people (John 2:19–21), and he dwells with the poor in spirit. He sent a Jewish remnant to Tarshish and beyond, and he makes priests of Gentiles. He also spoke more of this undying worm than anyone else in Scripture, because he came to rescue people from it. He suffered "outside the gate" (Heb 13:12), where the rebels lie in v24, so that rebels could become worshipers in the new heavens and new earth.
