*Setting:* Jerusalem, the day after the riot, about AD 57. The tribune Claudius Lysias still doesn't know what Paul is accused of. So he convenes the Sanhedrin, the 71-member Jewish high council, and sets his Roman-citizen prisoner in front of it. Paul once carried this council's letters to Damascus (22:5). Now he stands before it as the accused, as Jesus (Luke 22:66) and Stephen (Acts 6:12) did before him.

#### Paul Before the Council (vv1–11)
- **"Looking intently at the council" (v1).** The verb (*atenizō*) is a steady, searching gaze. Luke uses it of Stephen before this same council (6:15; 7:55). Some of these faces Paul would have known well from his years as a rising Pharisee.
- **"I have lived my life before God in all good conscience" (v1).** "Lived my life" is *politeuomai*, to live as a citizen. He means a life conducted as a loyal member of God's people. He isn't claiming sinlessness (1 Tim 1:15). He is saying that both before and after Damascus he followed God as he understood him. Even as a persecutor he thought he was serving God (26:9).
- **"The high priest Ananias commanded… to strike him on the mouth" (v2).** Ananias son of Nedebaeus was high priest about AD 47–58. Josephus remembers him as greedy and violent: his servants seized the tithes meant for ordinary priests. Jewish revolutionaries later killed him at the start of the war in AD 66. Striking a prisoner who had said nothing wrong broke the law's own rules for a fair hearing (Lev 19:15; Deut 25:1–2). Compare the officer who struck Jesus before Annas (John 18:22).
- **"God is going to strike you, you whitewashed wall!" (v3).** A whitewashed wall looks solid but is crumbling underneath. The image comes from Ezekiel 13:10–15 and Jesus' "whitewashed tombs" (Matt 23:27). Ananias is judging by the law while breaking it. The rebuke was accurate, and some read it as prophecy, since Ananias died violently.
- **"I did not know, brothers, that he was the high priest" (vv4–5).** This is one of the puzzles of Acts. The main explanations:
  - Paul, away for years, didn't recognize this high priest, who may not have been in his robes at an informal hearing.
  - Paul's eyesight was poor. This is inferred from Galatians 4:15 and 6:11 and is not certain.
  - Paul was speaking with irony: "I didn't recognize a high priest in a man who acts like that."
  - Whichever is right, Paul at once submits to Scripture, quoting Exodus 22:28: "You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people." He respects the office even when its holder is unworthy.
- **"I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees… the hope and the resurrection of the dead" (v6).** The council was split. The Sadducees, the priestly aristocracy, accepted only the five books of Moses and denied resurrection, angels and spirits (v8; Luke 20:27). The Pharisees believed all three. Paul's claim isn't a trick. The resurrection really was the center of his message, because Jesus' resurrection was the proof that the hope of Israel had arrived (26:6–8; 1 Cor 15:12–20). "Son of Pharisees" means his family were Pharisees too (Phil 3:5).
- **"The assembly was divided" (v7).** The verb (*schizō*) is the root of "schism." The court that had been united against him is now fighting itself.
- **"We find nothing wrong in this man. What if a spirit or an angel spoke to him?" (v9).** The Pharisaic scribes now defend him, echoing Gamaliel's old warning not to be found "opposing God" (5:39). They half-accept Paul's Damascus story only because it scores a point against the Sadducees.
- **"Afraid that Paul would be torn to pieces" (v10).** For the second time in two days Roman soldiers pull Paul out of a Jewish crowd. The tribune is no closer to knowing the charge.
- **"The following night the Lord stood by him and said, 'Take courage'" (v11).** This is the turning point. After a riot, a beating, chains, a near-flogging and a chaotic trial, Paul is alone in the barracks. Jesus himself comes. "Take courage" (*tharseō*) is what Jesus said to the paralytic (Matt 9:2), the woman healed of bleeding (Matt 9:22), and the disciples on the sea (Matt 14:27). **"As you have testified… in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome."** The word "must" (*dei*) is God's necessity. Paul's plan in 19:21 is now the Lord's promise. Everything from here to chapter 28 rests on this verse.
- **Commentators:** Guzik reads "good conscience" as Paul having dealt honestly with his sin, not as a claim to perfection. He agrees the rebuke was deserved, since the order violated Deuteronomy 25 and Ananias was notoriously greedy. On "I did not know," he offers both the poor-eyesight and sarcasm explanations. JFB calls the high priest's order "infamous" for a judge at a trial. It quotes Hackett that Paul's flash of anger fell short of Christ's calm when he was struck (John 18:22–23), but "the manner in which he atoned for his fault was Christ-like." Henry, on the Pharisees fighting among themselves, sees God turning Paul's enemies against each other. He reads v11 as proof that "he who has delivered does and will deliver." Guzik imagines Paul discouraged that night, feeling he had wasted two opportunities, and sees Jesus meeting him there with both comfort and a new assignment.

#### A Plot to Kill Paul (vv12–22)
- **"Bound themselves by an oath neither to eat nor drink till they had killed Paul" (v12).** The verb is *anathematizō*: they put themselves under a curse (*anathema*) if they failed. More than forty men joined (v13). Jewish teaching allowed such vows to be released when they became impossible, so they likely did not starve. Saul once made a rash fasting oath too (1 Sam 14:24).
- **"Give notice to the tribune… as though you were going to determine his case" (vv14–15).** The chief priests and elders, the nation's religious leaders, agree to lure a prisoner into an ambush. The zeal that killed Jesus is still at work.
- **"The son of Paul's sister heard of their ambush" (v16).** This is the only glimpse in the New Testament of Paul's family. Paul had a married sister living in Jerusalem, and her son was able to get into the barracks. How the boy heard, Luke doesn't say. The forty men had a scheme, but God had a nephew.
- **"Paul called one of the centurions" (v17).** Paul trusts God's promise in v11 and still acts sensibly. Faith and prudence go together.
- **"The tribune took him by the hand" (v19).** A kind gesture. It suggests the nephew was young, and it shows something good in this pagan officer.
- **"Tell no one" (v22).** The tribune believes him at once and keeps it quiet.
- **Commentators:** Henry traces the plot to Micah 2:1, men who plan evil on their beds and carry it out at morning light, and adds of the forty: "As they love cursing, so shall it come unto them." JFB notes that deliverance came only "at the last moment, when the plot was all prepared," as often happens to God's people. It reads the tribune's taking the boy by the hand as showing he "must have been quite in his boyhood." Guzik calls the conspirators' zeal "not according to knowledge" (Rom 10:2) and agrees that their vow could be released, so they probably didn't go hungry for long.

#### Paul Sent to Felix the Governor (vv23–35)
- **"Two hundred soldiers, with seventy horsemen and two hundred spearmen" (v23).** That's 470 men, nearly half the Jerusalem garrison, to protect one prisoner from forty assassins. They leave at "the third hour of the night," about 9 p.m. Jesus promised Rome, and Rome itself provides the escort.
- **Felix the governor (v24).** Antonius Felix governed Judea about AD 52–59. He was a former slave, freed by the emperor's family, who rose through his brother Pallas's influence at court. The Roman historian Tacitus said he wielded a king's power with a slave's mind. Chapter 24 shows his character.
- **The letter (vv26–30).** It is a standard official letter: sender, recipient, greeting, business. "Claudius Lysias" fits a man who took the emperor's name when he bought his citizenship (22:28), with Lysias as his Greek name. Notice how he edits events in his own favor. He "rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman citizen" (v27), when in fact he learned it only as Paul was about to be flogged. His verdict, though, matters: "charged with nothing deserving death or imprisonment" (v29). It's the first of several Roman judgments that Paul is innocent (25:25; 26:31). Some manuscripts add "Farewell" at the end, as the KJV does.
- **"By night to Antipatris" (v31).** A forced march of about 35–40 miles through hill country, to a town Herod the Great rebuilt and named for his father Antipater. Past Antipatris the country was mostly Gentile, so the foot soldiers turned back and the cavalry rode the last 25 or so miles to Caesarea (v32).
- **"What province he was from… Cilicia" (v34).** Felix checks jurisdiction. A man from Cilicia could be tried by the governor of Judea, where the alleged crime happened.
- **"Guarded in Herod's praetorium" (v35).** This was the palace Herod the Great built at Caesarea, now the governor's headquarters. Paul will spend two years here (24:27). Caesarea, on the coast, is the gateway to the sea road toward Rome.
- **Commentators:** Guzik sees Luke writing partly to show Roman officials judging Christianity fairly. Paul's case would eventually be heard in Rome, so the record of Roman verdicts mattered. JFB notes the letter's claim about summoning the accusers "was not done when he wrote" but would be done before it arrived. It reckons Antipatris "nearly forty miles from Jerusalem." Henry marvels that the same Providence that let the plot be formed made sure it "was brought to light, and so as effectually to be brought to nought."

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter turns on one sentence: "The Lord stood by him." Paul stands where Jesus stood, struck before the council, hated by the priests and plotted against at night. Unlike Jesus, he is rescued, because Jesus already went to the cross alone. The risen Lord who once asked "Why are you persecuting me?" now stands beside his servant in the dark and guarantees the road to Rome. Paul would later write that at his first defense in Rome "no one came to stand by me… But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me" (2 Tim 4:16–17).
