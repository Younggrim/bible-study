*Setting:* Philemon is Paul's shortest surviving letter, about 335 Greek words, and the most personal. Paul is in prison, most likely in Rome around AD 60–62 (Acts 28:30), though some scholars place it in an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus, about 100 miles from Colossae, and a few at Caesarea. Philemon is a well-off believer in Colossae, a small town in the Lycus valley of Asia (western Turkey today), whose house is large enough to host the church. He seems to have come to faith through Paul (v19), probably during Paul's long stay in Ephesus (Acts 19:10). His slave Onesimus has left him, possibly taking money or causing loss (v18), and has somehow ended up with Paul and become a Christian. Most think he simply ran away. Some scholars suggest instead that after offending his master he went looking for Paul as a friend who could plead for him, a step Roman jurists did not count as running away. Either way, Roman law gave a master almost unlimited power over a slave, and a runaway could be beaten, branded or worse. Now Paul is sending Onesimus home with Tychicus, carrying this letter and the letter to the Colossians together (Col 4:7–9).

#### Greeting (vv1–3)
- **"Paul, a prisoner for Christ Jesus" (v1).** In most letters Paul opens as "an apostle." Here he leaves the title out (as in Philippians and 1–2 Thessalonians) and calls himself a prisoner, the only letter that opens that way. He is about to ask a hard favor, and he comes not with rank but with chains. And he is Christ's prisoner, not Rome's: he is there because of Jesus and under his care.
- **"And Timothy our brother" (v1).** Timothy is with Paul and joins in the greeting, as in Colossians 1:1. But the letter quickly becomes Paul's own voice ("I," vv4–22).
- **"Philemon our beloved fellow worker" (v1).** Philemon is not just a convert but a partner in ministry. The word "beloved" sets the tone. Paul will appeal to love again and again (vv5, 7, 9, 16).
- **"Apphia our sister and Archippus our fellow soldier" (v2).** Apphia is most likely Philemon's wife. In a Roman household the wife ran the domestic slaves, so she had a direct stake in how Onesimus was received. Archippus may be their son. Colossians 4:17 gives him a charge to "fulfill the ministry," so he may have led the church. "Fellow soldier" pictures the Christian life as a shared campaign (Phil 2:25; 2 Tim 2:3).
- **"And the church in your house" (v2).** There were no church buildings for about two centuries. Believers met in homes like Philemon's (Rom 16:5; Col 4:15). Notice the effect: a private request about a slave is addressed to the whole house church, which would hear it read aloud. What Philemon does with Onesimus will be done in front of his brothers and sisters.
- **"Grace to you and peace" (v3).** "You" here is plural, for the whole church. Paul's usual greeting joins a Christian form of the Greek greeting (*chairein* becomes *charis*, grace) to the Hebrew *shalom*, both now gifts "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "to be a prisoner simply is no comfort nor honour"; the honor is in being Christ's prisoner. JFB: Apphia and Archippus, "if they had not belonged to his family, would not have been included." Guzik reads the missing title "apostle" as Paul choosing friendship over rank, and stresses that Apphia, as mistress of the household, would have had special reason to care about Onesimus.

#### Philemon's Love and Faith (vv4–7)
- **"I thank my God always when I remember you" (v4).** Ancient letters often followed the greeting with a thanksgiving, and Paul nearly always does (the exception is Galatians). This one is sincere, but it is also preparing the ground. Every quality Paul praises here is one he will ask Philemon to show toward Onesimus.
- **"Your love and… the faith that you have toward the Lord Jesus and for all the saints" (v5).** Paul hears of this by report, since he had never been to Colossae (Col 2:1). Faith toward the Lord, love toward the saints. "All the saints" will soon include one more: Onesimus.
- **"That the sharing of your faith may become effective" (v6).** The Greek is hard and has been translated many ways. "Sharing" is *koinōnia*, fellowship or partnership, the common life believers have in Christ, which often shows itself in generosity (Rom 15:26; 2 Cor 8:4). Many sermons take "sharing your faith" to mean evangelism, but that is not the word's usual sense. Paul is praying that Philemon's partnership in the faith will be active and fruitful, leading to "the full knowledge of every good thing that is in us for the sake of Christ." The word returns in v17, "if you consider me your partner" (*koinōnos*). Paul is already pointing at the request.
- **"The hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you" (v7).** "Hearts" is *splanchna*, literally the inner organs. Greeks located deep feeling there, as English speaks of the heart (the KJV has "bowels"). The word appears three times in this letter: Philemon has refreshed the saints' hearts (v7), Onesimus is Paul's "very heart" (v12), and Paul asks, "Refresh my heart in Christ" (v20). The logic is clear: refresh Onesimus, and you refresh Paul. "Refreshed" (*anapauō*) is the word Jesus used in "I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Love to Christ, and faith in him, are prime Christian graces," and Paul prays that "the fruits of Philemon's faith and love might be more and more conspicuous." JFB reads v6 as faith showing itself in "the imparting of it and its fruits," that is, active generosity. Guzik allows that v6 may include material giving and also draws a lesson about witness, that faith becomes effective when others see what God has done in a person. The Tyndale notes observe that v6 can be translated several ways, and that Paul is already hinting that God's goodness to Philemon should shape how he treats Onesimus.

#### Paul's Plea for Onesimus (vv8–22)
- **"Though I am bold enough in Christ to command you… yet for love's sake I prefer to appeal to you" (vv8–9).** Paul has apostolic authority and could simply give an order. He chooses not to, because forgiveness that is ordered is not quite forgiveness. He wants Philemon's response to be free (v14).
- **"I, Paul, an old man and now a prisoner also for Christ Jesus" (v9).** The word is *presbytēs*, "old man." Paul was likely in his late fifties, old by ancient standards, worn by beatings and travel. Some scholars, following Lightfoot, think Paul meant *presbeutēs*, "ambassador," because the two words were sometimes used interchangeably and Paul calls himself "an ambassador in chains" in Ephesians 6:20, written at the same time. No manuscript reads "ambassador," though, and most translations keep "old man." Either way, he appeals with age and chains, not authority.
- **"I appeal to you for my child, Onesimus" (v10).** Paul does not name him until the end of the sentence. First comes "my child," then "whose father I became in my imprisonment," and only then the name. By the time Philemon reads "Onesimus," he has already been told he is Paul's spiritual son (1 Cor 4:15; Gal 4:19). Paul led him to Christ in a prison cell.
- **"Formerly he was useless to you, but now he is indeed useful" (v11).** A pun. *Onēsimos* means "useful" or "profitable" and was a common slave name, the sort of name an owner gave hoping for a good return. He had not lived up to it. "Useless" is *achrēstos* and "useful" *euchrēstos*. Now, in Christ, he has become what his name says, "to you and to me." The same word "useful" is used of Mark in 2 Timothy 4:11, another man who once failed and was made useful again. Some suggest a second pun on *Christos*, since *chrēstos* sounded similar, but that is a guess.
- **"I am sending him back to you, sending my very heart" (v12).** Onesimus is now *splanchna*, Paul's own heart. To receive or reject him is to receive or reject Paul. Returning was a real risk for Onesimus. He went back anyway, carrying this letter, which says something about what the gospel had done in him.
- **"I would have been glad to keep him… but I preferred to do nothing without your consent" (vv13–14).** Paul would have liked Onesimus to stay and serve him "on your behalf," as Philemon's stand-in. That is a gentle hint that Philemon might send him back to Paul. But Paul will not presume. "That your goodness might not be by compulsion but of your own accord" is the same principle as cheerful giving (2 Cor 9:7). God's grace produces free responses, not forced ones.
- **"For this perhaps is why he was parted from you for a while, that you might have him back forever" (v15).** Two touches of great tact. "Was parted" is a softer way of saying "ran away," and some take the passive to hint that God was at work. The grammar does not require that, since the same form can just mean "he left." The real hint of providence is the "perhaps… that." Paul does not claim to know God's purposes for certain. He suggests that God may have used a wrong to bring about good, as Joseph said to his brothers (Gen 50:20). "For a while" is literally "for an hour," set against "forever." Philemon lost a slave for a short time and gained a brother for eternity.
- **"No longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother" (v16).** The heart of the letter. Paul does not say Onesimus ceases to be a slave in law, but he redefines everything. He is a brother "both in the flesh," in everyday human life in the household, "and in the Lord," in the church. Master and slave would now take the Lord's Supper at the same table. Paul writes elsewhere that in Christ "there is neither slave nor free" (Gal 3:28; Col 3:11) and that believing masters must see their slaves as brothers (1 Tim 6:2).
- **Philemon and slavery.** Paul does not command Philemon to free Onesimus, and pro-slavery writers in the nineteenth century used this letter to defend returning fugitives. Abolitionists read it the other way, and with better reason. A master who receives his slave "as you would receive me" (v17) and as "a beloved brother," and who is asked to do "even more than I say" (v21), cannot go on treating him as property. Many see v21 as a hint at freedom. The gospel here works from the inside, changing hearts and relationships, in ways that in time made slavery impossible to defend among Christians.
- **"If you consider me your partner, receive him as you would receive me" (v17).** "Partner" is *koinōnos*, from the *koinōnia* of v6. If Philemon and Paul share a common life in Christ, then Onesimus, Paul's heart, must be welcomed as Paul himself would be. Paul puts himself in Onesimus's place.
- **"If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account" (v18).** "Charge to my account" is *ellogaō*, a commercial term found in business papyri for entering a debt in a ledger. Its only other New Testament use is Romans 5:13, of sin not being "counted" where there is no law. It is not the same word as "counted as righteousness" in Romans 4, but the picture is closely related, and Christians have long seen the gospel in it: the innocent one takes the debt of the guilty and has it put on his own account.
- **"I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it" (v19).** Paul probably took the pen from his scribe here and wrote a formal IOU, a legally binding promise in his own handwriting (compare Col 4:18; Gal 6:11). Then a gentle reminder: "to say nothing of your owing me even your own self." Philemon owes Paul far more than Onesimus owes Philemon, his very life in Christ.
- **"Yes, brother, I want some benefit from you in the Lord" (v20).** Another pun. "Benefit" is *onaimēn*, from the same root as "Onesimus." Paul is saying, in effect, "Let me have some Onesimus-profit from you." "Refresh my heart in Christ" completes the thread from v7.
- **"Confident of your obedience… knowing that you will do even more than I say" (v21).** After all the gentle appeal, the word "obedience" appears. Paul trusts Philemon to respond as a believer under Christ. What the "even more" means is not stated. It may mean sending Onesimus back to serve Paul, or setting him free.
- **"Prepare a guest room for me" (v22).** Paul hopes to be released "through your prayers" and plans to visit. That is warm, and it also adds gentle accountability: Paul will see for himself how Onesimus has been received. He expected release from this imprisonment (Phil 1:25; 2:24), and most think it came.
- **Commentators:** Henry builds vv8–21 into a long chain of Paul's arguments and on v11 writes, "Unsanctified persons are unprofitable persons." On v15 he adds a caution: ministers "must speak warily, that kind providences of God towards sinners be not abused," that is, God bringing good out of sin does not make the sin right. On v16, "There is a spiritual brotherhood between all true believers, however distinguished in civil and outward respects." JFB calls "parted" "a softening term for 'ran away,' to mitigate Philemon's wrath," and sees v21 as a hint "at his possible manumission." On slavery JFB writes that Scripture "does not sanction slavery, but at the same time does not begin a political crusade against it." Guzik makes vv17–19 a picture of Christ taking our debt, citing Luther's well-known line from his preface to Philemon that "we are all his Onesimi, if we believe." Guzik also stresses something the others pass over: conversion did not cancel Onesimus's duty to make things right with the master he wronged.

#### Final Greetings (vv23–25)
- **"Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus" (v23).** Epaphras was a Colossian who brought the gospel to his hometown (Col 1:7; 4:12–13), probably the man through whom Philemon's household heard it. "Fellow prisoner" may mean he was arrested too, or that he chose to share Paul's confinement. In Colossians it is Aristarchus who gets that title (Col 4:10), which suggests friends took turns.
- **"Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, my fellow workers" (v24).** The same men send greetings in Colossians 4:10–14, which confirms the two letters were sent together. Mark once deserted Paul (Acts 13:13; 15:37–39) and is now a fellow worker, later "very useful to me" (2 Tim 4:11), using the same word as v11. Aristarchus of Thessalonica had shared Paul's dangers for years (Acts 19:29; 27:2). Demas would later desert Paul, "in love with this present world" (2 Tim 4:10). Luke, the doctor, stayed to the end (2 Tim 4:11).
- **"The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit" (v25).** "Your" is plural again, as in v3. The whole house church receives the blessing, and Onesimus, standing in the room, now belongs to it.
- **What happened next?** Scripture does not say. The fact that the letter was kept and copied suggests Philemon received Onesimus well. About fifty years later the martyr-bishop Ignatius wrote to the church at Ephesus praising its bishop, "Onesimus, a man of inexpressible love." Some think he was the same man, which would be a beautiful ending, but the name was common and the link is uncertain.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that in v24 "no mark of disgrace lay on" Demas yet, a sobering reminder that perseverance, not a good start, is the test. JFB suggests Epaphras may have been imprisoned "by the Roman authorities on suspicion." Guzik draws together the letter's lessons: change comes one heart at a time, forgiveness does not cancel restitution, and Christian love reorders even the most unequal relationships.

#### Pointing to Christ
Philemon is the gospel in miniature. A guilty runaway, useless to his master, finds someone who stands between them, calls him his own child, says "receive him as you would receive me," and takes his debt on himself: "charge that to my account… I will repay it." Luther said that what Christ did for us with the Father, Paul does for Onesimus with Philemon. Christ welcomed people who had run from God, made them useful and brothers, and paid what they owed. Every believer stands where Onesimus stood.
