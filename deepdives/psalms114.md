*Setting:* Psalm 114 is the second psalm of the "Egyptian Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), the songs of praise sung at Passover. With Psalm 113 it was sung before the Passover meal, and so it is very likely part of what Jesus and his disciples sang on the night of the Last Supper (Matt 26:30). It has no title or named author. Its date is uncertain. The pairing of "Judah" and "Israel" in v2 leads the Tyndale notes to suggest it was written after the kingdom split into two (1 Kings 12), and perhaps after the exile, though the pairing can also be read as two names for one people. Psalm 113 praised the God who stoops to lift the lowly. Psalm 114 tells the greatest example of it: God bringing a nation of slaves out of Egypt and into the land he promised. It is one of the most vivid poems in the Psalter. In eight verses it compresses forty years, from the Red Sea to the Jordan, and pictures sea, river and mountains reacting to God like startled animals. It is also unusual in holding back God's name until the very end. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's movement.

#### When Israel Went Out from Egypt (vv1–2)
- **"When Israel went out from Egypt" (v1).** The exodus was the defining rescue of the Old Testament, the event Israel looked back to the way Christians look back to the cross and the empty tomb. God introduced the Ten Commandments with it: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exod 20:2). The whole Passover meal is built around retelling it (Exod 12:26–27).
- **"The house of Jacob from a people of strange language" (v1).** "Strange language" translates a rare verb, *la'az*, which means to speak unintelligibly or babble. It appears only here in the Old Testament. To the Israelites, Egyptian was the noise of their oppressors, a language that was never theirs (81:5). After about four centuries in Egypt they still did not belong there. Isaiah and Jeremiah use the same kind of picture for foreign invaders whose speech Israel could not understand (Isa 28:11; Jer 5:15).
- **"Judah became his sanctuary, Israel his dominion" (v2).** "His" has no name attached. The psalm has not yet said who "he" is, and it will not until v7. That delay is deliberate and builds suspense. A "sanctuary" is a holy place where God lives, and "dominion" is the realm where he rules. The point is not only that God freed Israel from Egypt, but that he took them for himself. He promised it at Sinai: "you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exod 19:5–6). Where God lives, he also reigns.
- **"Judah… Israel" (v2).** Hebrew poetry often says one thing twice in different words, so "Judah" and "Israel" here can both mean the whole people. Judah is named first because it became the royal tribe, the tribe of David, and the home of the temple in Jerusalem (78:67–69). Jacob had promised that "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" (Gen 49:10).
- **Commentators:** JFB says the psalm "briefly and beautifully celebrates God's former care of His people," and explains that Judah is singled out "because of the destined, as well as real, prominence of that tribe." Guzik notes that Israel lived in Egypt for about four hundred years but never belonged there, and he applies that to believers, whose true home is with God. He passes on Kidner's point that the people are now defined by God and no longer by Egypt. Henry reads the exodus as a pattern of Christ's greater rescue: "When Christ comes for the salvation of his people, he redeems them from the power of sin and Satan."

#### The Sea Looked and Fled (vv3–6)
- **"The sea looked and fled; Jordan turned back" (v3).** Two crossings, forty years apart, are set side by side. The Red Sea split at the start of the journey (Exod 14:21–22), and the Jordan stopped flowing when the priests carrying the ark stepped into it at the end (Josh 3:14–17). Between them lie the whole wilderness years. The poem treats the exodus as one act, from leaving Egypt to entering the land. The sea "looked" and ran, as if it saw something terrifying. What it saw is not yet named.
- **"The mountains skipped like rams, the hills like lambs" (v4).** This most likely recalls Sinai, where "the whole mountain trembled greatly" when God came down on it (Exod 19:18). Psalm 29:6 uses the same picture for a thunderstorm making Lebanon "skip like a calf." The image is both frightening and almost playful: great mountains bounding like startled young animals. Some read it as fear, others as a kind of joy at God's arrival, and the Hebrew allows both.
- **"What ails you, O sea, that you flee?" (vv5–6).** The poet turns and questions the sea, the river and the mountains directly, as if they were people. This is a well-known device in Hebrew poetry, and it has a light, almost teasing tone: what is wrong with you, mighty sea? The questions repeat vv3–4 almost word for word, and the answer is held back for one more beat. The reader is meant to feel the answer coming before it is spoken.
- **Creation answers to its Maker.** In the ancient Near East, the sea was often pictured as a chaotic power that even the gods had to fight. Canaanite stories told of the storm god Baal battling Yam, the sea. The psalm needs no battle. The sea simply sees God and runs. The same is said elsewhere: "When the waters saw you, O God, when the waters saw you, they were afraid" (77:16). Jesus did the same thing on the Sea of Galilee, and his disciples asked, "Who then is this, that even wind and sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41).
- **Commentators:** JFB links the sea and the Jordan to Exodus 14:21 and Joshua 3:14–17, says the skipping "describes the waving of mountain forests," and observes that "the questions place the implied answers in a more striking form." Guzik draws a lesson from the questions: if sea and mountains cannot resist God, it is folly for people to try, and Spurgeon, whom he cites, adds that many still refuse to move at God's presence even though he has come near in Christ. Henry says, "There is no sea, no Jordan, so deep, so broad, but, when God's time is come, it shall be divided and driven back."

#### Tremble, O Earth, at the Presence of the Lord (vv7–8)
- **"Tremble, O earth, at the presence of the Lord" (v7).** Now the answer comes. The sea fled and the mountains leapt because of who was there. "Lord" here is *Adon*, "Lord" or "Master," not the personal name Yahweh, so the ESV prints it in ordinary type. It is the first time God is named at all. "Tremble" is *chul*, a word that often means to writhe in pain, especially the pains of childbirth (Isa 26:17). Some readers hear a picture of the earth in labor as a nation is born. The command now goes beyond the sea and the mountains to the whole earth.
- **"At the presence of the God of Jacob" (v7).** "Presence" is literally "from before the face of." The God who shakes the whole earth is called by the name of one man, Jacob, a schemer whom God chose and kept anyway. The God of the universe is also the personal God of a particular family. The psalm opened with "the house of Jacob" (v1) and closes with "the God of Jacob."
- **"Who turns the rock into a pool of water, the flint into a spring of water" (v8).** The psalm ends not with more trembling but with a gift. Twice in the wilderness God gave water from rock, at Massah and Meribah near the beginning (Exod 17:6) and at Kadesh near the end (Num 20:11). "Flint" is the hardest, driest stone in the desert, and God makes it run with water. The same power that sends the sea fleeing is used to give thirsty people a drink. Isaiah uses this memory to promise a new exodus (Isa 41:18; 43:19–20; 48:21).
- **Paul's reading of the rock.** Paul writes that Israel "drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). He sees the water from the rock as a picture of Christ, the one who gives living water (John 4:14; 7:37–38).
- **The psalm stops there.** There is no closing "Praise the LORD!" and no final lesson. Ancient Greek manuscripts and the Latin Vulgate join Psalms 114 and 115 into one psalm, which may be why. But the Hebrew text keeps them separate, and the abrupt ending works well on its own. The last picture is water flowing from stone.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes that "at the presence of" is literally "from before," and reads the line as a call to awe before God's power. Henry turns v8 to Christ: "The Son of God, the Rock of ages, gave himself to death, to open a fountain to wash away sins." He also warns, "Let sinners fear before their just and holy Judge." Guzik notes how the psalm withholds God's name until v7, citing Boice on the suspense this builds, and ties v8 back to the barren woman of Psalm 113: the God who parts seas also meets the small, personal needs of his people. He passes on Meyer's application that a hard human heart is like flint and needs the same miracle.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 114 sings of a rescue so great that the sea ran and the mountains jumped. The New Testament speaks of a greater one. At the Transfiguration, Moses and Elijah spoke with Jesus about his "departure," and the Greek word Luke uses is *exodos* (Luke 9:31). He would lead his people out of a slavery deeper than Egypt's, the slavery of sin and death. When he died, the earth shook and the rocks split (Matt 27:51), as the psalm calls the earth to tremble at the presence of the Lord. He is the Rock struck so that living water could flow to the thirsty (1 Cor 10:4; John 7:37–38). On the night before, he most likely sang this very psalm, about the God who brings his people out and makes them his dwelling place. Now, through him, his people are that sanctuary (1 Cor 3:16).
