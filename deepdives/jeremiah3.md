*Setting:* Jerusalem in the reign of Josiah (640–609 BC), early in Jeremiah's ministry. Verse 6 dates part of the chapter "in the days of King Josiah," most likely around the time of his reform in 622 BC, when the king purged idols and renewed the covenant (2 Kings 22–23). Chapter 2 laid out God's case against his unfaithful bride. Chapter 3 finishes that case (vv1–5), then turns to the northern kingdom of Israel, destroyed by Assyria a century earlier in 722 BC, as a warning to Judah. Then something surprising happens. The chapter that opens by asking whether a divorced wife can ever come home ends with God calling, "Return… I will heal your faithlessness," and his people answering, "Behold, we come to you." The key word is the Hebrew *shuv*, "turn" or "return," which appears in some form about a dozen times in the chapter.

#### Israel Forsakes the LORD (vv1–5)
- **"If a man divorces his wife… will he return to her?" (v1).** Jeremiah cites the law of Deuteronomy 24:1–4. If a divorced woman married another man, her first husband could never take her back, because it would "bring sin upon the land." The law guarded marriage from being treated as something passed back and forth. Judah's case is worse. She has not married one other husband. She has **"played the whore with many lovers."**
- **"And would you return to me?" (v1).** The Hebrew can be read two ways. Most translations, like the ESV, take it as a shocked question: after all this, do you think you can just come back? Older ones, like the KJV ("yet return again to me"), read it as an invitation. Verse 12 and verse 22 show that God does in the end call her back, so the shock and the invitation both belong to the chapter. What the law forbids, grace will do.
- **"Lift up your eyes to the bare heights" (v2).** The "bare heights" were hilltops cleared of trees, where shrines to Baal stood (2:20). **"Where have you not been ravished?"** The Hebrew verb is a crude one, and Jewish scribes marked it to be read aloud with a milder word. Jeremiah means it to shock.
- **"By the waysides you have sat awaiting lovers like an Arab in the wilderness" (v2).** Two pictures. A prostitute sat by the roadside waiting for customers, as Tamar did (Gen 38:14). And desert raiders lay in wait to ambush travelers. Judah is not seduced. She is watching for chances.
- **"Therefore the showers have been withheld" (v3).** Baal was worshiped as the god who sent rain. God answers by stopping the rain, as he had warned (Deut 11:16–17) and as he did in Elijah's time (1 Kings 17:1). The "spring rain" (*malqosh*, the "latter rain") fell in March and April and filled out the grain before harvest. Losing it meant a ruined crop.
- **"You have the forehead of a whore; you refuse to be ashamed" (v3).** The forehead stands for a hardened face that will not blush (Ezek 3:7–9). Even drought does not bring shame.
- **"My father, you are the friend of my youth" (v4).** The people use tender words. "Friend of my youth" is a phrase for a husband (Prov 2:17), and a young wife might call her older husband "father." In 2:27 they called a tree "father." Now they say it to God, and expect his anger to pass: **"Will he be angry forever?"**
- **"Behold, you have spoken, but you have done all the evil that you could" (v5).** Fine words, wicked deeds. Their prayers say the right things, but their lives do not change. That is the charge the rest of the chapter develops.
- **Commentators:** Guzik notes, with Feinberg, that scholars are divided on whether the last line of v1 is an invitation or an accusation, and cites Kidner's point that the divorce law guards against treating a spouse as something lent out and taken back. Henry reads v1 as invitation and promise together: "Return, and thou shalt be welcome." JFB calls the remarriage "unlawful" under Deuteronomy 24 and sees the "Arabian" of v2 as a desert robber who "lies in wait for travellers." JFB also notes that husband and father are "the two most endearing of ties," and that "my father" in v4 is set against the idol of wood they had called father in 2:27.

#### Faithless Israel Called to Repentance (vv6–25)
- **"In the days of King Josiah" (v6).** This is a separate message, dated to Josiah's reign. God asks Jeremiah to look back a hundred years at the northern kingdom.
- **"That faithless one, Israel" (v6).** "Faithless" is *meshuvah*, from the same root *shuv*, "turn." Israel is "Turning-away." It is almost a nickname, and it sets up the wordplay of the whole section: the one who turned away is called to turn back.
- **"She did not return, and her treacherous sister Judah saw it" (v7).** Judah is called *bagodah*, "treacherous," a word for betrayal, especially of a marriage (Mal 2:14). The two kingdoms are pictured as two sisters married to the same husband. Ezekiel tells the same story at length, with the sisters named Oholah and Oholibah (Ezek 23).
- **"I had sent her away with a decree of divorce" (v8).** The divorce was the fall of Samaria in 722 BC, when Assyria deported the northern tribes (2 Kings 17:6–23). Judah watched it happen and **"did not fear."**
- **"Committing adultery with stone and tree" (v9).** The standing stones and wooden poles of Canaanite shrines (2:27). **"She took her whoredom lightly"**: the word suggests something trivial, a sin that no longer feels like one.
- **"Not… with her whole heart, but in pretense" (v10).** This is likely a comment on Josiah's reform. The king's repentance was real (2 Kings 22:19; 23:25). The idols came down, the temple was cleaned, and the people pledged themselves to the covenant (2 Kings 23:3). But it was a reform from the top. Many went along outwardly while their hearts stayed with the old gods, and within a few years of Josiah's death idolatry was back (2 Kings 23:32, 37). The Hebrew for "pretense" is *sheqer*, "falsehood," one of Jeremiah's key words.
- **"Faithless Israel has shown herself more righteous than treacherous Judah" (v11).** A shocking comparison. Judah had more light: the temple, the Davidic king, Josiah's reform, and the warning of Israel's fall. Sinning against more light is worse (Ezek 16:51–52; Luke 12:48). "More righteous" does not mean Israel was innocent, only less guilty by comparison.
- **"Proclaim these words toward the north" (v12).** The north is where the exiles of Israel had been taken, to Assyria and beyond. Jeremiah, standing in Judah, preaches toward people who had been gone for a century. God has not forgotten them.
- **"Return, faithless Israel… I will not look on you in anger, for I am merciful" (v12).** In Hebrew, *shuvah meshuvah Yisrael*: "Turn back, Turning-away Israel." "Merciful" is *chasid*, related to *chesed*, God's loyal love. **"I will not be angry forever"** answers the people's question in v5, but notice the difference. There they assumed it. Here God promises it, on one condition.
- **"Only acknowledge your guilt" (v13).** That condition is honest confession, nothing more and nothing less. They must name three things: they rebelled, they gave themselves to foreign gods "under every green tree," and they did not obey God's voice. Leviticus 26:40–42 promised that if exiles confessed, God would remember his covenant. 1 John 1:9 makes the same promise.
- **"Return, O faithless children… for I am your master" (v14).** The wife has become children, both pictures of belonging. "I am your master" is *baʿalti*, which can also mean "I am your husband." It is a sharp pun. The people have been running after the Baals, but the real *baʿal*, the real husband and lord, is God (Hos 2:16).
- **"One from a city and two from a family" (v14).** The return will be small, a remnant gathered a few at a time. God's mercy reaches even a scattered handful. Paul speaks of "a remnant, chosen by grace" (Rom 11:5).
- **"Shepherds after my own heart, who will feed you with knowledge and understanding" (v15).** In 2:8 the shepherds rebelled. Here God promises new ones like David, the man "after his own heart" (1 Sam 13:14). The promise grows later into the promise of one Shepherd, the righteous Branch of David (23:4–6; Ezek 34:23).
- **"They shall no more say, 'The ark of the covenant of the LORD'" (v16).** A startling promise. The ark was the most sacred object in Israel, the golden chest in the Most Holy Place, where God was enthroned above the cherubim. In the coming age it will not be missed and **"shall not be made again."** The ark disappeared, probably when Babylon destroyed the temple in 586 BC, and it was never rebuilt for the second temple. Its disappearance is the subject of later legends (2 Maccabees 2:4–8), but Jeremiah says it will not matter. God's presence will be so full that the symbol is no longer needed.
- **"Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the LORD, and all nations shall gather to it" (v17).** The ark was called God's footstool or throne (1 Chr 28:2; Ps 99:1). Now the whole city becomes his throne, and the nations come, as Isaiah and Micah also saw (Isa 2:2–4; Mic 4:1–3). **"They shall no more stubbornly follow their own evil heart"**: the promise points toward the new heart of the new covenant (31:33).
- **"The house of Judah shall join the house of Israel" (v18).** The split between north and south had lasted since 930 BC. God promises reunion, both returning together "from the land of the north" (Ezek 37:15–22).
- **"How I would set you among my sons" (v19).** God speaks of what he had hoped. Daughters did not normally inherit land in Israel (Num 27:1–8 was an exception), so to give Israel a share "among my sons" was a gift beyond custom. **"I thought you would call me, My Father."** The same hope as in v4, but here it is God's longing, not the people's flattery.
- **"As a treacherous wife leaves her husband" (v20).** The two pictures, son and wife, come together. God is both father and husband, and both kinds of love have been betrayed.
- **"A voice on the bare heights is heard, the weeping and pleading of Israel's sons" (v21).** The same bare heights where they sinned (v2) now become the place of weeping. Jeremiah hears, in prophetic vision, the people beginning to grieve.
- **"Return, O faithless sons; I will heal your faithlessness" (v22).** Another wordplay: *shuvu banim shovavim… erpah meshuvotekem*, "Turn back, turning sons, I will heal your turnings." Hosea 14:4 has the same promise: "I will heal their apostasy." God does not only forgive the faithlessness. He heals it, as a disease.
- **"Behold, we come to you, for you are the LORD our God" (v22).** The people answer at last. Some, like the Tyndale notes, hear this as another insincere confession that God rejects in 4:1–2. Others read vv22–25 as the model of true repentance God wants, written for the people to say. Either way the words are right, and 4:1–2 makes clear that they must be matched by a changed life.
- **"Truly the hills are a delusion, the orgies on the mountains" (v23).** "Orgies" is literally "commotion" (ESV footnote), the noise of the rites on the high places. It was all a lie. **"Truly in the LORD our God is the salvation of Israel."** The confession sets the two "truly"s side by side.
- **"The shameful thing has devoured all for which our fathers labored" (v24).** "The shameful thing" is *boshet*, a word Israel's writers sometimes put in place of the name Baal (Ishbaal is called Ishbosheth, 2 Sam 2:8; Hos 9:10). Baal promised fertility and wealth. Instead he consumed them: flocks and herds offered to him, and children burned in sacrifice (7:31; 19:5).
- **"Let us lie down in our shame" (v25).** No excuses and no bargaining, only lying down under the shame they deserve. "We and our fathers, from our youth even to this day." That is very different from "I am innocent" (2:35) and "will he be angry forever?" (v5). Confession takes responsibility for the whole history.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws out v10: "We deceive ourselves if we think to deceive God by a feigned return to him." Guzik, citing Morgan, reads it as proof that Josiah's reforms were outward and never reached the heart. On v14 Henry says Judah was "though a treacherous wife, yet a wife, for I am married to you," and JFB notes that the Hebrew means "I am Lord," as a husband. JFB says the plural in "bill of divorce" (literally "a writing of cuttings off") signals complete separation. On v16 Henry sees the gospel's fulfilment: "in the gospel temple Christ is the ark; he is the propitiatory, or mercy-seat." JFB says the ark "disappeared at the Babylonian captivity." On v22 Henry writes, "God will heal our backslidings by his pardoning mercy, his quieting peace, and his renewing grace." Guzik reads vv22–25 as the shape of real repentance (weeping, confession, no excuses). That differs from the Tyndale notes, which treat the confession as insincere. Guzik also passes on Trapp's view that "the shameful thing" in v24 is Baal, which JFB shares.

#### Pointing to Christ
The law said a divorced, remarried wife could never go back to her first husband. God's grace goes beyond the law. He calls his faithless people home and promises to heal their faithlessness. That costly grace is what the cross makes possible. Christ is the true Shepherd after God's heart, the one who gathers a remnant "one from a city and two from a family," and the presence the ark only pictured, "the mercy seat" Paul speaks of in Romans 3:25. In him the nations gather to God's throne and Judah and Israel become one flock. The two sisters are joined with the Gentiles into one people, his bride (Eph 2:14–16; Rev 21:2).
