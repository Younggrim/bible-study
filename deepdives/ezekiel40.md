*Setting:* Babylonia, April 573 BC, twenty-five years after Ezekiel went into exile and fourteen years after Jerusalem fell. This is Ezekiel's last great vision, and it fills the rest of the book (chs 40–48). Nineteen years earlier, in his vision of chapters 8–11, God carried Ezekiel to Jerusalem to see the temple full of idols and then watched with him as God's glory left it. Now God carries him back to see a new temple, laid out with perfect order, ready for the glory to return (43:1–5). Chapters 40–42 are a guided tour, mostly measurements: walls, gates, courts, rooms and finally the temple building itself. Chapters 43–46 give the laws for its worship, and chapters 47–48 describe a river flowing from it and the land shared out around it. The measurements can feel dry, and they are hard to picture even for experts, since some technical words occur nowhere else. This study does not try to reconstruct every room. It follows the tour, explains what the main features meant to Ezekiel's first readers, and notes how Christians have understood the vision.

#### Vision of the New Temple (vv1–4)
- **"In the twenty-fifth year of our exile, at the beginning of the year, on the tenth day of the month" (v1).** If "the beginning of the year" means the spring month of Nisan, the date is the day Israel chose its Passover lambs (Exod 12:3). The tenth day of the seventh month was the Day of Atonement, and the day on which every fiftieth year the Year of Jubilee was announced (Lev 25:9–10). Some scholars note that twenty-five years is half a Jubilee cycle, the halfway point to release. Those links are suggestive, not certain. What is certain is the anchor: **"in the fourteenth year after the city was struck down."** The vision is God's answer to the ruin of 586 BC.
- **"The hand of the LORD was upon me, and he brought me to the city" (v1).** The same phrase opened the vision of chapter 8 and the valley of bones (8:1–3; 37:1). **"In visions of God"** (v2) tells us this is a vision, as in 1:1 and 8:3, not a bodily journey.
- **"A very high mountain, on which was a structure like a city to the south" (v2).** The temple mount in Jerusalem was not "very high," but in vision it is lifted up, as Isaiah and Micah foretold: "the mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established as the highest of the mountains" (Isa 2:2; Mic 4:1). Moses received the pattern for the tabernacle on a mountain (Exod 25:9, 40), and Ezekiel receives the pattern for a new sanctuary on one.
- **"A man whose appearance was like bronze, with a linen cord and a measuring reed in his hand" (v3).** He is a heavenly guide, gleaming like the living creatures of 1:7. In chapter 9 the man in linen carried a writing case to mark the faithful before the city was destroyed. This man carries builders' tools. The linen cord was for long distances, and the reed for shorter ones. Zechariah 2:1–5 and Revelation 11:1 and 21:15 also show a heavenly figure measuring God's city or temple. To measure something in the Bible is often to claim it and protect it.
- **"Look with your eyes, and hear with your ears, and set your heart upon all that I shall show you… Declare all that you see to the house of Israel" (v4).** Ezekiel is a witness, and the vision is meant for the exiles. In 43:10–11 God explains the purpose: when the people see the plan of the temple, they will be "ashamed of their iniquities."
- **How should chapters 40–48 be read?** This is one of the most debated passages in the Old Testament, and faithful readers hold several views. Some think it was a real plan for the temple to be built after the return, though the second temple and Herod's temple never matched it, and the people of Ezra's day did not try to follow it. Many dispensational interpreters take it as a literal temple to be built in a future thousand-year reign of Christ on earth, with sacrifices offered as memorials of his finished work, not as payment for sin. Many others, including most older Protestant commentators, read it as a God-given picture, set out in the architecture of a temple, of the perfect worship and holy presence of God that are fulfilled in Christ, his church and finally the new creation. Revelation 21–22 draws heavily on these chapters but says of the new Jerusalem, "I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" (Rev 21:22). Whatever view one takes, the vision's main point is plain: God's dwelling among his people will be holy, ordered and permanent.
- **Commentators:** Henry thinks the vision first assured the returning exiles that they would rebuild and worship again, but that its scale is too vast to be literal: "these things cannot be literally, but must spiritually, understood," foreshowing "the spiritual worship that should be performed in gospel times." On the measuring line he says, "The temple of God is built by line and rule." JFB agrees that some features are "so improbable physically as to preclude a purely literal interpretation," though he expects a future realization. On v3 he says such appearances of heavenly beings as men "prepared men's minds for the coming incarnation." Guzik sets out four main views and the objections to each, and adopts the dispensational one. He argues that the detailed measurements fit a real future temple and that its sacrifices would be memorials, comparable to the Lord's Supper.

#### The East Gate to the Outer Court (vv5–16)
- **"A wall all around the outside of the temple area" (v5).** The first thing measured is the boundary. The wall is one reed thick and one reed high, roughly 10 feet each way. Its purpose is stated later: "to make a separation between the holy and the common" (42:20). The priests of Ezekiel's day had failed to teach that distinction (22:26), and the old temple had been crowded in by the palace and by royal tombs (43:7–8). Here holiness is marked out clearly.
- **"Six long cubits, each being a cubit and a handbreadth in length" (v5).** An ordinary cubit, the length from elbow to fingertip, was about 18 inches (45 cm). The "long" or royal cubit added a handbreadth, making it about 20.5 inches (52 cm). So the measuring reed was about 10 feet 3 inches (3.1 m). JFB gives about 21 inches for the long cubit.
- **"Then he went into the gateway facing east, going up its steps" (v6).** The tour begins at the east gate, because the east gate is where God's glory left (10:19; 11:23) and where it will come back (43:1–4). Afterward that gate will be shut for good, because the LORD has entered by it (44:1–2).
- **The side rooms (vv7, 10, 12).** Three rooms lined each side of the gate passage. Archaeologists have found gates with this layout, six chambers along a passage, at Megiddo, Hazor and Gezer, cities fortified in Solomon's time. They were guardrooms. These are much larger, about 50 cubits long and 25 wide (v15; compare v21), around 85 by 43 feet. The new temple's gates are built like fortress gates: nothing unclean will get in.
- **"The gateway had windows all around… and on the jambs were palm trees" (v16).** The windows were probably narrow openings, wider inside than out. Palm trees were carved throughout Solomon's temple too (1 Kgs 6:29, 32). They suggest life, fruitfulness and the garden of Eden, God's first dwelling place with people.
- **Commentators:** JFB explains the long cubit as "twenty-one inches in all" and describes the windows as "latticed." Guzik notes that the tour begins where the glory departed, and that the gate's great height and size give it a tower-like appearance. The Tyndale notes compare the gates with those found at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer.

#### The Outer Court (vv17–19)
- **"Chambers and a pavement, all around the court. Thirty chambers faced the pavement" (v17).** The outer court was open to ordinary worshipers. The rooms along its wall are not explained here. They may have been for the people to eat their share of the sacrifices (46:21–24 places kitchens in the corners), or for storage and the Levites' work. Jeremiah once met the Rechabites in one of the rooms of the old temple (Jer 35:2–4).
- **"The lower pavement" (v18).** The temple rises in levels. The outer court is lower than the inner one, and the temple building is higher still. The higher one goes, the holier the space.
- **"A hundred cubits on the east side and on the north side" (v19).** From the outer gate to the inner gate is about 170 feet. Ezekiel's numbers favor round figures and multiples of 25 and 50, which gives the whole plan a sense of order and symmetry.

#### The North Gate (vv20–23)
- **"Of the same size as those of the first gate" (v21).** The north gate repeats the east gate exactly: 50 by 25 cubits, three rooms on each side, windows, palm trees. The repetition is the point. Nothing in this temple is improvised or uneven.
- **"By seven steps people would go up to it" (v22).** The outer gates are reached by seven steps. The inner gates will need eight more (v31), and the temple porch ten more (v49). Worshipers climb as they draw nearer to God.
- **"Opposite the gate on the north, as on the east, was a gate to the inner court" (v23).** Each outer gate faces a matching inner gate across the court.

#### The South Gate (vv24–27)
- **"There was a gate on the south… they had the same size as the others" (v24).** The south gate matches the east and north. There is no west gate. Behind the temple, on the west, stood a separate building (41:12), and the holiest place lay at the western end. Worshipers always approached God facing west, with their backs to the rising sun that pagans worshiped (8:16).
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that the temple had gates not only to the east but to the north and south, and sees in them an open door to all nations. Guzik observes that there was no west gate because the temple building sat toward the western side.

#### The Inner Court (vv28–43)
- **"Then he brought me to the inner court through the south gate" (v28).** The three inner gates, south, east and north, are measured in turn (vv28–37). They are the same size as the outer gates, but they face the other way: their vestibules open toward the outer court. **"Its stairway had eight steps" (vv31, 34, 37).** Seven steps for the outer court, eight for the inner, ten for the temple.
- **"A chamber… where the burnt offering was to be washed" (v38).** Burnt offerings had to be washed before they were placed on the altar (Lev 1:9).
- **"Tables… on which the burnt offering and the sin offering and the guilt offering were to be slaughtered" (vv39–41).** Eight tables for slaughtering stood at the north gate, and four of cut stone for the tools (v42). In Leviticus, burnt offerings and sin offerings were killed "on the north side of the altar" (Lev 1:11; 6:25). The three offerings listed cover devotion to God (the burnt offering), cleansing from sin (the sin offering) and making amends for wrongs (the guilt offering).
- **"Hooks, a handbreadth long, were fastened all around within" (v43).** These were probably for hanging the carcasses or the tools. The Hebrew word is rare and its meaning uncertain.
- **Sacrifices in the new temple?** This is a hard question. Hebrews says Christ "offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins" and that "there is no longer any offering for sin" (Heb 10:12, 18). Christians who read chapters 40–48 as a future literal temple usually say these sacrifices will be memorials, looking back to the cross as the Lord's Supper does. Those who read the vision as a picture say Ezekiel used the only language of worship his hearers knew, and that the sacrifices are fulfilled in Christ. The Tyndale notes observe that this temple is focused on sacrifice and purity more than any other: the problem of sin and access to God is front and center.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes that "the ascent into the inner court at each gate was by eight steps," and that "here were eight tables provided, whereon to slay the sacrifices." JFB notes that "the outer porch had only seven" steps and that the sacrifices were prepared at the north gate. JFB has no comment on vv39–42. Guzik, defending a future fulfilment, argues that the offerings often stressed consecration and purification, not only atonement.

#### Chambers for the Priests (vv44–47)
- **"Two chambers in the inner court" (v44).** The Hebrew reads "chambers for the singers," and the KJV follows it. The ESV, following the Greek translation, reads "two chambers," which fits the description of two rooms that follows. JFB also favors two rooms, following the Greek.
- **"For the priests who have charge of the temple… for the priests who have charge of the altar" (vv45–46).** The priestly work is divided. "Have charge" means "keep the watch," a guard duty.
- **"These are the sons of Zadok, who alone among the sons of Levi may come near to the LORD" (v46).** Zadok served as priest under David and became high priest under Solomon, when Abiathar supported the rebel Adonijah (1 Kgs 1:7–8; 2:35). Chapter 44 explains why only Zadok's descendants may serve at the altar: they stayed faithful when other priests led Israel into idolatry (44:10–16). The name Zadok comes from *tsadaq*, "righteous."
- **"A hundred cubits long and a hundred cubits broad, a square. And the altar was in front of the temple" (v47).** The inner court is a perfect square, about 170 feet on each side. The Most Holy Place in Solomon's temple was a perfect cube (1 Kgs 6:20), and the new Jerusalem in Revelation is a cube too (Rev 21:16). At the center of the square stands the altar.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the sons of Zadok were "lineally descended from Aaron," and that Solomon gave them the high priesthood in place of Abiathar's family. Guzik, citing Block, takes the "charge" as guard duty, and notes the meaning of Zadok's name.

#### The Vestibule of the Temple (vv48–49)
- **"Then he brought me to the vestibule of the temple" (v48).** At last the tour reaches the temple itself. The vestibule, or porch, was the entrance hall.
- **"People would go up to it by ten steps" (v49).** The Hebrew lacks "ten." The ESV follows the Greek translation. Seven, eight and ten steps make a steady climb toward God's presence.
- **"There were pillars beside the jambs, one on either side" (v49).** Solomon's temple had two great bronze pillars named Jachin ("he establishes") and Boaz ("in him is strength") at its porch (1 Kgs 7:15–22). These pillars recall them, though no names are given. The vestibule's width is twelve cubits in the Greek text, eleven in the Hebrew.
- **Commentators:** JFB notes the difference between eleven and twelve cubits and that the steps "were ten in number." Guzik, like most, sees the two pillars as a reminder of Jachin and Boaz.

#### Pointing to Christ
Ezekiel was shown a temple guarded on every side, raised up step by step, with its altar at the center of a perfect square, so that God could live among his people without being defiled by them. Jesus spoke of his own body as the temple (John 2:19–21). In him the holy God lives among people, and through his sacrifice the way into the holiest place is opened (Heb 10:19–20). The apostles call believers God's temple built on Christ (1 Cor 3:16; Eph 2:19–22). And at the end, John sees a city measured by an angel with a golden reed, where there is no temple at all, because God and the Lamb are its temple (Rev 21:15–22).
