*Setting:* "To the choirmaster. Of the Sons of Korah. According to Alamoth. A Song." *Alamoth* means "young women" or "maidens," and probably refers to a high-pitched setting, for soprano voices or high-tuned instruments (1 Chr 15:20). Psalm 46 is the first of three "songs of Zion" in a row (46–48) that celebrate God's presence in Jerusalem and his victory over the nations. No occasion is named. JFB and many others connect it with the night in 701 BC when the Assyrian king Sennacherib besieged Jerusalem in Hezekiah's reign, and "the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians. And when people arose early in the morning, behold, these were all dead bodies" (2 Kgs 19:35). That fits v5 ("when morning dawns") and vv8–9 remarkably well, but it remains a suggestion. Others think of Jehoshaphat's victory, when Korahite singers led the army (2 Chr 20:19–22). The psalm has three stanzas, the last two closed by a refrain ("The LORD of hosts is with us," vv7, 11). Martin Luther loved this psalm, and his hymn *Ein feste Burg*, "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God," is a free paraphrase of it.

#### God Is Our Refuge and Strength (vv1–3)
- **"God is our refuge and strength" (v1).** The psalm begins with God, not with the trouble. "Refuge" (*machseh*) is a shelter one runs into, like a cave from a storm. JFB: "a place of trust." "Strength" is the power to stand. God is both the place to hide and the strength to hold out.
- **"A very present help in trouble" (v1).** Literally "a help in troubles, found exceedingly," or "well proved." The idea is not just that God is nearby but that he has been found to be a help, again and again, when trouble came. Henry: "He is a Help, a present Help, a Help found." "Trouble" is literally "tight places," the straits where a person is pressed in.
- **"Therefore we will not fear" (v2).** "Therefore" is the hinge. Fear is not overcome by pretending things are fine but by knowing who God is. The rest of the stanza imagines the worst that could happen.
- **"Though the earth give way, though the mountains be moved into the heart of the sea" (v2).** In the ancient world, mountains were the most solid things there were, the pillars of the earth. The sea was the symbol of chaos, the untamed deep (Gen 1:2). The psalm pictures creation coming undone, the mountains sliding back into the chaos waters. Even then, "we will not fear." Jesus spoke of "nations in perplexity because of the roaring of the sea and the waves" before his return (Luke 21:25–26).
- **"Though its waters roar and foam, though the mountains tremble at its swelling" (v3).** "Roar" is the same verb as "turmoil" in the refrain of 42:5 and 43:5. The soul's turmoil there is matched by the sea's turmoil here. "Swelling" is literally "pride," the word for arrogance. JFB reads the raging waters as the "pride of insolent foes," and v6 will say the nations "rage" with the same verb as the sea's roar.
- **"Selah" (v3).** A pause, and a good one: picture the world shaking, and then take in the first line again.
- **Commentators:** Henry warns that those who build on shifting ground will be troubled, but "let not those be alarmed who are led to the Rock, and there find firm footing." Guzik notes that the psalm opens with God's provision rather than with the crisis, and that it is God's nearness that removes fear. JFB reads the earthquake imagery as a picture of "the most violent civil disorders," the upheaval of nations.

#### The City of God (vv4–7)
- **"There is a river whose streams make glad the city of God" (v4).** After the roaring sea, a quiet river. The contrast is deliberate. Jerusalem had no river. Babylon had the Euphrates, Egypt the Nile, Nineveh the Tigris. Jerusalem had only the Gihon spring and the waters it fed. Isaiah makes the same contrast in Hezekiah's era: Israel "refused the waters of Shiloah that go gently," so God would bring against them "the waters of the River, mighty and many, the king of Assyria" (Isa 8:6–7). Hezekiah cut a tunnel through the rock to bring Gihon's water inside the city walls before the Assyrian siege (2 Kgs 20:20; 2 Chr 32:30). The tunnel can still be walked today.
- **The river of God.** But the river here is more than water. It is God's presence and blessing flowing through his city, like the river that watered Eden (Gen 2:10). Isaiah 33:21: "there the LORD in majesty will be for us a place of broad rivers and streams." Ezekiel saw a river flowing from the temple, growing deeper, bringing life wherever it went (Ezek 47:1–12). Revelation ends with "the river of the water of life, bright as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb" (Rev 22:1). Psalm 36:8 has already spoken of "the river of your delights." JFB: the river symbolizes God's favor, flowing by channels to every part of the church.
- **"The holy habitation of the Most High" (v4).** "Most High" (*'Elyon*) is the ancient title used by Melchizedek, king of Salem, the old name of Jerusalem (Gen 14:18–20). The God of the whole earth chose to dwell in this small city.
- **"God is in the midst of her; she shall not be moved" (v5).** The mountains could be "moved" (v2), the same verb. The city will not be. Not because her walls are strong, but because God is inside them. Zephaniah: "The LORD your God is in your midst, a mighty one who will save" (Zeph 3:17).
- **"God will help her when morning dawns" (v5).** Literally "at the turning of the morning." JFB: the critical moment when night changes to day. Armies often attacked at dawn. The night of danger may be long, but help comes with the morning (30:5: "joy comes with the morning"). It was "early in the morning" that the people of Jerusalem looked out on the dead Assyrian camp (Isa 37:36), and "early on the first day of the week" that the women found the tomb empty (Mark 16:2).
- **"The nations rage, the kingdoms totter" (v6).** "Rage" is the same verb as the waters "roar" in v3. The chaos of nature in vv2–3 was a picture of the chaos of nations. "Totter" is the same verb as "moved" in vv2 and 5. The kingdoms shake; the city of God does not. Compare Psalm 2:1: "Why do the nations rage?"
- **"He utters his voice, the earth melts" (v6).** One word from God and all the noise of the nations dissolves. No battle is described. God does not need one. Amos 9:5: "the Lord GOD of hosts, he who touches the earth and it melts."
- **"The LORD of hosts is with us" (v7).** "Hosts" (*tseva'ot*) means armies, the angelic armies of heaven and every power in creation. The God who commands them all is "with us." "With us" in Hebrew is *'immanu*, the same word that forms the name Immanuel, "God with us" (Isa 7:14; 8:8, 10). Isaiah gave that name during the Assyrian crisis, and Matthew applies it to Jesus (Matt 1:23).
- **"The God of Jacob is our fortress" (v7).** "Fortress" is a high, secure place out of reach, a stronghold on a cliff (JFB: "a high place"). "The God of Jacob" is a striking title. Jacob was a schemer and a struggler, not a hero, and God was still his God (Gen 28:13–15; 32:28). The LORD of hosts is mighty; the God of Jacob is gracious to people like us. Guzik draws out both: sovereign over the whole world, and personally near.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the river as "the graces and consolations of the Holy Spirit," bringing joy to "every believer," and says "It is promised that the church shall not be moved." He applies it personally: "If God be in our hearts, by his word dwelling richly in us," we will be steadied. Guzik points out that Jerusalem had no great river, so the picture points to something greater, and he connects it with Eden, Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22. JFB reads "right early" as the turning point from night to morning.

#### Be Still, and Know That I Am God (vv8–11)
- **"Come, behold the works of the LORD" (v8).** An invitation to go and look, like walking over a battlefield the morning after. "Behold" means to look closely, to gaze.
- **"How he has brought desolations on the earth" (v8).** The desolations are on God's enemies. JFB: God has destroyed the enemies. This is the aftermath of God's judgment, not random disaster.
- **"He makes wars cease to the end of the earth" (v9).** The goal of God's judgment is peace. Isaiah and Micah look to the day when nations "shall beat their swords into plowshares… neither shall they learn war anymore" (Isa 2:4; Mic 4:3).
- **"He breaks the bow and shatters the spear; he burns the chariots with fire" (v9).** After a battle, the victors would gather the enemy's weapons and burn them so they could not be used again (Josh 11:6, 9; Ezek 39:9–10). The bow, the spear and the chariot were the main weapons of ancient armies. The chariot was the tank of its day, the pride of Egypt and Assyria (20:7). God disarms the world.
- **"Be still, and know that I am God" (v10).** Now God himself speaks. This line is often read as a gentle call to quiet devotion, and it can rightly be used that way. But in context it is first a command to the raging nations (v6). "Be still" is the verb *raphah*, which means to let drop, relax, let go. The lexicon notes it is used of letting the hand drop, as when God told the destroying angel, "It is enough; now stay your hand" (2 Sam 24:16, the same verb). The sense here is "Stop! Drop your weapons! Cease fighting and acknowledge who I am." JFB: a command to stop opposing God and troubling his people. Guzik, following Kidner and Boice, reads it as a rebuke to the restless world.
- **For God's people too.** The same word speaks to anxious believers. The people of God are not called to take up arms in this psalm. They are called to stop striving and trust. Moses said at the Red Sea: "The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent" (Exod 14:14). Isaiah: "In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength" (Isa 30:15).
- **"I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth!" (v10).** The nations that raged will one day honor God. This is not only a threat but a promise. The next psalm calls them to "clap your hands" and shout to God (47:1).
- **"The LORD of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our fortress. Selah" (v11).** The refrain closes the psalm. Some scholars think it once stood after v3 as well, since the first stanza lacks it, but there is no manuscript evidence for that. Guzik passes on John Wesley's dying words, "The best of all is, God is with us," which echo this refrain.
- **Commentators:** Henry: "Come and see the effects of desolating judgments, and stand in awe of God," and "in silent submission let us worship and trust in our almighty Sovereign." He closes with Paul's question: "If God be for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31). Guzik insists that v10 is first a command to the hostile world to stop fighting God, not mainly a call to quiet meditation, and the lexicon's sense of *raphah* ("let drop, relax") supports that. Guzik also passes on Trapp's report that Luther, in his darkest times, would say, "Come, let us sing the forty-sixth psalm."

#### Pointing to Christ
The refrain of Psalm 46 is the gospel in two words: *'immanu*, "with us." Isaiah took up the same word for the child who would be born, Immanuel, and Matthew says that child is Jesus (Matt 1:23). In him the God who is "in the midst" of his city came and lived among his people (John 1:14). He spoke to a raging sea, "Peace! Be still!" (Mark 4:39), and it obeyed. He promised the river of the Spirit flowing out of all who believe in him (John 7:37–39). And his last words in Matthew are the refrain of this psalm made personal: "Behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20). The mountains may fall into the sea. The city of God, built on him, "shall not be moved."
