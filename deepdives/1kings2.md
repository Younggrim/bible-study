*Setting:* About 970 BC, in Jerusalem. Solomon has just been anointed king at the Gihon spring while his half-brother Adonijah's rival coronation feast collapsed (ch 1). David is still alive but dying, and Solomon is young. David later calls him "young and inexperienced" (1 Chr 22:5; 29:1), and Solomon calls himself "a little child" (3:7). Most estimates put him around twenty. The throne is his, but the men who backed Adonijah are still powerful: Joab, who has commanded the army for some forty years, and Abiathar the priest. There are old grudges too, from the days of Absalom's revolt. The chapter covers David's last words and death, and then a hard sequence in which Solomon secures his throne. Its last line is its summary: "So the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon" (v46). Readers have long wrestled with this chapter. Is it justice or political revenge? The writer presents each death as deserved, but he does not hide how ruthless it looks.

#### David's Instructions to Solomon (vv1–9)
- **"I am about to go the way of all the earth" (v2).** A gentle phrase for death, the road everyone walks. Joshua used the same words at the end of his life (Josh 23:14). JFB calls it "a beautiful and impressive periphrasis for death."
- **"Be strong, and show yourself a man" (v2).** These are the words Moses and God spoke to Joshua as he took over from Moses (Deut 31:7; Josh 1:6–9). David hands on the leadership the way Moses did. "Show yourself a man" is not about toughness for its own sake. The Tyndale notes point out that the Greek Old Testament uses the same words Paul uses in 1 Corinthians 16:13, "act like men, be strong," and that the dying Polycarp was said to have heard the same call from heaven. Henry: "Be strong and show thyself a man, though in years but a child."
- **"Keep the charge of the LORD your God… as it is written in the Law of Moses" (v3).** The real strength David has in mind is obedience. The four words, "statutes, commandments, rules, testimonies," are the vocabulary of Deuteronomy (Deut 6:1–2; 8:11), and the king was required to keep his own copy of the law and read it all his life (Deut 17:18–20). The promise, "that you may prosper," echoes Joshua 1:7–8.
- **"If your sons pay close attention to their way… you shall not lack a man on the throne of Israel" (v4).** God's covenant with David (2 Sam 7:12–16) promised a lasting dynasty. Here its conditional side shows: individual kings would enjoy the blessing only if they walked faithfully. This tension runs through the whole of Kings. God repeats it to Solomon (9:4–7), and the book ends with the line of David in exile. Yet God never abandons the promise (Ps 89:30–37), and it finds its answer in the one Son of David who walked perfectly.
- **"You also know what Joab the son of Zeruiah did to me" (v5).** Joab had been David's nephew (1 Chr 2:16), his army chief and his enforcer for decades. He murdered Abner after David had made peace with him (2 Sam 3:27) and murdered Amasa, his own cousin, with a kiss of greeting and a hidden sword (2 Sam 20:9–10). He killed Absalom against David's direct order (2 Sam 18:5, 14) and helped arrange Uriah's death (2 Sam 11:14–17). "Avenging in time of peace for blood that had been shed in war" means he killed men in peacetime as if it were battle. "The blood of war on the belt around his waist and on the sandals on his feet" is a vivid picture: Amasa's blood ran down onto Joab's own belt and feet (2 Sam 20:8–10).
- **"Do not let his gray head go down to Sheol in peace" (v6).** Why didn't David punish Joab himself? He admitted he felt too weak against the sons of Zeruiah (2 Sam 3:39). Unpunished bloodshed was thought to bring guilt on the land and the king's house (Num 35:33; Deut 19:13), and David wants it cleared. JFB says Joab's popularity with the army meant any move against him needed "great prudence and deliberation." "Act according to your wisdom" leaves the how and when to Solomon.
- **"Deal loyally with the sons of Barzillai the Gileadite" (v7).** "Deal loyally" is *chesed*, covenant kindness. Barzillai, a wealthy old man from Gilead east of the Jordan, fed David's exhausted followers when they fled from Absalom (2 Sam 17:27–29; 19:31–39). Eating at the king's table meant a lifelong place in the royal household and its support, as Mephibosheth had (2 Sam 9:7). David's last instructions are not only about punishment but about gratitude.
- **"Shimei the son of Gera… who cursed me with a grievous curse" (v8).** Shimei was a relative of Saul's who threw stones and dust at David as he fled Jerusalem, calling him a "man of blood" (2 Sam 16:5–13). When David returned victorious, Shimei came groveling with a thousand Benjaminites, and David swore not to kill him (2 Sam 19:16–23). Cursing the king was a serious crime under the law (Exod 22:28).
- **"Do not hold him guiltless… you shall bring his gray head down with blood to Sheol" (v9).** David keeps his own oath while leaving Shimei to Solomon's judgment. Readers disagree about this. Some see a bitter old man settling scores from his deathbed. Others see a king doing his duty to remove a real threat: Shimei was a leader in Benjamin, Saul's tribe, where resentment of David's house still smoldered (2 Sam 20:1). The text records it without comment.
- **Commentators:** Henry defends David's instructions as justice, not grudge: "Magistrates are the avengers of the blood of those they have the charge of," and the charge about Shimei "proceeded not from personal revenge, but a prudent zeal for the honour of the government." JFB agrees that David spoke "in the character of a king and magistrate." Guzik calls Joab a complex man, fiercely loyal yet ruthless and often disobedient. He doubts the old theory that Joab blackmailed David over Uriah. On Shimei he says it was right for David to keep his word while still seeing that justice was done. He links v3 to Matthew 6:33: obedience first, and the rest follows.

#### The Death of David (vv10–12)
- **"David slept with his fathers and was buried in the city of David" (v10).** "Slept with his fathers" is the standard phrase in Kings for a king's death, a gentle way of saying he died and joined his ancestors. The City of David was the old Jebusite fortress on the ridge south of the later temple. JFB notes that burial was not normally allowed inside a city, Jerusalem being the exception for the royal family. On Pentecost, Peter said, "his tomb is with us to this day" (Acts 2:29). Its location is unknown now; the traditional "Tomb of David" on today's Mount Zion is a medieval identification.
- **"He reigned seven years in Hebron and thirty-three years in Jerusalem" (v11).** Forty years in round numbers (2 Sam 5:4–5 gives seven years and six months in Hebron). The greatest king in Israel's history is summed up in a sentence. Henry: "It is well there is another life after this, for death stains all the glory of this, and lays it in the dust."
- **"So Solomon sat on the throne of David his father, and his kingdom was firmly established" (v12).** The verse sounds like the end of the story, but the rest of the chapter shows what "firmly established" will mean. The same idea returns at v46, framing everything in between.

#### Solomon's Reign Established (vv13–46)
- **"Do you come peacefully?" "Peacefully" (v13).** The word is *shalom*, the root of Solomon's own name (*Shelomoh*). Bathsheba is wary of Adonijah, and with reason.
- **"You know that the kingdom was mine… However, the kingdom has turned about… for it was his from the LORD" (v15).** Adonijah's words sound humble but leak resentment. He claims all Israel wanted him, which ch 1 does not support. The Tyndale notes observe that "the kingdom was mine" shows he had not really made peace with the outcome.
- **"Give me Abishag the Shunammite as my wife" (v17).** This was far more than a love match. In the ancient Near East, taking a dead king's wife or concubine was a claim to his throne. Absalom did it publicly with David's concubines (2 Sam 16:21–22), Abner's affair with Saul's concubine was taken as a bid for power (2 Sam 3:7), and God gave Saul's wives into David's keeping (2 Sam 12:8). Abishag had been part of David's household (1:4). Adonijah's request was a quiet first step toward the crown.
- **"The king rose to meet her and bowed down to her… she sat on his right" (v19).** The queen mother (*gebirah*, "great lady") was an honored office in Judah. Kings names the king's mother for almost every king of Judah (14:21; 15:2). Sitting at the right hand was the place of highest honor (Ps 110:1). The scene contrasts with ch 1, when Bathsheba bowed before David.
- **"I have one small request" (v20).** Did Bathsheba see the danger? Some think she was naive and trusted Adonijah's humble tone (JFB). Others, including Guzik, think she knew exactly what it meant and passed it on so that Solomon would see Adonijah for what he was; he reads "small request" as gentle irony. The text does not say.
- **"Ask for him the kingdom also, for he is my older brother" (v22).** Solomon sees it at once, and he names Abiathar and Joab as the men behind it. His mercy in 1:52 had a condition, "if wickedness is found in him, he shall die," and Solomon judges that it has been.
- **"Adonijah shall be put to death today" (v24).** "God do so to me and more also" (v23) is a solemn oath formula, calling God's judgment on oneself. Benaiah, now the king's enforcer, carries out the sentence. JFB defends it as no cruelty, since the request was treason by the customs of the time. Many modern readers find it harsh, and the writer does not soften it.
- **"Go to Anathoth, to your estate, for you deserve death" (v26).** Anathoth was a priestly town a few miles northeast of Jerusalem (Josh 21:18). It was later the hometown of the prophet Jeremiah, himself from a priestly family there (Jer 1:1), possibly Abiathar's descendants. Solomon spares Abiathar for his long loyalty: he carried the ark and shared David's hardships, from the massacre at Nob (1 Sam 22:20–23) to the flight from Absalom (2 Sam 15:24–29).
- **"Thus fulfilling the word of the LORD that he had spoken concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh" (v27).** About a century earlier God had told Eli that his family would lose the priesthood because of his sons' corruption (1 Sam 2:27–36; 3:11–14). Abiathar was Eli's descendant. With Zadok, of the line of Eleazar son of Aaron (1 Chr 6:3–8), the high priesthood passed to the line God promised Phinehas (Num 25:13). The author shows that Solomon's decision, whatever his motives, worked out a word God had spoken long before.
- **"Joab fled to the tent of the LORD and caught hold of the horns of the altar" (v28).** "Although he had not supported Absalom" is a fair note. Joab stayed loyal through that revolt, but not this one. He copies Adonijah's move from 1:50. But the law said that a deliberate murderer should be taken even from God's altar (Exod 21:14).
- **"No, I will die here" (v30).** Joab may have hoped that Solomon would not dare shed blood at the altar. Solomon says do as he says (v31). Benaiah kills him where he stands. JFB notes that God's own law authorized this.
- **"Take away from me and from my father's house the guilt for the blood that Joab shed without cause" (v31).** This is the key to Solomon's reasoning. Innocent blood left unpunished stained the land and the king (Deut 19:10–13; 21:1–9). Abner and Amasa were "more righteous and better than himself" (v32). David had already called down their blood on Joab's house (2 Sam 3:28–29), as JFB notes.
- **"For David and for his descendants… there shall be peace from the LORD forevermore" (v33).** Again *shalom*. Solomon, "the man of peace," secures peace by removing the men of blood.
- **"Buried in his own house in the wilderness" (v34).** Even Joab is given burial at his family estate, probably near Bethlehem in the Judean wilderness (2 Sam 2:32). Benaiah takes his place over the army, and Zadok replaces Abiathar (v35).
- **"Build yourself a house in Jerusalem and dwell there… on the day you go out and cross the brook Kidron, know for certain that you shall die" (vv36–37).** Shimei's home was Bahurim, just east of Jerusalem over the Mount of Olives, toward Benjamin. Crossing the Kidron Valley eastward meant going home to his tribe, where he might stir up trouble. House arrest kept him under watch. The Tyndale notes say this was its purpose. Shimei agrees and swears to keep it (v42).
- **"Two of Shimei's servants ran away to Achish… king of Gath" (v39).** Gath was a Philistine city about 30 miles west, where David himself once fled (1 Sam 21:10; 27:2), and this Achish may be a descendant of that king or the same man grown old. Shimei went west, not east over the Kidron, but the order covered "any place whatever" (v42), and he knew it.
- **"You know in your own heart all the harm that you did to David my father" (v44).** Solomon gives two charges: breaking his oath "to the LORD" (v43) and his old crime against the king. "The LORD will bring back your harm on your own head." Benaiah strikes once more.
- **"So the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon" (v46).** The phrase that framed this section at v12 closes it. Every threat named in David's charge has been dealt with.
- **How should this be read?** Christians differ. Some read Solomon as a just ruler carrying out his father's charge and the law's demands. Others see political purging covered with religious language, a first warning of the self-serving king Solomon would later become (ch 11). The writer seems to allow both readings. He presents each man as guilty by his own deeds, while showing that the throne of David is secured with blood, as most thrones are.
- **Commentators:** Henry notes the irony that Solomon "was to be a prince of peace" yet "began his reign with some remarkable acts of justice," and treats each death as deserved. JFB agrees, stressing that the altar gave no protection to a murderer and that Shimei died for perjury as well as his old treason. Guzik, quoting Spurgeon, sees Joab as a man who ignored God's altar all his life and grabbed it only in danger, a warning against trusting religious acts without real faith. He also passes on Trapp's application, to deal as firmly with one's own besetting sins as Solomon did with his rebels. The Tyndale notes add that Zadok's appointment restored the high priesthood to the line of Eleazar.

#### Pointing to Christ
David's charge, "If your sons pay close attention to their way… you shall not lack a man on the throne," hangs over all of Kings, and no son of David met it. Solomon began well and ended badly. The throne in this chapter is secured by the sword, with each rival removed. Jesus, the true Son of David, kept the charge of the LORD perfectly. His kingdom is established another way: not by shedding the blood of his enemies, but by shedding his own for them. He took the guilt of innocent blood not off his own house but onto himself, "making peace by the blood of his cross" (Col 1:20). Joab ran to the altar and found no refuge, but those who run to Christ find that his blood "speaks a better word than the blood of Abel" (Heb 12:24), and he says "whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (John 6:37).
