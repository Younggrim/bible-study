*Setting:* Paul, writing to Timothy in Ephesus in the early 60s AD, has dealt with the false teachers (ch 1) and with prayer and conduct in worship (ch 2). Now he turns to leadership. The false teaching in Ephesus seems to have come partly from within the leadership itself, just as Paul warned the Ephesian elders it would (Acts 20:30). So the church needs leaders of proven character, and the list of qualifications, read aloud to the whole congregation, would also show plainly who did not measure up. Titus 1:5–9 gives a close parallel list for the churches of Crete. The chapter ends with one of the earliest Christian hymns.

#### Qualifications for Overseers (vv1–7)
- **"The saying is trustworthy" (v1).** The third of these formula sayings (see 1:15). Some think it refers back to 2:15, but most take it with what follows. Either way, Paul is affirming something the churches already said.
- **"If anyone aspires to the office of overseer" (v1).** "Overseer" is *episkopos*, literally "one who watches over," from which comes "bishop" (KJV) and "episcopal." In the Greek world it was a general word for a supervisor or manager. In the New Testament the same leaders are called overseers and elders (*presbyteroi*): Paul calls the Ephesian elders "overseers" in Acts 20:17, 28, and Titus 1:5–7 uses the two words for one office. They are also told to "shepherd" (pastor) the flock (Acts 20:28; 1 Pet 5:1–2). By the early second century, Ignatius of Antioch describes a single bishop over each city's elders. Episcopal, Presbyterian and congregational churches each read the New Testament evidence in their own way, but nearly all agree that here overseer and elder are the same.
- **"He desires a noble task" (v1).** Two different words for desire, both strong: to reach out for something and to set the heart on it. Paul calls the office "a noble work," not a noble title. Wanting to serve this way is good, as long as it is the work and not the status that draws a person.
- **"Above reproach" (v2).** This heads the list and sums it up: nothing in his life that an accuser could rightly seize on. It doesn't mean sinless. It means no open, ongoing fault that would discredit him and the gospel.
- **"The husband of one wife" (v2).** Literally "a one-woman man." This is debated. The main readings: (1) faithful to his one wife, not a womanizer or adulterer; (2) no polygamy; (3) no remarriage after divorce; (4) no second marriage at all, even after a wife's death, which was an honoured ideal in Roman culture and the view of several early church fathers. Most modern interpreters take the first view as the heart of it: the stress is on marital faithfulness. The phrase assumes a married man as the normal case but does not require marriage, since Paul himself was single (1 Cor 7:7–8).
- **"Sober-minded, self-controlled, respectable" (v2).** Clear-headed and alert; *sōphrōn*, sensible and restrained (the same root as "self-control" for women in 2:9 and 2:15); orderly and well-behaved.
- **"Hospitable" (v2).** Literally "a lover of strangers." Churches met in homes, travelling believers depended on fellow Christians for lodging, and inns had a bad reputation. An elder's home had to be open.
- **"Able to teach" (v2).** The one skill on the list, and the one that most separates overseers from deacons. In a church troubled by false teachers, leaders must know the truth well enough to teach it and correct error (Titus 1:9).
- **"Not a drunkard, not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome, not a lover of money" (v3).** A man in control of himself, who isn't a brawler, doesn't pick fights and isn't in it for the money. The false teachers were doing exactly that: they treated godliness as "a means of gain" (6:5).
- **"He must manage his own household well" (v4).** A Greco-Roman household was a small community: spouse, children, often servants or slaves, sometimes relatives. Running it well with "dignity" was a recognised test of character. His children's respect for him shows how he leads.
- **"How will he care for God's church?" (v5).** The church is God's household (v15). Notice the verb: "care for," the same word used of the Good Samaritan caring for the wounded man (Luke 10:34–35). Paul's picture of leading is caring, not ruling.
- **"Not a recent convert" (v6).** The word is *neophytos*, "newly planted," the root of our "neophyte." The church at Ephesus was about ten years old and had mature believers to choose from. (In Crete, a younger church, the requirement is not listed.) A man raised too quickly may become "puffed up," literally wrapped in smoke, blinded by his own importance.
- **"The condemnation of the devil" (v6).** This can mean the condemnation the devil fell under because of his pride, or the condemnation the devil (the accuser) brings against a fallen leader. The parallel in v7, "a snare of the devil," leans toward the second, but both make sense, and pride is the danger either way.
- **"Well thought of by outsiders" (v7).** The church's reputation in Ephesus matters. A leader with a bad name in town gives the gospel a bad name.
- **Commentators:** JFB reads "desire" as meaning to "stretch one's self forward to grasp." Henry praises the desire when it comes from love for Christ and for people, and calls it a man seeking to be "employed in a good work." Guzik stresses that the list is about character, not gifting, training or natural leadership, and that the different titles (overseer, elder, pastor) describe one office. On "one wife," JFB argues that since polygamy was already forbidden to all believers, Paul must mean that a leader should not remarry, while Guzik takes the "one-woman man" reading. Henry thinks it excluded both polygamy and divorce followed by remarriage. On the novice, Henry warns that pride "is a sin that turned angels into devils," and Guzik picks up the "newly planted" picture: roots need time.

#### Qualifications for Deacons (vv8–13)
- **"Deacons likewise" (v8).** *Diakonos* means servant, assistant or agent, and the word is used of any kind of service. Only here and in Philippians 1:1 (and perhaps Romans 16:1) does it clearly name a church office. Many trace the office back to the seven chosen to serve tables in Acts 6:1–6, though Luke doesn't call them deacons. Deacons are not said to teach or lead. Their work was practical service, such as care for the poor and the church's material needs.
- **"Not double-tongued" (v8).** Literally "two-worded," saying one thing to one person and another to the next. People who visit many homes on church business are especially tempted this way.
- **"Not addicted to much wine, not greedy for dishonest gain" (v8).** If deacons handled the church's money, greed was a real danger (compare Judas, John 12:6).
- **"Hold the mystery of the faith with a clear conscience" (v9).** In Paul, a "mystery" is not a puzzle but a truth once hidden and now revealed by God (Rom 16:25–26). The false teachers had lost their consciences (1:19; 4:2). Deacons must hold the true faith with nothing to hide.
- **"Let them also be tested first" (v10).** "Also" implies overseers were tested too. This probably means a period of observation, not a formal exam. Character shows over time.
- **"Their wives likewise" (v11).** The Greek simply says "women" (*gynaikas*); there's no "their." The word can mean women or wives. Some read the wives of deacons, since vv8–10 and 12 speak of male deacons and the verse sits in the middle. Others read women deacons, pointing out that "likewise" introduces a new group as in v8, that no such rule is given for overseers' wives, and that Phoebe is called a *diakonos* of the church at Cenchreae (Rom 16:1). Around AD 112 the Roman governor Pliny wrote of questioning two Christian slave women "who were called deaconesses" (*ministrae*). The ESV footnote gives "wives likewise" or "women likewise."
- **"Not slanderers" (v11).** The word is *diabolos*, the same word used as a name for the devil, the slanderer. Gossip does the devil's work.
- **"Husband of one wife, managing their children and their own households well" (v12).** The same standards as for overseers (vv2, 4). Serving in the church starts with faithfulness at home.
- **"A good standing… and also great confidence in the faith" (v13).** Faithful deacons gain respect in the church and before God, and boldness (*parrēsia*) in their faith. Paul gives no hint that deacon is a lesser calling.
- **Commentators:** Henry describes deacons' work as being "to distribute the charity of the church," following Acts 6, and Guzik also takes Acts 6 as the background while insisting the office is no less important than the overseer's. On v11 JFB reads "the women" as deaconesses, pointing to Phoebe, and says the Greek marks a new class of people. Guzik leaves both readings open, and the Tyndale notes lean slightly toward wives. Guzik notes the "slanderers" word is literally "devils," which the lexicon supports: *diabolos* means a slanderer, and it is the devil's own title.

#### The Mystery of Godliness (vv14–16)
- **"I hope to come to you soon… if I delay" (vv14–15).** These verses state the letter's purpose. Paul expects to come but isn't sure, so he puts his instructions in writing. They are for Timothy, and for the whole church listening.
- **"How one ought to behave in the household of God" (v15).** Not just how Timothy should behave, but how anyone in God's house should. "Household of God" ties the whole letter together. Overseers and deacons manage their households (vv4–5, 12) because the church itself is a family under God the Father.
- **"The church of the living God" (v15).** In a city dominated by Artemis's lifeless statue and her temple, the church is the dwelling of the living God (2 Cor 6:16).
- **"A pillar and buttress of the truth" (v15).** Two building words. The temple of Artemis, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world, was famous for its 127 columns. The church doesn't invent the truth; it holds it up and makes it visible, like a pillar raising a public notice, and holds it firm like a foundation. This was a call to the church at Ephesus to stand firm against false teaching.
- **"Great indeed, we confess, is the mystery of godliness" (v16).** "We confess" (or "by common confession") introduces something the churches said or sang together. The ESV prints what follows as poetry. It is almost certainly an early Christian hymn or creed: six lines, each a verb with a phrase, in balanced pairs.
- **"He was manifested in the flesh" (v16).** The KJV reads "God was manifest in the flesh." In the earliest manuscripts the reading is "who" (*hos*), and the ESV renders this "He." In Greek capital letters, "God" was often abbreviated ΘΣ with a line over it, very close to ΟΣ ("who"), and a scribe could easily turn one into the other. Most scholars think "who" is original. Either way the line teaches that Christ existed before he "was manifested" in human flesh (John 1:14). No doctrine hangs on the variant.
- **The hymn's pattern (v16).** The lines move in pairs, swinging between earth and heaven: *flesh / Spirit*, *angels / nations*, *world / glory*. "Vindicated by the Spirit" most likely means his resurrection proved him right, declaring him the Son of God (Rom 1:4). "Seen by angels" points to his exaltation before heavenly powers (1 Pet 3:22). "Proclaimed among the nations… believed on in the world" is the church's mission, already happening. "Taken up in glory" is the ascension (Acts 1:9–11).
- **Why it's here.** Against the false teachers' myths and rules, Paul sets the real "mystery": not a secret for insiders, but a person. True godliness grows from knowing him.
- **Commentators:** JFB says the "pillar and ground" phrase is "evidently predicated of the Church, not of 'the mystery of godliness,'" and that the church supports the truth while Christ is its ground in the highest sense. Henry adds a sharp warning: when a church "ceases to be the pillar and ground of truth," believers may rightly leave it. Guzik stresses the church doesn't create the truth but displays it. On v16 JFB notes that "the oldest manuscripts and versions read" "He who" rather than "God," agreeing with modern editions, and sets out the paired contrasts. Henry sums it up: "The mystery of godliness is Christ." Guzik reads the six lines as a short summary of the whole gospel story.

#### Pointing to Christ
The lists for overseers and deacons can feel like a measuring stick that no one meets. But each line describes Christ himself: the faithful husband of his bride, the gentle one who did not quarrel, the host who welcomes strangers to his table, the servant (*diakonos*) who came "not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45), the shepherd who cares for God's household. Leaders in his church are to look like him. And the chapter's last word is not about leaders at all, but about him: manifested in the flesh, vindicated, proclaimed, believed and taken up in glory. The church holds up the truth, and the truth is a person.
