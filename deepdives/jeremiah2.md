*Setting:* Jerusalem, probably early in Jeremiah's ministry, in the reign of Josiah (around 627–620 BC), before or during the king's reform. Chapter 1 called the young prophet and promised that disaster would come "out of the north." Chapter 2 is his first great sermon, and it explains why. It is built like a court case. God is the wronged husband and the judge, Judah is the accused, and the heavens are called as witnesses. The charges run through Israel's whole history: a nation God rescued and married has left him for idols and foreign alliances, and now denies it. Chapters 2 and 3 belong together. The ESV heading at 2:1 runs to 3:5.

#### Israel Forsakes the LORD (vv1–37)
- **"Go and proclaim in the hearing of Jerusalem" (v2).** Jeremiah's home was Anathoth, a few miles away. His first task is to take the message into the capital, to the temple courts and the gates where crowds gathered. "Thus says the LORD" was the formula of a royal herald. The words are the king's, not the messenger's.
- **"I remember the devotion of your youth, your love as a bride" (v2).** "Devotion" is *chesed*, the loyal love that keeps covenant. The lexicon lists this verse as one of the rare places where *chesed* means Israel's love for God, not God's for Israel. The picture is the exodus as a honeymoon. At Sinai Israel said "All that the LORD has spoken we will do" (Exod 19:8), and God took her as his bride (Ezek 16:8). Hosea used the same picture a century earlier (Hos 2:14–15). The honest record of the wilderness years includes the golden calf, but God chooses to remember the first love.
- **"In a land not sown" (v2).** The wilderness had no farms. Israel followed God where there was nothing to live on except what he provided: manna, water from the rock, the pillar of cloud.
- **"Israel was holy to the LORD, the firstfruits of his harvest" (v3).** The first grain of every harvest belonged to God and could not be eaten by ordinary people (Lev 22:10; 23:10). Israel was God's firstfruits among the nations, the first part of a harvest that would one day include them all. So **"all who ate of it incurred guilt"**: anyone who attacked Israel was robbing God, and suffered for it, as Amalek and others did.
- **"What wrong did your fathers find in me?" (v5).** God speaks like a husband asking what he did to deserve being left. The answer is nothing. Micah 6:3 asks the same: "O my people, what have I done to you?"
- **"Went after worthlessness, and became worthless" (v5).** A play on one Hebrew word, *hevel*, "breath, vapor," the same word that runs through Ecclesiastes ("vanity of vanities"). They chased vapor and became vapor. People become like what they worship (Ps 115:8). 2 Kings 17:15 says the same of the northern kingdom.
- **"They did not say, 'Where is the LORD who brought us up from the land of Egypt?'" (v6).** The sin started with forgetting. The wilderness is described in six grim phrases: deserts, pits, drought, deep darkness, a land no one crosses, where no one lives. God brought them through all of it, and they stopped asking where he was.
- **"I brought you into a plentiful land… But when you came in, you defiled my land" (v7).** The Hebrew for "plentiful land" is literally "a land of the Carmel," a garden land. The land belonged to God ("my land," "my heritage," Lev 25:23), and they filled it with idols.
- **Four kinds of leaders fail (v8).** The **priests** did not seek God. **"Those who handle the law"**, the teachers who taught it, "did not know me." The **shepherds**, a common Near Eastern word for kings and rulers (ESV footnote), rebelled. The **prophets** "prophesied by Baal." Baal was the Canaanite storm and fertility god, believed to send rain and harvest. **"Things that do not profit"** may be a sound-play on Baal (*baʿal* and *yoʿilu*). Every part of the leadership has failed, so the whole nation is lost.
- **"Therefore I still contend with you" (v9).** "Contend" is *riv*, a legal case or a quarrel. The prophets often present God's complaint as a lawsuit (Isa 3:13; Hos 4:1; Mic 6:1–2). It reaches to "your children's children," because the sin is passed down.
- **"Cross to the coasts of Cyprus… send to Kedar" (v10).** Cyprus (Hebrew *Kittim*) was the far west, out in the Mediterranean. Kedar was an Arab tribe descended from Ishmael, out in the eastern desert (Gen 25:13). Search the world from west to east, God says, and find a nation that has done this.
- **"Has a nation changed its gods, even though they are no gods?" (v11).** Pagan nations stayed loyal to their gods, though those gods were nothing. Israel had the one true God and traded him away. **"Their glory"** is God himself, the glory that lived among them (Ps 106:20 says the same of the golden calf). Paul uses this language in Romans 1:23: they "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images."
- **"Be appalled, O heavens" (v12).** Heaven and earth were the witnesses when the covenant was made (Deut 30:19; 32:1), so they are called to witness its breaking. The three verbs are strong: shudder, bristle with horror, be laid waste.
- **"They have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters" (v13).** The most famous verse of the chapter. "Living water" is running water from a spring, fresh and constant. In a dry land it was the most valuable thing a town could have.
- **"Hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water" (v13).** A cistern was a pit cut into limestone and lined with plaster to catch rainwater in the winter months. It took hard work to dig, the water went stale, and if the plaster cracked the water drained away. The two evils are a matter of exchange: leaving free, living water, and laboring to build something that leaks. Jesus takes up this picture when he offers "living water" (John 4:10–14; 7:37–38).
- **"Is Israel a slave? Is he a homeborn servant?" (v14).** A "homeborn servant" was born into slavery in the master's house. Israel was God's son (Exod 4:22), so why is he being carried off like a slave? The answer comes in v17.
- **"The lions have roared against him" (v15).** Lions picture invading armies, especially Assyria, which had wasted the northern kingdom in 722 BC (Nah 2:11–12 uses the same image).
- **"The men of Memphis and Tahpanhes have shaved the crown of your head" (v16).** Memphis was the old capital of Lower Egypt, and Tahpanhes a fortress city on the eastern edge of the Nile Delta. Shaving a captive's head was a sign of humiliation. If Jeremiah preached this early, it may refer to Egypt's growing power over Judah. Egypt killed Josiah at Megiddo in 609 BC and made Judah a vassal (2 Kings 23:29–35), and the line may have been sharpened when the sermon was written down later. Jeremiah himself would end his life in Tahpanhes (43:7).
- **"Have you not brought this upon yourself?" (v17).** The disasters are not bad luck. They are the result of leaving God "when he led you in the way."
- **"What do you gain by going to Egypt to drink the waters of the Nile?" (v18).** "Nile" is *Shihor* (ESV footnote), and "Euphrates" is just "the River." Judah's politicians swung between Egypt and Assyria (later Babylon) for protection. God connects it to v13: they keep looking for water from foreign rivers when the fountain is at home.
- **"Your evil will chastise you" (v19).** Sin carries its own punishment. Their apostasy will be their teacher, and the lesson is that it is "evil and bitter" to forsake God.
- **"Long ago I broke your yoke… but you said, 'I will not serve'" (v20).** God broke Egypt's yoke at the exodus (Lev 26:13). Freed slaves should have served their rescuer. Instead they refused. A small textual note: the written Hebrew text can be read "I will not transgress," but the traditional reading and the ancient versions have "I will not serve," which the ESV follows.
- **"On every high hill and under every green tree" (v20).** Canaanite worship took place at open-air shrines on hilltops and in groves, with wooden poles for the goddess Asherah and standing stones for Baal (Deut 12:2). Fertility rites there included ritual sex, so the picture of Israel bowing "like a whore" is both a metaphor for unfaithfulness and a literal description.
- **"I planted you a choice vine, wholly of pure seed" (v21).** "Choice vine" is *soreq*, a fine red grape (Isa 5:2). Israel as God's vine is a familiar picture (Ps 80:8–16; Isa 5:1–7). God planted the best stock, and it turned into a wild vine with useless fruit. Jesus calls himself "the true vine" (John 15:1).
- **"Though you wash yourself with lye and use much soap" (v22).** "Lye" is natron, a mineral soda, and "soap" was an alkali made from plant ash. The strongest cleaners of the day cannot remove this stain. Only God can (Isa 1:18; Ps 51:7).
- **"How can you say, 'I am not unclean, I have not gone after the Baals'?" (v23).** The people deny the charge. Many probably mixed Baal practices into what they still called the worship of the LORD. Inscriptions from about a century earlier, found at Kuntillet Ajrud in the Sinai and elsewhere, mention "the LORD and his Asherah." **"Look at your way in the valley"** probably means the Valley of Hinnom just south of Jerusalem, where children were burned in sacrifice (7:31).
- **"A restless young camel… a wild donkey… in her heat sniffing the wind" (vv23–24).** Two coarse pictures of animals in season. The young she-camel runs around without direction. The wild donkey in heat hunts for a mate, and no one can hold her back. Males do not need to chase her: "in her month they will find her." Judah is not lured into idolatry. She goes looking for it.
- **"Keep your feet from going unshod" (v25).** Stop running after lovers until your sandals wear out and your throat is parched. But the answer is: **"It is hopeless, for I have loved foreigners."** This is the voice of addiction. The people know it is wrong and say they cannot stop.
- **"As a thief is shamed when caught" (v26).** The thief is only sorry because he was caught. Every class of leader is named again: kings, officials, priests, prophets.
- **"Who say to a tree, 'You are my father,' and to a stone, 'You gave me birth'" (v27).** The tree is the wooden Asherah pole and the stone the standing pillar. With biting irony Jeremiah swaps the usual genders, so that the wood (usually the goddess's symbol) is called "father" and the stone "mother." The people turn their backs to God, but when trouble comes, they turn round and cry, **"Arise and save us!"**
- **"Where are your gods that you made for yourself?" (v28).** If they are real, let them save you. **"As many as your cities are your gods"**: every town had its own shrine and local god. The same line returns in 11:13.
- **"In vain have I struck your children; they took no correction" (v30).** God's discipline had not worked. Worse, **"your own sword devoured your prophets."** Manasseh "shed very much innocent blood" (2 Kings 21:16), and Jewish tradition holds that Isaiah was among his victims. Later King Jehoiakim killed the prophet Uriah (26:20–23).
- **"Have I been a wilderness to Israel, or a land of thick darkness?" (v31).** The words echo v6. God brought them through the wilderness. He was never one himself. Yet they say, **"We are free, we will come no more to you."** They think leaving God is freedom.
- **"Can a virgin forget her ornaments, or a bride her attire?" (v32).** A bride would never forget her wedding jewelry or sash. Israel has forgotten her husband himself, "days without number."
- **"How well you direct your course to seek love!" (v33).** Bitter sarcasm. Judah is so skilled at chasing lovers that she could teach "wicked women" a thing or two.
- **"On your skirts is found the lifeblood of the guiltless poor" (v34).** Idolatry and injustice go together. The law allowed a householder to kill a burglar caught breaking in at night (Exod 22:2). These poor people were not burglars, and they were killed anyway.
- **"You say, 'I am innocent'… I will bring you to judgment for saying, 'I have not sinned'" (v35).** The worst sin in the chapter is the denial. God's judgment falls not only because of what they did but because they refuse to admit it (1 John 1:8–10).
- **"You shall be put to shame by Egypt as you were put to shame by Assyria" (v36).** Ahaz had paid Assyria for help, and it only brought Assyria's yoke (2 Kings 16:7–9). Turning to Egypt will end the same way.
- **"With your hands on your head" (v37).** The gesture of grief and disgrace, as Tamar did after Amnon's crime (2 Sam 13:19). **"The LORD has rejected those in whom you trust."** Judah's allies cannot save, because God is against the alliance.
- **Commentators:** Henry takes v2 as Israel's early zeal toward God: "God remembers the kindness of our youth and the love of our espousals." JFB disagrees and reads it as God's kindness to Israel, since Israel's early record was mostly rebellion. The lexicon sides with Henry, listing this verse as one of the few where *chesed* means Israel's devotion to God. On v5 Henry asks, "Have you, upon trial, found God a hard master?" All three dwell on v13. Guzik calls the cisterns both disloyal and foolish, and cites Morgan's point that stored water goes stale. Henry describes a cistern that "cracks and cleaves in hot weather," and JFB notes that cisterns were valued where wells were scarce. On v11 Henry says, "Idolaters change God's glory into shame and so they do their own." JFB identifies natron as the cleaner in v22 and the Valley of Hinnom as the "valley" in v23. Guzik draws on Harrison for the *soreq* vine and the bride's sash, and on Thompson for the tree-and-stone line as satire. On v32 Henry turns the bride's ornaments into a warning: "It is sad that any should be more in love with their fine clothes than with their God."

#### Pointing to Christ
Jeremiah's sermon describes the heart of all sin: leaving the fountain of living water for cisterns that leak, then insisting "I have not sinned." Jesus stood in the temple courts where Jeremiah once preached and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37). He offered a Samaritan woman, who had tried many lovers, water that would become "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14). He is the true vine where Israel became a wild one, and his blood does what lye and soap never could. The husband who remembered "the devotion of your youth" did not wait for his bride to come back on her own. In Christ he came after her, and gave himself for her (Eph 5:25).
