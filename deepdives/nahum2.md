*Setting:* Nahum wrote this between 663 BC and 612 BC, while Assyria still looked unbeatable. Chapter 1 announced who would bring Nineveh down: the LORD, slow to anger but great in power, who promised to break Assyria's yoke off Judah. Chapter 2 shows how, in a burst of battle poetry so vivid that readers have long called it one of the finest war scenes in ancient literature. What Nahum saw happened in the summer of 612 BC. After Ashurbanipal, the last great Assyrian king, died around 631 BC, the empire began to crack. The Babylonians under Nabopolassar broke free, and the Medes from the Iranian highlands under Cyaxares joined them. They took the old city of Ashur in 614 BC and then laid siege to Nineveh. A Babylonian tablet in the British Museum, the "Fall of Nineveh Chronicle," records that the city fell after about three months, that its king died, and that huge spoil was carried off. A remnant of Assyria's army fled west to Harran, which is why Pharaoh Neco was marching north in 609 BC when Josiah tried to stop him (2 Chr 35:20).

#### The Destruction of Nineveh (vv1–13)
- **Verse numbers.** English Bibles end chapter 1 with the herald of good news (1:15). Hebrew Bibles make that verse 2:1, so Hebrew 2:2–14 is English 2:1–13. The ESV follows the English numbering.
- **"The scatterer has come up against you" (v1).** The attacker is not named. Historically it was the combined Median and Babylonian army, but the point is that God is behind it. "Scatterer" (*mephits*) is the one who breaks a people apart and drives them away, the very thing Assyria had done to Israel when it deported the ten northern tribes in 722 BC. Now the scatterer is scattered.
- **"Man the ramparts; watch the road; dress for battle; collect all your strength" (v1).** Four short, sharp commands, like orders shouted along the wall. The tone is ironic. The prophet tells Nineveh to do everything it can, knowing it will not be enough (1:12).
- **"For the LORD is restoring the majesty of Jacob as the majesty of Israel" (v2).** In the middle of the battle comes the reason for it. "Jacob" and "Israel" may stand for the southern and northern peoples, both crushed by Assyria and both to be restored. Or "Jacob" may be the humble name and "Israel" the honored one, given to Jacob when he wrestled with God (Gen 32:28). Either way, Nineveh falls so that God's people can be lifted up. "Plunderers have plundered them and ruined their branches" pictures Israel as a vine stripped bare (Ps 80:8–16; Isa 5:1–7). The Tyndale notes connect the image to God's promise of a restored vineyard (Isa 27:2–6).
- **"The shield of his mighty men is red; his soldiers are clothed in scarlet" (v3).** Red shields may have been dyed or covered with red leather, or they may be copper and bronze flashing in the sun. JFB gives both views. Ezekiel describes the Chaldeans painted in vermilion with dyed turbans (Ezek 23:14–15). Some also think of blood. The effect is terrifying color in motion.
- **"The chariots come with flashing metal… the cypress spears are brandished" (v3).** "Flashing metal" is literally "fire of steel" or "fire of iron fittings," the gleam of the chariots' fittings in the light. The "cypress spears" are spear shafts of hard wood, shaken as the army forms up.
- **"The chariots race madly through the streets… they gleam like torches; they dart like lightning" (v4).** The scene has moved inside the city. Chariots, the tanks of the ancient world, are careering through the squares. Guzik notes, citing Chuck Smith, that some have tried to read this as a prophecy of modern cars, which misses entirely that Nahum is describing Nineveh's fall.
- **"He remembers his officers; they stumble as they go" (v5).** Most take "he" as the Assyrian king, rallying his best troops. They stumble in their haste, perhaps over the bodies already in the streets (3:3). **"The siege tower is set up."** The Hebrew word (*sokek*) means a "covering," some kind of protective shelter. The Tyndale notes say the meaning is unclear, and it may be a shield screen used by either side. Assyrian palace reliefs show exactly such siege engines, used by the Assyrians against others for two centuries.
- **"The river gates are opened; the palace melts away" (v6).** Nineveh lay where the small Khosr River flows into the Tigris. The Khosr ran right through the city, controlled by dams and sluice gates, and the Assyrians had built canals to bring in water. "The river gates" may be these sluices, opened or broken, or the city gates on the river side. The Greek historian Diodorus, writing in the first century BC, says that in the siege's third year, heavy rains swelled the river until it broke down part of the wall. His dates are muddled, and many historians doubt the details, but the tradition of a flood fits Nahum's picture (1:8; 2:8). "Melts away" is the word for dissolving. It can mean the palace collapses, or that its people melt with fear.
- **"Its mistress is stripped; she is carried off" (v7).** The first Hebrew word of this verse (*huṣṣab*) is one of the hard words of the book. The KJV took it as a proper name, "Huzzab," perhaps a queen. Others translate it "it is decreed," that is, the city's exile is fixed by God. The ESV takes it as the city's "mistress," the city pictured as a noblewoman, or perhaps a queen or the image of the goddess Ishtar, whose great temple stood in Nineveh. All three readings are held by careful scholars. The picture is the same: the proud lady of the city is led away stripped.
- **"Her slave girls lamenting, moaning like doves and beating their breasts" (v7).** The mourning of doves is a stock image for low, sad crying (Isa 38:14; 59:11). Beating the breast is the gesture of grief and helplessness. The tax collector in Jesus' parable does it (Luke 18:13).
- **"Nineveh is like a pool whose waters run away" (v8).** Nineveh had been a reservoir, collecting the wealth and people of the world. Now it drains. The image may connect with the flood of v6. Waters can also picture peoples (Rev 17:15), so the city's population is pouring out of the broken walls. **"'Halt! Halt!' they cry, but none turns back."** Officers shout to stop the rout, and no one listens.
- **"Plunder the silver, plunder the gold! There is no end of the treasure" (v9).** Now the conquerors are speaking. Nineveh had stripped the nations for centuries. Assyrian records boast of tribute from every direction, including Hezekiah's gold and silver (2 Kgs 18:14). Now what was taken is taken. The Fall of Nineveh Chronicle confirms that "great quantities of spoil" were carried off. Guzik and JFB both note that excavators have found little gold or silver in the ruins.
- **"Desolate! Desolation and ruin!" (v10).** The Hebrew is a burst of sound: *buqah umebuqah umebullaqah*. The Tyndale notes suggest an English echo like "devastated, despoiled, and destroyed." The words sound like something being emptied out. **"Hearts melt and knees tremble… all faces grow pale."** Fear is described from the inside out: heart, knees, loins, faces.
- **"Where is the lions' den?" (v11).** Now comes a taunt song, the first of three in the book (the Tyndale notes count 2:11–13; 3:8–13; 3:14–19). The lion was Assyria's royal symbol. Kings compared themselves to lions in their inscriptions, and the famous reliefs from Ashurbanipal's palace at Nineveh, now in the British Museum, show the king hunting and killing lions as proof of his power. Nahum turns the boast into a riddle. Where is that den now?
- **"With none to disturb" (v11).** Nineveh lived for centuries without fear. No one dared to touch the lion's cubs.
- **"The lion tore enough for his cubs… he filled his caves with prey and his dens with torn flesh" (v12).** This is a deliberately ugly image of what Assyria's wealth was made of. The palaces of Nineveh were stocked by plunder from other people's homes. Assyrian kings' own records speak openly of cruelty to the conquered, and Nahum lets the picture speak for itself.
- **"Behold, I am against you, declares the LORD of hosts" (v13).** These five Hebrew words are the turning point of the whole book, and they come again in 3:5. "LORD of hosts" (*YHWH tseva'ot*) means the LORD of armies, the commander of heaven's forces. Assyria trusted its armies. It now faces the one whose armies are without number. Guzik sets this beside Romans 8:31: if God is for his people, no one can stand against them. The reverse is also true. If God is against a people, no one can be for them.
- **"I will burn your chariots in smoke, and the sword shall devour your young lions" (v13).** The chariots of vv3–4 and the lions of vv11–12 are picked up and answered. Archaeology tells a grim story here too. Excavations at Nineveh's Halzi Gate uncovered the skeletons of defenders who died in the final assault, and thick burn layers show the city went up in flames. Ironically, the fire baked hard the clay tablets of Ashurbanipal's great library, which is why so many survived to be found in the 1850s, among them the Babylonian flood story and the Epic of Gilgamesh.
- **"The voice of your messengers shall no longer be heard" (v13).** Assyria's messengers had once stood outside Jerusalem's walls and mocked the LORD (2 Kgs 18:17–35; 19:9–13). Now they fall silent. The contrast with 1:15 is deliberate. Assyria's heralds go quiet, and the herald of good news runs over the mountains to Judah.
- **Commentators:** Henry outlines the chapter as alarm, cause, terror and consequence. On v2 he insists that "God's quarrel with them is for the violence done to Jacob," and he identifies the attacker as Nebuchadnezzar, though the Babylonian army in 612 BC was led by his father Nabopolassar together with the Medes. Henry reads v6 as the gates on the Tigris and v8 as defenders who "shall flee away like cowards." JFB calls the invader God's "battle axe," reads the commands of v1 as ironic, and on v6 passes on Diodorus's account of the flood. On v7 JFB sets out both the "queen" and "it is decreed" readings of *huṣṣab*. Guzik sees Nahum as watching a real battle in his vision. He notes the lion as Assyria's emblem, passes on Boice's remark that the city was stripped bare, and calls "I am against you" one of the most fearful things a people could ever hear from God.

#### Pointing to Christ
The lion that filled its den with torn flesh is gone, and its messengers are silent. The Bible ends its story with another Lion, "the Lion of the tribe of Judah," who conquers not by tearing prey but by being slain as a Lamb (Rev 5:5–6). Nineveh's power was built on taking. Christ's victory comes by giving himself. Through him the fearful words "I am against you" are turned around for all who trust him: "If God is for us, who can be against us? He who did not spare his own Son… how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?" (Rom 8:31–32).
