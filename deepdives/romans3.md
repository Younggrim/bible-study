*Setting:* Paul writes from Corinth in the winter of about AD 57 (Acts 20:2–3), to a church in Rome he has never visited. Since 1:18 he has been building a courtroom case. Chapter 1 exposed the idolatry and moral collapse of the Gentile world. Chapter 2 turned on the person who judges others, and then on the Jew who trusts in the law and circumcision. Now Paul answers the objections his argument has stirred up, closes the case against all humanity, and then turns, with "But now" (v21), to the heart of the whole letter: how a guilty world is made right with God.

#### God's Righteousness Upheld (vv1–8)
- **"Then what advantage has the Jew?" (v1).** If circumcision does not save (2:25–29), was being Jewish worth anything? Paul writes in the style of a *diatribe*, a teaching form of the day in which the speaker raises an imagined objector's questions and answers them. He had preached for over twenty years and knew exactly what people would ask.
- **"Much in every way" (v2).** Paul will not dismiss Israel's privileges. "To begin with" (literally "first") never gets a second item here. The fuller list comes in 9:4–5: adoption, glory, covenants, the law, worship, promises, the patriarchs, and the Messiah himself.
- **"Entrusted with the oracles of God" (v2).** The word is *logia*, "oracles" or "divine sayings," used in the Greek Old Testament for God's spoken word (Ps 19:14). Israel was given God's own words to keep, the Scriptures, and with that gift came responsibility. "Entrusted" is a steward's word: what is entrusted is meant to be passed on.
- **"Does their faithlessness nullify the faithfulness of God?" (v3).** Paul plays on one root: *apistia* (faithlessness) against God's *pistis* (faithfulness). Israel's unbelief does not cancel God's promises. That question will come back at length in chapters 9–11.
- **"By no means!" (v4).** Greek *mē genoito*, literally "may it never be," Paul's strongest denial. It appears ten times in Romans (3:4, 6, 31; 6:2, 15; 7:7, 13; 9:14; 11:1, 11), always at a moment when someone might twist his teaching.
- **"Let God be true though every one were a liar" (v4).** Paul quotes Psalm 51:4, David's confession after Bathsheba. David's point was that God was right to judge him. Paul quotes the Greek Old Testament, which reads "prevail when you are judged," where the Hebrew has "blameless in your judgment." Either way the sense is the same: when God and a sinner are set side by side, God is in the right.
- **"Is God unrighteous to inflict wrath on us?" (v5).** Here is the clever objection: if my sin makes God's righteousness shine brighter, why punish me? I am doing him a favor. Paul flags it as "a human way" of talking, the kind of reasoning he would never endorse.
- **"For then how could God judge the world?" (v6).** Paul doesn't argue the point at length. Every Jew believed God would judge the world (Gen 18:25: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?"). If sin excused itself by serving God's glory, no one could ever be judged.
- **"Why not do evil that good may come?" (v8).** The objection pushed to its end. Paul says some people "slanderously" claimed he taught exactly this. The rumor shows what he actually preached: grace so free that critics thought it licensed sin. He answers the question fully in 6:1–2. Here he simply says, "Their condemnation is just."
- **Commentators:** All three treat vv1–8 as objections answered one by one. Henry insists sin "has never the less of malignity and demerit in it though God bring glory to himself out of it," and adds that "Let us do evil that good may come is oftener in the heart than in the mouth of sinners." Guzik illustrates with Judas: God brought salvation out of the betrayal, but that did not make the betrayer innocent. He also suggests testing a teaching by following its logic to the end. JFB calls the "evil that good may come" reasoning "a damnable principle," which Paul simply holds up for condemnation instead of refuting.

#### No One Is Righteous (vv9–20)
- **"Are we Jews any better off? No, not at all" (v9).** This doesn't contradict v2. Israel had real privileges, but privilege is not innocence. The Greek verb (*proechometha*) is unusual and can be read two ways, "Are we better?" or "Are we making excuses?" The ESV footnote gives an alternative. Either way the answer is no.
- **"All… are under sin" (v9).** "Under" means under the power of, like a slave under a master (7:14, "sold under sin"). Humanity's problem is not just a record of wrong acts but a captivity. That is why the answer will involve *redemption* (v24), the freeing of slaves.
- **A string of Scriptures (vv10–18).** Paul links six or so Old Testament passages into one chain, a Jewish teaching method sometimes called "pearl-stringing." The sources: Psalms 14:1–3 and 53:1–3 (vv10–12), Psalm 5:9 (v13a), Psalm 140:3 (v13b), Psalm 10:7 (v14), Isaiah 59:7–8 (vv15–17), and Psalm 36:1 (v18). The point is that Israel's own Scriptures make the charge, not Paul.
- **"None is righteous, no, not one" (v10).** Psalm 14 begins with "the fool" who says there is no God, but it widens to "the children of man" in general. Paul's "no, not one" leaves no exceptions. Some manuscripts of the Greek Psalms actually copied Paul's whole chain into Psalm 14 (LXX 13), a scribe's addition made under Romans' influence.
- **"No one seeks for God" (v11).** People are very religious (1:23), but left to themselves they seek gods they can manage, not the true God. Any real seeking starts with God seeking us (John 6:44).
- **"Together they have become worthless" (v12).** The Greek Old Testament word means gone useless or gone bad, like milk that has soured.
- **Throat, tongue, lips, mouth (vv13–14).** The first part of the chain is about speech, and each line names a different part of the body used to speak. "An open grave" is vivid: what comes out of the mouth smells of death. "The venom of asps" is a cobra's poison.
- **"Their feet are swift to shed blood" (vv15–17).** From Isaiah 59, a prophet's lament over Israel's own violence and injustice. "The way of peace they have not known" sets up 5:1, "we have peace with God."
- **"No fear of God before their eyes" (v18).** The chain ends at the root. The Tyndale notes point out that v18 begins with the same Greek words as v10 (*ouk estin*, "there is no"), bracketing the whole list. Throat, tongue, lips, mouth, feet, eyes: sin reaches the whole person.
- **"Whatever the law says it speaks to those who are under the law" (v19).** "The law" here means the whole Old Testament, since he has just quoted Psalms and Isaiah. A Jewish reader might think these verses were about pagans. Paul says no: they were written to Israel. If God's own people stand guilty, the rest of the world certainly does.
- **"So that every mouth may be stopped" (v19).** A courtroom picture: the defendant has nothing left to say. "Held accountable" means liable to judgment. The case begun in 1:18 is closed.
- **"By works of the law no human being will be justified" (v20).** An echo of Psalm 143:2. "Works of the law" means doing what the law of Moses commands. Scholars debate whether Paul has in mind mainly the Jewish identity markers (circumcision, Sabbath, food laws) or law-keeping of every kind. In context the principle is wide: if God's own law cannot make anyone right with him, nothing else will.
- **"Through the law comes knowledge of sin" (v20).** The law is a diagnosis, not a cure. Paul develops this in 7:7.
- **Commentators:** Henry splits vv10–18 into the "habitual" condition (no righteousness, no understanding, no seeking) and actual conduct (words and ways), with the root in the missing fear of God. "No righteousness is born with us," he writes, and on v20, "We can never be justified and saved by the law that we have broken." He adds a medical line: "That which is searching is not sanative," meaning what exposes the disease does not heal it. JFB reads the body-part list as Isaiah 1:6 come true: "from the sole of the foot even to the head there is no soundness." Guzik makes the same whole-person point, and adds that a broken law can only condemn, since even perfect obedience from now on could not erase past guilt.

#### The Righteousness of God Through Faith (vv21–31)
- **"But now" (v21).** Two of the most important words in the Bible. After two and a half chapters of darkness, God acts. "Now" is both logical and historical: a new era has arrived in the death and resurrection of Jesus.
- **"The righteousness of God" (v21).** This picks up the theme announced in 1:17. Christians have read the phrase as God's own righteous character, as the right standing God gives to believers, or as God's saving action in putting things right. Most readers see more than one at work here, and v26 holds them together: God shows that he is righteous *and* he makes sinners righteous.
- **"Apart from the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it" (v21).** Apart from law-keeping as the way in, but not apart from the Old Testament. Chapter 4 will call Abraham and David as witnesses.
- **"Through faith in Jesus Christ" (v22).** The ESV footnote gives "through the faithfulness of Jesus Christ." The Greek (*pistis Iēsou Christou*) can mean either. The traditional reading, held by most Reformed and evangelical scholars, is faith *in* Christ. A growing number of scholars read it as Christ's own faithfulness, his obedience unto death, with "for all who believe" then describing our response. Both readings keep faith as the way we receive it. The debate is about where Paul puts the emphasis in this phrase.
- **"There is no distinction" (v22).** The same words as 10:12. Jew and Gentile are equal in sin and equal in the way of rescue.
- **"All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (v23).** "Have sinned" looks back on a whole life as one act. "Fall short" (*hystereō*) is present tense: to lack, to come up short, as the rich young man asked, "What do I still lack?" (Matt 19:20). The glory of God is what humans were made to reflect (Gen 1:27; Ps 8:5) and what they exchanged for idols (1:23). It is restored in Christ (5:2; 8:30).
- **"Justified by his grace as a gift" (v24).** "Justify" (*dikaioō*) is a law-court word meaning to declare righteous, to rule in someone's favor. It does not mean God pretends sin never happened. It is a verdict. "As a gift" is *dōrean*, freely. The same word says Jesus was hated "without a cause" (John 15:25). There is no cause in us for the gift.
- **"Through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (v24).** *Apolytrōsis* means release by payment of a ransom. The word comes from the world of freeing slaves and prisoners. For Israel it recalled the exodus, when God redeemed his people from Egypt. Free to us, it was costly to him (1 Pet 1:18–19).
- **"Whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood" (v25).** *Hilastērion* is the Greek Old Testament word for the "mercy seat," the gold lid of the ark of the covenant (Exod 25:17; Lev 16:14–15; Heb 9:5). On the Day of Atonement the high priest sprinkled blood there, between the law inside the ark and the presence of God above it. The ESV footnote offers "mercy seat." "Propitiation" means a sacrifice that turns away God's wrath (1:18). Some scholars prefer "expiation," a sacrifice that wipes away sin, but in a letter that has spent two chapters on God's wrath, most read the word as including both. Note who acts: *God* put him forward. Jesus is not appeasing an unwilling Father. God himself provides the sacrifice (1 John 4:10).
- **"Put forward" (v25).** The mercy seat was hidden in the Most Holy Place, seen by one man once a year. Jesus was set forth publicly, on a cross outside the city.
- **"He had passed over former sins" (v25).** The word is *paresis*, "passing by," used only here in the New Testament. It is not the usual word for forgiveness (*aphesis*). Before the cross God held back full judgment, and sacrifices could not truly remove sin (Heb 10:4). That raised a real question: was God simply overlooking evil? The cross answers it. The penalty was not cancelled but postponed, and paid at last in Christ.
- **"That he might be just and the justifier" (v26).** The great problem of the Old Testament: how can a righteous God declare the guilty righteous (Exod 23:7; Prov 17:15)? At the cross God's justice is not set aside but satisfied, so he can be both just and merciful at once.
- **"What becomes of our boasting? It is excluded" (v27).** Boasting here means confidence before God in what we bring. Chapter 2 targeted the Jew who boasted in the law (2:17, 23). Faith shuts the door, because faith brings nothing but empty hands. "The law of faith" probably means the principle or order of faith, though some read it as the law of Moses rightly understood through faith.
- **"Justified by faith apart from works of the law" (v28).** Luther's German Bible added "alone" (*allein*) here, and he defended it as the sense of the passage. The Greek doesn't have the word, but "apart from works" says much the same. James 2:24 ("not by faith alone") is often set against this. Most Protestant readers see the two addressing different questions: Paul, how a sinner is accepted by God, and James, what real faith looks like when it is alive.
- **"Since God is one" (vv29–30).** Paul builds on the Shema, Israel's daily confession, "The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4). If there is only one God, he is the God of the nations too, and there can be only one way to him. "By faith" and "through faith" are probably just variety of style, not two different methods.
- **"Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means!" (v31).** The third *mē genoito* of the chapter. Faith does not tear the law down. Chapter 4 will show the law itself testifying to faith, and 8:4 shows the Spirit enabling believers to fulfil "the righteous requirement of the law."
- **Commentators:** Guzik frames vv24–25 with three pictures: justification from the law court (guilt), redemption from the slave market (bondage), and propitiation from the temple (offense against God). He notes that the passed-over sins were like an IOU, paid in full at the cross. JFB agrees with the lexicon that v25 means "pretermission" or "passing by," not remission, and calls "just and the justifier" a "glorious paradox," resolved by the atonement. Henry: "Christ is the propitiation; there is the healing plaster provided," and God would not "demand the debt of the principal when the surety has paid it." On v27 he describes faith as "a depending, self-emptying, self-denying grace," which "casts every crown before the throne." On v31 Henry and JFB both insist believers are still bound by the moral law, and JFB points ahead to chapter 6 for Paul's full answer. Guzik reads v31 in light of chapter 4: the gospel upholds the law by fulfilling what it foretold.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter closes every escape route: privilege, religion, good intentions, and clever arguments all fall silent. Then God does what no one else could. The mercy seat, hidden for centuries behind a curtain, is set out in the open at Calvary. Blood once sprinkled on a gold lid is now the blood of God's own Son. There God shows himself fully just, punishing sin, and fully gracious, freely declaring the guilty righteous. No one boasts at the foot of the cross, but anyone, Jew or Gentile, may come there by faith.
