*Setting:* Joseph has been in Egypt about thirteen years: sold as a slave at seventeen (37:2), made steward of Potiphar's house, falsely accused by his master's wife, and imprisoned (ch 39). In prison he correctly interpreted the dreams of Pharaoh's cupbearer and baker, and he asked the cupbearer to remember him. The cupbearer forgot (40:23). Two more years pass. The Pharaoh in this chapter is not named, and scholars debate which period of Egyptian history the story belongs to. Some place it in the Middle Kingdom (about 1900–1800 BC), and others in the later period when the Hyksos, a Semitic people, ruled northern Egypt. Either way, Egypt was rich and orderly, and its life depended entirely on the yearly flood of the Nile. When the river rose well, the land fed nations. When it failed, people starved. This is the chapter where Joseph's long descent turns around in a single day.

#### Joseph Interprets Pharaoh's Dreams (vv1–36)
- **"After two whole years" (v1).** Literally "two years of days," full and long. The cupbearer's forgetting cost Joseph two more years. Henry notes it "was not till the end of two full years" that God acted, and uses it to commend patience. God's timing was deliberate. If Joseph had been released earlier, he might have gone home to Canaan. Instead he is ready at the very moment Pharaoh needs him.
- **"Standing by the Nile" (v1).** The Nile was Egypt's life. Almost no rain falls in Egypt, so every harvest depended on the river flooding its banks each summer and leaving behind water and rich mud. Egyptians worshiped the Nile's flood as a god, and Pharaoh was thought to be responsible for keeping the order of nature. A dream about the Nile failing was a dream about Pharaoh's own power failing.
- **The cows (vv2–4).** Seven fat cows come up out of the river and graze in the "reed grass," an Egyptian word for the marsh grass along the banks. Then seven thin, ugly cows come up and eat them. Cows grazing in the Nile shallows were a common sight. Cattle were also sacred to some Egyptian gods. The picture of cows eating cows is unnatural and disturbing, and that is the point.
- **The grain (vv5–7).** Seven full ears on one stalk are swallowed by seven thin ones "blighted by the east wind." The east wind was the hot, dry wind from the desert that could wither crops in days. Two dreams, one message.
- **"All the magicians of Egypt and all its wise men" (v8).** "Magicians" (*hartummim*) is a word probably borrowed from Egyptian, used for the priestly scholars who studied sacred texts and interpreted signs. The same word is used for Pharaoh's magicians in Moses' day (Exod 7:11) and for Nebuchadnezzar's experts in Babylon (Dan 2:2). Egypt's best minds have no answer. The Tyndale notes point out the pattern: in Exodus and in Daniel too, God confounds the wise men of the empire through a Hebrew captive.
- **"I remember my offenses today" (vv9–13).** At last the cupbearer remembers, and he admits he had forgotten. "Remember" answers 40:23, where he "did not remember Joseph." He describes Joseph in small terms, "a young Hebrew… a servant," perhaps to keep from looking foolish for not mentioning him sooner. But he tells the key fact: "as he interpreted to us, so it came about."
- **"They quickly brought him out of the pit" (v14).** After years of waiting, things move fast. "The pit" is again the word *bor*, used for the cistern his brothers threw him in (37:24) and for the prison (40:15). **"When he had shaved himself and changed his clothes."** Egyptians were clean-shaven, unlike Semitic peoples, and no one appeared before Pharaoh unshaven or in prison clothes. The garments that mark every turn of Joseph's story change once more: his robe was stripped (37:23), his cloak left in a woman's hand (39:12), and now he is dressed for the palace.
- **"It is not in me; God will give Pharaoh a favorable answer" (vv15–16).** Pharaoh has heard that Joseph can interpret dreams. Joseph's first words are a correction. "It is not in me" is a single, emphatic word in Hebrew, something like "Not me!" He points to God, as he did in prison (40:8). "Favorable answer" is literally "the *shalom* of Pharaoh," his welfare. Many commentators, Guzik among them, see real growth here. The young man who announced his own dreams to his brothers now refuses any credit in front of the most powerful man on earth.
- **Pharaoh retells the dreams (vv17–24).** The retelling adds Pharaoh's own feelings. The thin cows were "such as I had never seen in all the land of Egypt," and after eating the fat ones "no one would have known that they had eaten them." The famine will swallow the plenty and leave no trace of it.
- **"The dreams of Pharaoh are one; God has revealed to Pharaoh what he is about to do" (vv25–28).** Joseph speaks of God four times in his answer (vv16, 25, 28, 32). Pharaoh, considered divine by his people, is told that the true God controls the Nile and the harvest. Seven good cows and ears are seven years of plenty. Seven thin ones are seven years of famine.
- **"The famine will consume the land" (vv29–31).** The famine will be so severe that "all the plenty will be forgotten." An Egyptian inscription carved much later, the so-called Famine Stela, tells a legend of seven years of low Nile floods and famine in the time of an earlier king. It is not the same event, but it shows that seven-year famines were remembered in Egypt as a real terror.
- **"The doubling of Pharaoh's dream means that the thing is fixed by God" (v32).** Two dreams mean certainty, and soon. The reader thinks back: Joseph too had two dreams (37:5–9). If God's doubled word to Pharaoh is fixed, so is his doubled word to Joseph.
- **"Let Pharaoh select a discerning and wise man" (vv33–36).** Joseph does not stop at the interpretation. He gives a plan: appoint an overseer, set officials over the land, collect a fifth of the harvest in the good years, store it in the cities, and keep it as a reserve. A fifth was a heavy but not crushing tax, and it later became permanent (47:24–26). The Tyndale notes connect this with the wisdom of planning ahead, like the ant in Proverbs, which "gathers her food in harvest" (Prov 6:6–8). Henry: "Fair warning should always be followed with good counsel."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the doubled dream as a sign that the event was certain and near, and notes that Egypt's harvests depended on the Nile, not on rain. He sees Joseph's answer in v16 as humility, not false modesty. Guzik observes that God's work can seem slow for years and then come together in a day, and that the dreams themselves came at the end of the two years as part of God's timing. He also notes that a responsible person who brings bad news should bring a plan. JFB says Joseph spoke "disclaiming all merit" and points out that the word for "swallowed" in v24 differs from "ate" in v4, adding the idea of destroying. The Tyndale notes point out the three pairs of dreams in the story: Joseph's, the prisoners', and Pharaoh's.

#### Joseph Rises to Power (vv37–57)
- **"A man… in whom is the Spirit of God" (v38).** Pharaoh sees in Joseph something beyond human cleverness. The Hebrew can be read "the Spirit of God" or "the spirit of the gods," as a pagan king might mean it. Nebuchadnezzar said something similar of Daniel, "in whom is the spirit of the holy gods" (Dan 4:8). Pharaoh may not have understood fully what he was saying, but he spoke more truly than he knew.
- **"You shall be over my house" (vv39–40).** Joseph ran Potiphar's house (39:4) and the prison (39:22). Now he runs Pharaoh's house and kingdom. **"All my people shall order themselves as you command."** The verb is unusual and literally may mean "kiss," as in kissing the hand or ground in submission, or "be ruled" or "be armed." The sense is clear either way: all Egypt is to obey him. **"Only as regards the throne will I be greater than you."** Joseph becomes, in effect, Egypt's chief minister, a role Egyptian records call the vizier.
- **Signet ring, fine linen and a gold chain (v42).** The signet ring carried Pharaoh's seal, used to sign laws and documents, so handing it over gave Joseph Pharaoh's authority to act in his name (compare Esth 8:2). "Fine linen" was the clothing of the Egyptian upper class and priesthood. A gold collar or chain was a known Egyptian reward for loyal officials, and tomb paintings show officials being given one by the king. The boy stripped of his robe is now robed by Pharaoh.
- **"His second chariot… 'Bow the knee!'" (v43).** Chariots were the newest and most prestigious form of transport and war. The cry in Hebrew is *abrek*, a word found only here. Its meaning is uncertain. The Hebrew dictionary lists several suggestions: an Egyptian word meaning "Attention!" or "Make way!", or a title like the Akkadian word for a high royal steward. "Bow the knee" follows an old Jewish reading that links it to the Hebrew word for "knee" (*berek*). JFB calls it "an Egyptian term, not referring to prostration." Whatever it meant, everyone knew who was coming. The dream of the sheaves bowing (37:7) is beginning to come true, though the brothers are not here yet.
- **"I am Pharaoh" (v44).** Pharaoh puts his own name behind the appointment. Henry suggests the grant is repeated so solemnly because some at court may have resented it. A Hebrew slave now gives orders to the officials of the oldest kingdom on earth.
- **"Zaphenath-paneah" (v45).** Pharaoh gives Joseph an Egyptian name, a mark of belonging to his court (compare Daniel and his friends in Dan 1:7). Its meaning is debated. Many Egyptologists read it as "God speaks and he lives" or "the god has said he will live." Older Jewish and Christian writers, JFB among them, read it as "revealer of secrets." **Asenath** probably means "belonging to Neith," an Egyptian goddess. Her father **Potiphera** is "he whom Ra gave," and he is priest of **On**, the city the Greeks called Heliopolis, "sun city," a center of the worship of the sun god near today's Cairo. Joseph marries into one of Egypt's most important priestly families. The text makes no comment on it. This was before the Law of Moses forbade marriage to foreign women who served other gods, and Joseph gives his sons Hebrew names that honor his God (vv51–52). A later Jewish story, *Joseph and Aseneth*, imagines her turning to the God of Israel, but that is legend, not Scripture.
- **"Joseph was thirty years old" (v46).** Thirteen years have passed since he was sold. In Israel thirty was the age at which priests began their service (Num 4:3), the age at which David became king (2 Sam 5:4), and Jesus "was about thirty years of age" when he began his ministry (Luke 3:23). Joseph's first act is to go "through all the land of Egypt," learning the country he must manage.
- **"Like the sand of the sea" (vv47–49).** The years of plenty come exactly as Joseph said. Grain piles up "until he ceased to measure it." "Like the sand of the sea" is the language of God's promise to Abraham about his offspring (22:17; 32:12). God's abundance now flows through Abraham's descendant to Egypt.
- **"Manasseh… God has made me forget" (vv50–51).** Joseph's sons are born before the famine. Manasseh sounds like the Hebrew for "making to forget." "God has made me forget all my hardship and all my father's house." This is not amnesia. It means the pain has been healed and no longer rules him. Yet "my father's house" is about to walk back into his life, and he will find that some things have not been forgotten at all (42:21–24).
- **"Ephraim… God has made me fruitful in the land of my affliction" (v52).** Ephraim sounds like "doubly fruitful." "Affliction" is *'oni*, the same word used when God "has looked upon my affliction" (29:32) and "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt" (Exod 3:7). Egypt is still "the land of my affliction" to Joseph, even in his success, but God made him fruitful there. Both names give the credit to God. Later Jacob adopts these two boys as his own (48:5), and they become two of the tribes of Israel.
- **"In all the land of Egypt there was bread" (vv53–54).** The famine comes "as Joseph had said." It strikes "all lands," which probably means the whole region around Egypt, including Canaan. Only Egypt is ready.
- **"Go to Joseph. What he says to you, do" (v55).** When the people cry to Pharaoh for bread, he sends them to Joseph. The words are strikingly close to Mary's at Cana: "Do whatever he tells you" (John 2:5).
- **"Joseph opened all the storehouses and sold" (vv56–57).** The grain is sold, not given. The next chapters show the cost to Egypt as the famine goes on (47:13–26). **"All the earth came to Egypt to Joseph to buy grain."** God promised Abraham that through his offspring "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (12:3). Here, in a small way, it begins to happen. And among "all the earth" are ten men from Canaan, which is where the next chapter begins.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees the exaltation as "typical of the exaltation of Christ, that great revealer of secrets." He says Pharaoh gave Joseph a new name "to show his authority over him," and lists his marriage to "a prince's daughter" among Pharaoh's honors. Guzik notes that Joseph's rise took about thirteen years, and that the fifth Joseph collected was a heavy tax. He sees in the sons' Hebrew names that Joseph's faith survived his success, and he lists more than thirty parallels between Joseph and Jesus: loved by his father, rejected by his brothers, sold for silver, condemned though innocent, raised from humiliation to the right hand of power, and the source of bread for the world. JFB explains the ring as "used for signing public documents," the chain as "a badge of rank," and the new name as "revealer of secrets" or "saviour of the land." On vv51–52 JFB says the names "develop the piety of his character." The Tyndale notes observe that Joseph's first move as ruler was to learn the land he governed.

#### Pointing to Christ
In one day Joseph goes from the pit to the right hand of the throne. He was rejected and condemned though innocent, and now he is exalted, given a name, and set over all, so that people "bow the knee" before him and the whole world comes to him for bread. Peter preached that God raised the rejected Jesus and "exalted him at his right hand as Leader and Savior" (Acts 5:31). Paul says God "highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Phil 2:9–10). Pharaoh said, "Go to Joseph." Jesus says, "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger" (John 6:35). Joseph stored bread to save Egypt from famine. Jesus gives himself as bread for the life of the world.
