*Setting:* Psalm 148 is the third of the five "Hallelujah psalms" (146–150) that close the Psalter. The circle of praise has been widening: one soul in Psalm 146, the city of Jerusalem in Psalm 147, and now the whole universe. Everything God made, from angels and stars to sea creatures, hailstorms, cedar trees, cattle, kings and children, is summoned to praise the LORD. The psalm has no Hebrew heading. The Greek Septuagint links it, with Psalms 146 and 147, to the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, which would place it after the return from exile, though that heading is a later tradition. The structure is simple and deliberate. The first half calls on the heavens (vv1–4) and gives the reason: God created them by his command (vv5–6). The second half calls on the earth (vv7–12) and gives the reason: God's name alone is exalted, and he has raised up a horn for his people (vv13–14). Both reasons begin with the same words: "Let them praise the name of the LORD." The order of creatures loosely follows Genesis 1, from heavens and lights to sea creatures, land animals and finally humans. This psalm inspired a long line of Christian hymns, among them the *Benedicite* (an ancient canticle added to Daniel 3 in the Greek Bible and sung in Christian worship for centuries), Francis of Assisi's "Canticle of the Sun," and the hymn "All Creatures of Our God and King" that grew out of it. JFB says its scope is "the same as that of the preceding" psalm. The ESV gives no section headings in the Psalms. The headings below follow the psalm's two halves and their two reasons, grouping the ESV's short stanzas.

#### Praise the LORD from the Heavens (vv1–4)
- **"Praise the LORD! Praise the LORD from the heavens; praise him in the heights!" (v1).** The verb "praise" (*hallelu*) appears nine times in vv1–4 alone. The command is plural, addressed to a crowd. "From the heavens" means starting from the top: the call goes out first to the sky and everything above it. JFB says the "heavens [and] heights" are synonymous. The Tyndale notes point out that the crowds at Jesus' entry into Jerusalem drew on this verse. They shouted, "Hosanna in the highest!" and "Glory in the highest!" (Matt 21:9; Luke 19:38).
- **"Praise him, all his angels; praise him, all his hosts!" (v2).** The angels are God's messengers (*mal'akhim*) and his "hosts" (*tseva'im*), his armies, the heavenly beings who serve him. Psalm 103:20–21 calls on them in the same way: "Bless the LORD, O you his angels… all his hosts, his ministers, who do his will." Isaiah saw seraphim crying "Holy, holy, holy" (Isa 6:3), and Revelation shows countless angels around the throne (Rev 5:11). The angels are called to praise, not to be praised. Kidner, as Guzik reports, sees here a quiet warning against worshiping angels (Col 2:18; Rev 22:8–9).
- **"Praise him, sun and moon, praise him, all you shining stars!" (v3).** In the nations around Israel, the sun, moon and stars were worshiped as gods. Babylon had its moon god Sin and its sun god Shamash, and Egypt had Ra. Israel was warned not to bow to "the sun and the moon and the stars, all the host of heaven" (Deut 4:19). This psalm turns the would-be gods into worshipers. They are creatures, and like every creature they owe praise to their Maker. They praise him simply by shining as he made them to shine (Ps 19:1–4). JFB has no note on this verse.
- **"Praise him, you highest heavens" (v4).** Literally "heavens of heavens," the Hebrew way of forming a superlative: the very highest heavens. JFB: "the very highest." Solomon used the same phrase: "heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you" (1 Kings 8:27).
- **"And you waters above the heavens!" (v4).** This recalls Genesis 1:7, where God separated "the waters that were above the expanse" from the waters below. Ancient Israelites, like their neighbors, described the sky as they saw it: rain comes from above, so there are waters above. JFB takes them as "clouds, resting above the visible heavens." Kidner, as Guzik reports, calls the phrase a poetic or popular way of speaking about rain clouds. The psalm is not teaching a model of the cosmos. It is calling on every part of the world, as people saw it, to praise.
- **Commentators:** Henry says the angels are always praising God, and the psalmist wants God "praised in the best manner." Our praise, he adds, joins us with them. Guzik contrasts this psalm with Psalm 19: there the heavens declare God's glory by their nature, while here they are called on to praise him directly. He passes on Maclaren's remark that among the stars obedience rules.

#### He Commanded and They Were Created (vv5–6)
- **"Let them praise the name of the LORD! For he commanded and they were created" (v5).** Here is the first reason. The heavens owe their very existence to God's word. Genesis 1 repeats "And God said… and it was so." Psalm 33:9 says, "he spoke, and it came to be; he commanded, and it stood firm." JFB notes that "He" is emphatic in the Hebrew, "ascribing creation to God alone." The sun and stars are not eternal powers. They were made, and something made cannot be God. "Created" (*nivra'u*) is from *bara'*, the verb used in the Old Testament only with God as subject.
- **"And he established them forever and ever" (v6).** God did not only make the world. He keeps it in place. "Established" (*ya'amidem*) means made them stand. The regular paths of sun, moon and stars, the reliable seasons, are signs of God's faithfulness. God appeals to them as proof of his promises: "If you can break my covenant with the day and my covenant with the night… then also my covenant with David my servant may be broken" (Jer 33:20–21; also 31:35–36).
- **"He gave a decree, and it shall not pass away" (v6).** "Decree" (*choq*) is a fixed boundary or law, the same word used for the "statutes" of the covenant in 147:19. God gives laws to the stars and laws to his people. The stars obey. JFB says God's ordinances for them "shall not change." The Tyndale notes say the created order is stable because of the Lord's word. JFB adds the qualification that this permanence is "subject to Him who formed it." Scripture also speaks of a day when heaven and earth will be renewed (Isa 65:17; 2 Pet 3:13), and Jesus said, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Matt 24:35).
- **Commentators:** Guzik passes on Spurgeon's argument that the doctrine of creation leads straight to worship, and that God is the world's Preserver and Ruler as well as its Maker. Henry treats vv1–6 as a single call to the upper world, grounded in creation and preservation.

#### Praise the LORD from the Earth (vv7–12)
- **"Praise the LORD from the earth, you great sea creatures and all deeps" (v7).** The call turns from the top of creation to the bottom. "Great sea creatures" (*tanninim*) is the same word as Genesis 1:21, "God created the great sea creatures." Elsewhere it is used of serpents (Exod 7:9) and of the sea monster that stood for chaos and evil (Isa 27:1, "the dragon that is in the sea"), which is why the KJV has "dragons." "Deeps" (*tehomot*) are the ocean depths, the *tehom* of Genesis 1:2, the dark waters at the beginning. Ancient peoples feared the sea and its monsters as forces of chaos. The psalm calls them to praise. Nothing in creation is outside God's rule. JFB says the "most destructive and ungovernable agents of inanimate nature are introduced" here.
- **"Fire and hail, snow and mist, stormy wind fulfilling his word!" (v8).** "Fire" probably means lightning, paired with hail as in the plagues of Egypt (Exod 9:23–24) and Psalm 18:12. "Mist" (*qitor*) may be smoke or thick cloud. The key phrase is "fulfilling his word." Storms are not random, and they are not the work of rival gods. The Tyndale notes explain that ancient peoples assigned different parts of nature to different gods, while the LORD rules them all. They do his bidding (Ps 104:4; 147:15–18). A great wind brought quail to Israel (Num 11:31) and a storm caught Jonah (Jonah 1:4). Jesus' disciples, after he stilled the storm, asked, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41).
- **"Mountains and all hills, fruit trees and all cedars!" (v9).** JFB contrasts "trees of fruit" with forest trees. The cedars of Lebanon were the tallest and grandest trees Israel knew, used in the temple (1 Kings 5:6). Fruit trees fed people, and cedars were admired. Both are called to praise. Isaiah pictured the trees clapping their hands (Isa 55:12).
- **"Beasts and all livestock, creeping things and flying birds!" (v10).** Wild animals, domestic animals, small creatures and birds: JFB says "Wild and domestic, large and small animals are comprehended." These are the land animals of Genesis 1:24–25 and the birds of 1:20. All of them, in their own way, give glory to God by being what he made them.
- **"Kings of the earth and all peoples, princes and all rulers of the earth!" (v11).** Last of all come human beings, as in Genesis 1, where humanity is created on the sixth day. The call starts with the powerful. Kings and rulers are often the last to bow to anyone. Here they are told to praise the King above them (Ps 2:10–11; 72:11). JFB glosses "princes" as military leaders. "All peoples" means not only Israel but every nation.
- **"Young men and maidens together, old men and children!" (v12).** Every age and both sexes. The Tyndale notes say the psalm calls all humans to praise, whatever their status, age or gender. No one is too young, too old or too unimportant. JFB: "all rational beings, from the highest in rank to little children." Jesus quoted Psalm 8:2 when children shouted praise in the temple: "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise" (Matt 21:16).
- **Commentators:** Henry: "The powers of nature, be they ever so strong, so stormy, do what God appoints them, and no more." He adds that even those who rebel against God's word are "more violent than even the stormy winds, yet they fulfil it." Guzik stresses that the animals are called to worship God, which is the opposite of worshiping nature, and passes on Spurgeon's sharp remark that people who refuse to praise God fall below the beasts. Poole, as Guzik reports, weighs whether the sea creatures are serpents of the deep or whales.

#### For His Name Alone Is Exalted (vv13–14)
- **"Let them praise the name of the LORD, for his name alone is exalted" (v13).** The second reason repeats the opening words of v5. God's "name" stands for God himself as he has revealed himself (JFB: "as representing His perfections"). "Alone" is the key word. Sun, moon, sea monsters and kings have all been called to praise. None of them is exalted. Only the LORD is.
- **"His majesty is above earth and heaven" (v13).** "Majesty" (*hod*) means splendor, glory. JFB: "Their united splendors fail to match His." Everything named in the psalm, all together, cannot match the glory of the one who made it.
- **"He has raised up a horn for his people" (v14).** The horn of a bull or wild ox was a picture of strength, the animal's weapon and pride. To "raise up a horn" means to give strength, victory and honor. Hannah sang, "my horn is exalted in the LORD" (1 Sam 2:1), and prayed that God would "exalt the horn of his anointed" (2:10). Psalm 132:17 promised, "There I will make a horn to sprout for David." JFB: "established power." For a people who had come back from exile weak and few, this was a reason to praise. The God of the whole universe had lifted up his small people.
- **"Praise for all his saints, for the people of Israel who are near to him" (v14).** The horn is "praise for all his saints": either God's gift gives his people a reason to praise, or it makes them praised among the nations. "Saints" (*chasidim*) are his faithful, devoted ones, the people bound to him in loyal love (*chesed*). The Tyndale notes call them his trusting and devoted servants. "Near to him" (*'am qerovo*) is a remarkable phrase. The God whose glory is above heaven and earth has a people who are close to him. Moses said it: "what great nation is there that has a god so near to it as the LORD our God is to us?" (Deut 4:7). JFB says they stand in "a peculiarly intimate relation" to him.
- **"Praise the LORD!" (v14).** The psalm ends as it began. All creation praises God, but his people have a special reason: he has drawn them near.
- **Commentators:** Henry reads the horn as Christ, exalted as Prince and Saviour, "who is indeed the defence and the praise of all his saints, and will be so for ever." He adds, "In redemption, that unspeakable glory is displayed, which forms the source of all our hopes and joys." Guzik passes on VanGemeren's point that God loves all his creation but has a special bond with his people, and Morgan's observation that the psalm never mentions mercy because it never mentions evil: it is a picture of creation as it should be.

#### Pointing to Christ
Psalm 148 calls everything in heaven and on earth to praise the LORD, and the New Testament says that this is exactly where history is heading. Paul says that at the name of Jesus "every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth" (Phil 2:10). John hears "every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea" praising the Lamb (Rev 5:13), the very list of this psalm. Jesus is the Word by whom all things were created (John 1:3; Col 1:16), and when he came, the winds and the sea obeyed him. He is the "horn of salvation" that God "raised up… in the house of his servant David," as Zechariah sang at the birth of John the Baptist (Luke 1:69). And through him the Gentiles who "were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ" (Eph 2:13). The people "near to him" now include believers from every nation, and they lead the song all creation will one day sing.
