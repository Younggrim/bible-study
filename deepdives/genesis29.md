*Setting:* Jacob has left Bethel with God's promise ringing in his ears: "I am with you and will keep you wherever you go" (28:15). He is heading for Haran, in upper Mesopotamia (now southeastern Turkey), where his grandfather Abraham's family stayed when Abraham went on to Canaan (11:31–12:4). It is roughly 450 miles from Beersheba. Two generations earlier, Abraham's servant made this same journey and found Rebekah at a well outside the town (ch 24). Now Rebekah's son arrives alone, with no camels and no gifts, only his staff (32:10). He will stay twenty years (31:38, 41). This chapter covers his welcome, his marriages and the first four of his sons. It is also where the deceiver of chapter 27 meets his match in his uncle Laban, and is paid back in kind.

#### Jacob Marries Leah and Rachel (vv1–30)
- **"Then Jacob went on his journey" (v1).** The Hebrew is literally "Jacob lifted up his feet." The Tyndale notes take it as a fresh spring in his step after Bethel, and JFB reads it the same way. "The people of the east" is a broad term for the peoples east and northeast of Canaan, here the Arameans around Haran.
- **The well in the field (vv2–3).** A well in the open country was a shared resource, and its mouth was capped with a heavy stone to protect the water from dust, animals and theft. The local custom was to wait until every flock had arrived, then roll the stone away together, water all the sheep and replace it. That made sure everyone got a fair share and no one used the water alone.
- **A well scene again.** Genesis readers have seen this before. Abraham's servant met Rebekah at a well (24:11–27), and later Moses will meet Zipporah at a well (Exod 2:15–21). A traveler far from home, a young woman coming to draw water, water given, a run home with the news, a welcome and a marriage. The pattern invites comparison. Abraham's servant prayed before anything happened (24:12–14). Jacob does not pray, yet God's providence brings Rachel to the well at exactly the right moment. The Tyndale notes see the timing as God leading Jacob toward the promises. Jesus' meeting with the Samaritan woman at "Jacob's well" deliberately echoes this pattern (John 4:5–26).
- **"My brothers, where do you come from?" (v4).** The shepherds answer, "We are from Haran." Jacob asks after "Laban the son of Nahor" (v5). Laban was actually Nahor's grandson, the son of Bethuel (24:29), but "son" in Hebrew often means descendant, and Nahor was the family's head. "Is it well with him?" (v6) is literally "Is there *shalom* to him?" The shepherds' reply turns all eyes to Rachel.
- **"It is still high day" (v7).** Jacob, a shepherd himself, sees men idling at midday and tells them to water and go. It may also be that he wants to meet Rachel without an audience. The shepherds explain the custom (v8). The Tyndale notes contrast their idleness with Jacob's energy.
- **"Rachel came with her father's sheep, for she was a shepherdess" (v9).** Her name means "ewe," as the lexicon notes. A daughter tending the flock was ordinary for a family of herders (compare Exod 2:16). Henry approves: "Nobody needs to be ashamed of honest, useful labour."
- **"Jacob came near and rolled the stone" (v10).** The custom said all the shepherds rolled it together, and JFB says such stones could need two or three men. Jacob does it alone. It is a hint of his strength and a preview of the night he will wrestle with God (32:24). Notice the phrase "Laban his mother's brother," repeated three times in one verse. Jacob sees family, and the narrator keeps pointing to Laban.
- **"Jacob kissed Rachel and wept aloud" (v11).** A kiss was a normal greeting between relatives (v13; 33:4). His tears are those of a man who fled for his life and has finally found his own people. Then he introduces himself, as "her father's kinsman" and "Rebekah's son" (v12). Rachel runs home, as Rebekah once did (24:28).
- **"Laban... ran to meet him" (v13).** The last time Laban ran to meet a visitor, it was after seeing the gold jewelry on his sister's arms (24:29–30). Some readers see the same eye for profit here, and Guzik suggests Laban knew Isaac's heir might bring wealth. If so, he is disappointed, because Jacob arrives empty-handed. "Jacob told Laban all these things." Exactly how much he told about Esau and the blessing is not said.
- **"Surely you are my bone and my flesh!" (v14).** This is family language. It echoes Adam's words about Eve (2:23), and later the tribes say it to David (2 Sam 5:1). Laban treats Jacob as one of the household. After "a month," though, he turns to business.
- **"Should you therefore serve me for nothing?" (v15).** It sounds generous. It also turns a nephew into a hired worker. The word "serve" (*avad*), from the same root as "servant," runs through chapters 29–31. Jacob, who was promised that his brother would serve him (27:29), spends twenty years serving his uncle.
- **"Leah's eyes were weak, but Rachel was beautiful in form and appearance" (v17).** The word for Leah's eyes is *rak*, which the lexicon gives as "tender, delicate, soft." It can mean "weak" (the ESV footnote offers "soft"). Some take it as a defect, others as gentle eyes that lacked sparkle. Either way, the contrast with Rachel is clear. The words for Rachel's beauty are the same ones used of Joseph, her son (39:6). Leah's name may mean "wild cow," as the lexicon suggests with some hesitation, and Rachel's means "ewe." Both are herdsmen's names.
- **"I will serve you seven years for your younger daughter Rachel" (v18).** A man normally paid a bride price (*mohar*) to the bride's family (34:12; Exod 22:16–17). Jacob has no money, so he offers work. The Tyndale notes call seven years a very high price. Laban's answer, "It is better that I give her to you than... to any other man" (v19), was a normal preference for marrying within the family. But it is vague, and Laban never repeats Rachel's name.
- **"They seemed to him but a few days because of the love he had for her" (v20).** This is one of the tenderest lines in Genesis. There is also an irony. Rebekah had told Jacob to stay with Laban "a few days" (27:44), and the same Hebrew phrase appears here. Her "few days" are turning into years.
- **"Give me my wife" (v21).** Jacob's request is blunt, and the narrator does not soften it. Laban throws a feast for "all the people of the place" (v22). Wedding feasts in that world could last a week (v27; Judg 14:12) and involved a great deal of wine.
- **"In the evening he took his daughter Leah" (v23).** How could Jacob not know? The bride was brought to the groom after dark, probably veiled (compare 24:65), after a day of feasting. The Tyndale notes trace the exact parallels with chapter 27. Isaac was fooled through food and wine, his blindness, borrowed clothes and touch. Jacob is fooled through feasting, darkness, a veil and touch. Leah must have cooperated, or at least been unable to refuse her father. The text says nothing of her feelings or Rachel's.
- **"Laban gave his female servant Zilpah to his daughter Leah" (v24).** The parenthesis seems small, but it matters. A father often gave his daughter a female servant as part of her dowry (compare 24:59, 61). Zilpah and Bilhah (v29) will become mothers of four of Israel's tribes (30:3–13).
- **"And in the morning, behold, it was Leah!" (v25).** The Hebrew is just as abrupt: "and behold, she was Leah." Jacob's protest uses phrases from his own past. "What is this you have done to me?" is what Pharaoh and Abimelech said to Abraham and Isaac when they were caught in lies (12:18; 20:9; 26:10). "Why then have you deceived me?" uses the verb *ramah*. The lexicon confirms it comes from the same root as Isaac's word for what Jacob did to him: "Your brother came deceitfully" (*mirmah*, 27:35).
- **"It is not so done in our country, to give the younger before the firstborn" (v26).** This is the sting. In Jacob's country, the younger *had* pushed in before the firstborn. Jacob had stood before his blind father and said, "I am Esau your firstborn" (27:19). Now the older sister has stood in for the younger in the dark. Laban's excuse may well reflect a real local custom, but he chose to keep quiet about it for seven years. Jacob does not argue back. The Tyndale notes describe it as God giving the deceiver a taste of his own deception.
- **"Complete the week of this one" (v27).** Jacob must finish the seven-day wedding celebration with Leah. Then Rachel is given to him too, at the price of another seven years' work, served after the wedding. Jacob ends up married to two sisters within a single week. The later law of Moses forbids exactly this, taking a woman's sister as a rival wife while she lives (Lev 18:18). The chapter shows why.
- **"He loved Rachel more than Leah" (v30).** This one sentence sets up the rest of the story. Jacob grew up in a home where "Isaac loved Esau, but Rebekah loved Jacob" (25:28), and now he repeats the pattern. Later he will love Joseph, Rachel's son, more than all his other sons (37:3), with terrible results. The Tyndale notes trace much of the later strife among the tribes to this favoritism.
- **What about polygamy?** Genesis never commands it or praises it. It simply reports it and shows the damage it causes: Sarah and Hagar (ch 16), Rachel and Leah here, and later Hannah and Peninnah (1 Sam 1:6). The ideal stands at the start, "they shall become one flesh" (2:24), and Jesus goes back to it (Matt 19:4–6). The law regulated plural marriage, for example by protecting the son of an unloved wife (Deut 21:15–17), but it never presented it as God's design.
- **Commentators:** All three see the deceiver deceived. Henry: "Jacob was paid in his own coin," while still insisting that "this was Laban's sin; he wronged both Jacob and Rachel." On v20 he writes, "Love makes long and hard services short and easy." On polygamy he grants that it was tolerated then, but says "it will by no means justify the like practice now." Guzik calls the fourteen years Jacob's training in the school of hard experience, and stresses that God's good purposes did not excuse Laban. He notes that the veil and the darkness made the switch possible, and that Leah and Rachel were under their father's authority. JFB explains that "brother" was used loosely for any relative (v12), and treats Jacob's service as standing in for a bride price. JFB has no separate notes on vv5–7, 13 or 22–27, and covers the wedding customs together under v21.

#### Jacob's Children (vv31–35)
- **The section continues.** The ESV heading "Jacob's Children" runs on to 30:24. These five verses give Leah's first four sons, and chapter 30 completes the list. The births come in a rush, with no time markers. It all happens within the second seven years (compare 30:25–26).
- **"When the LORD saw that Leah was hated" (v31).** "Hated" is *sane*, and the lexicon notes that it is often set against "love" and is used for the "unloved" wife in a two-wife household (Deut 21:15–17). Jacob did not necessarily hate Leah in the modern sense, but she was rejected in favor of her sister. JFB calls it being loved less than she ought to have been. Jesus uses the same kind of comparison when he speaks of "hating" family (Luke 14:26 with Matt 10:37). It was still a heavy grief. The LORD saw her. As with Sarah, Rebekah and later Hannah, children are God's gift: "he opened her womb, but Rachel was barren."
- **Reuben (v32).** The ESV footnote says Reuben means "See, a son." Leah explains it with a near-rhyme: "the LORD has looked upon my affliction" (in Hebrew *ra'ah be'onyi*). It echoes Hagar, who learned that God sees the mistreated (16:11, 13). Her hope is plain: "now my husband will love me."
- **Simeon (v33).** The name sounds like the Hebrew for "heard," says the ESV footnote. "The LORD has heard that I am hated." Leah has moved from being seen to being heard, but she still uses the word "hated." Her circumstances have not changed.
- **Levi (v34).** The name sounds like "attached," again per the ESV footnote. The lexicon says the root of the name is uncertain but notes that the text plays on "joined." "Now this time my husband will be attached to me." The hope gets smaller: not love now, just closeness. Later, Numbers plays on the same word when the Levites are "joined" to Aaron in serving the LORD (Num 18:2, 4). Leah's son would be attached not to a husband's affection but to God's service.
- **Judah (v35).** "This time I will praise the LORD." For the first time Leah does not mention Jacob at all. The Tyndale notes suggest she has stopped looking to her husband to fill her need and found her comfort in God. Jacob will later bless this son with a play on the same name: "Judah, your brothers shall praise you" (49:8).
- **What God did with the unloved wife.** From Leah came Levi, the priestly tribe, and Judah, the royal tribe of David. Through Judah came the Messiah (Matt 1:2–3; Rev 5:5). Rachel's sons, Joseph and Benjamin, were deeply important, but the line of kings and of Christ ran through the wife Jacob did not choose. At the end of his life Jacob asked to be buried beside Leah, not Rachel (49:31). The book of Ruth remembers both sisters as the women "who together built up the house of Israel" (Ruth 4:11).
- **"Then she ceased bearing" (v35).** This is a pause, not the end. Leah will bear two more sons and a daughter (30:17–21). The pause is what pushes Rachel to the desperate measures of chapter 30.
- **Commentators:** Guzik calls Leah the innocent party and points to God as a husband to the forsaken (Isa 54:5). He notes that the priestly and royal tribes, and so the Messiah, came through Leah, not Rachel. Henry notes how Leah "thankfully acknowledges the kind providence of God in hearing her," and reads v35 as her resolve: "Now will I praise the Lord more and better than I have done." JFB treats "hated" as comparative, and notes that the boys' names record Leah's own feelings at each birth. All three agree that God's kindness to Leah stands out against Jacob's neglect.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two threads in this chapter meet in Jesus. One is justice. Jacob reaps what he sowed (Gal 6:7), and the story does not pretend his deception was harmless. The other is grace to the unwanted. The LORD "saw" Leah, "heard" her, and from the wife who was not chosen he brought Judah, whose line leads to the "Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David" (Rev 5:5). Jesus was not born from the line of the beautiful, favored wife but from the one who was overlooked. The same God who saw Leah's affliction later sent his Son to sit by Jacob's well and speak to a Samaritan woman who had been married five times (John 4:16–18). He offered her not another husband but living water. Leah moved from "now my husband will love me" to "this time I will praise the LORD." That is the turn the gospel invites: from looking for worth in human love to finding it in the God who sees.
