*Setting:* Judah, probably during the reign of Ahaz or early in Hezekiah's reign, about 730–715 BC. Chapter 1 announced that the LORD was coming down in judgment, first on Samaria and then on the towns of Judah all the way to Jerusalem's gate. Chapter 2 gives the reason in plain terms. The sin is not only idolatry but greed: powerful men in Jerusalem were taking the family farms of ordinary Israelites, the kind of people Micah grew up among in the countryside. When the prophet objected, they told him to stop preaching. The chapter ends the first round of Micah's book with a sudden, bright promise of a Shepherd-King who will gather his flock and break out at their head. The ESV gives it a single heading.

#### Woe to the Oppressors (vv1–13)
- **"Woe to those who devise wickedness and work evil on their beds!" (v1).** "Woe" (*hoy*) was the cry of mourners at a funeral (1 Kgs 13:30). Micah sings a funeral song over people who are still alive. They lie awake at night planning their schemes, the opposite of the godly man who meditates on God's law "day and night" (Ps 1:2; compare Ps 36:4).
- **"When the morning dawns, they perform it, because it is in the power of their hand" (v1).** Their only rule is what they can get away with. Might, not right. Guzik suggests the morning may also hint at the courts held at the city gate at dawn, where the powerful bent the law their way. The real test of character is what someone does when they have the power to sin and no one can stop them.
- **"They covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away" (v2).** "Covet" is *chamad*, the word of the tenth commandment: "You shall not covet your neighbor's house… or anything that is your neighbor's" (Exod 20:17). Coveting starts in the heart and ends in seizure. Isaiah, preaching in the same city, said the same: "Woe to those who join house to house, who add field to field, until there is no more room" (Isa 5:8).
- **"They oppress a man and his house, a man and his inheritance" (v2).** In Israel land was not a commodity. God had divided it among the tribes and families under Joshua, and it was meant to stay in the family for good: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine" (Lev 25:23). The Jubilee every fiftieth year was designed to return land to its original families. To take a man's inheritance was to take his family's place in God's people. The classic case is Naboth, who refused to sell his vineyard to King Ahab, "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers," and was murdered for it (1 Kgs 21:3).
- **"Therefore thus says the LORD: behold, against this family I am devising disaster" (v3).** The same two Hebrew words come back. They "devise" (*chashab*) "evil" (*ra'*, v1). Now God "devises" *ra'ah*, which means both moral evil and disaster. The Tyndale notes point out the wordplay. They plotted evil, and God plots evil for them, in the sense of calamity. The punishment fits the crime. **"This family"** sounds like a whole clan, but it means the whole nation (Amos 3:1).
- **"From which you cannot remove your necks, and you shall not walk haughtily" (v3).** The picture is a yoke, the wooden beam laid across the necks of oxen. Proud men who walked with heads high will be bowed under a conqueror's yoke.
- **"They shall take up a taunt song against you… 'We are utterly ruined; he changes the portion of my people'" (v4).** Enemies will mock them by repeating their own lament in a sing-song voice. The land grabbers will wail that their land has been taken and given to others, exactly what they did to their neighbours. **"To an apostate he allots our fields."** The word (*shobeb*) means a turncoat or rebel, probably the pagan conqueror. JFB mentions Maurer's rendering, "a rebel."
- **"You will have none to cast the line by lot in the assembly of the LORD" (v5).** When Israel entered the land, the tribes' shares were measured out with a cord and assigned by lot (Josh 14:2; 18:6–10; Ps 16:6, "The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places"). When God restores his people and divides the land again, these men will have no descendants there to receive a share. Those who stole other men's inheritance will lose their own.
- **"'Do not preach'—thus they preach—'one should not preach of such things'" (v6).** The Hebrew word is *nataph*, "to drip," used of prophetic speech as words dripping out (Amos 7:16; Ezek 21:2). It may carry a sneer, like "stop your drivel" or "stop your prattle." Micah's opponents "preach" at him to stop preaching. The word appears five times in vv6 and 11, and the repetition mocks them. **"Disgrace will not overtake us."** They believed that because they were God's covenant people, with his temple in their city, judgment could not touch them (compare 3:11, "Is not the LORD in the midst of us? No disaster shall come upon us").
- **"Should this be said, O house of Jacob? Has the LORD grown impatient?" (v7).** "Grown impatient" is literally "Is the Spirit of the LORD shortened?" (the ESV footnotes it). It could mean "Has God's patience run out?" or "Is God's power limited?" Their slogan was that the LORD would never act like this. Micah answers: **"Are these his deeds?"** Judgment is not God's preferred work. Their sin brought it.
- **"Do not my words do good to him who walks uprightly?" (v7).** God's word is not the problem. For anyone walking straight, it is good news. The only people who find the prophet's message unbearable are those it accuses.
- **"But lately my people have risen up as an enemy" (v8).** The Hebrew here is difficult, and translations differ, but the sense is clear: the people of God have become an enemy army toward their own countrymen. **"You strip the rich robe from those who pass by trustingly with no thought of war."** They treat peaceful travellers as if they were prisoners of war, taking even the cloak off a man's back. The law said a poor man's cloak taken as a pledge had to be returned before nightfall, because it was his blanket (Exod 22:26–27).
- **"The women of my people you drive out from their delightful houses" (v9).** These are probably widows, whose homes were the easiest to seize because no man stood up for them. God's law singled out widows and orphans for protection (Exod 22:22–24; Deut 10:18). **"From their young children you take away my splendor forever."** "My splendor" is God's blessing resting on Israel's children: their share in the land, their freedom, perhaps their place in God's people. Taking a child's inheritance was robbing God.
- **"Arise and go, for this is no place to rest" (v10).** The land was meant to be Israel's "rest" (Deut 12:9; Ps 95:11). Now God tells the oppressors to get up and leave. The words may echo what they said to the widows they evicted. **"Because of uncleanness that destroys with a grievous destruction."** The law warned that a defiled land would "vomit out" its people as it had the Canaanites (Lev 18:25–28). Their sin had made the promised land unlivable.
- **"If a man should go about and utter wind and lies… 'I will preach to you of wine and strong drink,' he would be the preacher for this people!" (v11).** Here is the bitter punchline. "Wind" (*ruach*) can also mean "spirit," a jab at prophets who claimed the Spirit but spoke hot air. A preacher who promises good harvests, plenty of wine and good times would draw a crowd. Paul says the same of every age: people will "accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" (2 Tim 4:3).
- **"I will surely assemble all of you, O Jacob; I will gather the remnant of Israel" (v12).** Without warning the tone changes completely. This is Micah's pattern: every round of judgment ends in hope. **"Remnant"** (*she'erit*) is the group God preserves through judgment, a major theme in Micah (4:7; 5:7–8; 7:18) and in his contemporary Isaiah, who named a son Shear-jashub, "a remnant shall return" (Isa 7:3).
- **"Like sheep in a fold, like a flock in its pasture, a noisy multitude of men" (v12).** "Fold" may be the name Bozrah, a town in Edom famous for sheep, whose name means "enclosure"; the ESV and most translations take it as "fold." The scattered flock is gathered into one pen, and there are so many that they make a racket. The remnant is small compared with the nation, but large in God's hands.
- **Readings differ on vv12–13.** A few interpreters think these verses continue the false prophets' smooth words from v11, which Micah then demolishes in chapter 3. Most, including all three commentators here and the Tyndale notes, read them as God's genuine promise, matching the pattern of hope after judgment in 4:1–8 and 7:8–20.
- **"He who opens the breach goes up before them" (v13).** The Hebrew is one word, *happorets*, "the breaker," from *parats*, to break through. Picture sheep penned inside a stone fold, or captives inside an enemy city. Someone breaks a hole in the wall, and the whole flock pours out after him. The same verb named Perez, the twin who "broke out" first (Gen 38:29) and became an ancestor of David and of Jesus (Matt 1:3). David used it too, when the LORD "broke through" his enemies "like a breaking flood" (2 Sam 5:20).
- **"They break through and pass the gate, going out by it" (v13).** This is a new exodus. The remnant is led out of captivity through the gate of the enemy city, as Israel was once led out of Egypt with God going before them (Exod 13:21).
- **"Their king passes on before them, the LORD at their head" (v13).** The breaker, the king and the LORD stand in parallel. The one who breaks open the way is the King, and the King is the LORD himself. Later Jewish interpreters sometimes saw the breaker as Elijah and the king as the Messiah. Christian readers have long seen here a picture of Christ. The Tyndale notes say the deliverance from exile points ahead to the greater freedom Jesus gives.
- **Commentators:** Henry sums up the charge as "covetousness and oppression, fraudulent and violent practices," and the treatment of the vulnerable as "dealing barbarously, even with women and children, and other harmless people." He notes the people's "delighting in false prophets," and reads vv12–13 as "gracious promises of comfort, reserved for the good people among them, in the Messiah." JFB traces three stages of sin in v1, devising, working out and executing. He reads the "breaker" as "Jehovah-Messiah" breaking through every obstacle in the way of his people's restoration, and the King at their head as the Messiah. JFB thinks the return from Babylon fulfilled this only in part. Guzik stresses that planned sin is worse than sin of impulse. With Spurgeon, he argues that the people, not God, were the limit on what God would do among them. Like JFB, he takes "the Breaker" as a title for Jesus, the one who goes ahead of his flock and clears the way. The lexicon supports the sense "one who breaks through" from an enclosure (BDB lists Mic 2:13 under breaking out of an enclosure).

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter ends with a flock penned in, a wall broken open, and the King going out at their head. Jesus took up the picture: "I am the door of the sheep… If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture," and the good shepherd "goes before them, and the sheep follow him" (John 10:4, 9). He is the Breaker. He broke out of the tomb first, "the firstborn from the dead" (Col 1:18), and opened a way through the last enemy for everyone who follows him (Heb 2:14–15). And where the powerful of Micah's day stole the inheritance of the poor, Christ makes the poor heirs: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matt 5:5).
