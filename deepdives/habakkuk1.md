*Setting:* Habakkuk is a prophet we know almost nothing about outside his book. He probably preached in Judah around 609–605 BC, in the early reign of King Jehoiakim. Josiah's reforms had ended with his death at Megiddo in 609 BC, and his son Jehoiakim undid much of what his father had done. Jeremiah describes him as a king who built his palace with unpaid labor and shed innocent blood (Jer 22:13–17). Meanwhile, the world was changing fast. Nineveh fell in 612 BC, and in 605 BC the Babylonians crushed Egypt at Carchemish and became masters of the whole region. Habakkuk's book is unusual among the prophets. Most prophets speak for God to the people. Habakkuk speaks to God about the people, and then about God's own ways. The book is a conversation: a complaint, an answer, a second complaint, a second answer (ch 2), and finally a prayer (ch 3). It begins with "how long?" and ends with "yet I will rejoice."

#### Habakkuk's Complaint (vv1–4)
- **"The oracle that Habakkuk the prophet saw" (v1).** "Oracle" is *massa'*, the word Nahum also used (Nah 1:1). It can mean a "burden," a heavy message to carry. Guzik says that here the burden is double: a heavy message, and heavy questions. Habakkuk is one of only three Old Testament prophets called "the prophet" in his book's opening line (with Haggai and Zechariah). Some think that means he served officially at the temple. Chapter 3 is written like a psalm with musical directions, which fits that idea.
- **The name Habakkuk.** It appears nowhere else in the Bible. It probably comes from the Hebrew verb *chabaq*, "to embrace." Guzik takes it to mean "one who embraces" or "one who clings," a fitting name for a man who holds on to God through his questions. Some scholars instead connect it with an Akkadian word for a garden plant. A later legend in the Apocrypha (Bel and the Dragon) has Habakkuk carried by an angel to bring food to Daniel in the lions' den, but that story is not historical.
- **"O LORD, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear?" (v2).** "How long?" is the cry of the lament psalms (Ps 13:1–2; 74:10; 90:13). It is not unbelief. A person only cries "how long?" to someone they still expect to answer. The Tyndale notes point out that crying out and being answered is a mark of a close relationship with God (Ps 145:18).
- **"Or cry to you 'Violence!' and you will not save?" (v2).** "Violence" (*chamas*) was the word a person would shout when attacked, like crying "Help! Police!" Job uses it (Job 19:7), and Jeremiah (Jer 20:8). Habakkuk has been shouting it to God, and nothing seems to happen.
- **"Why do you make me see iniquity, and why do you idly look at wrong?" (v3).** Habakkuk puts two complaints side by side. God makes *him* see the evil, and God himself only *looks*. Guzik suggests reasons God lets believers see evil: to keep them humble, to deepen their hatred of sin, to make grace more wonderful and to move them to reach others. The verbs "see" and "look" will come back in God's answer (v5) and in Habakkuk's second complaint (v13).
- **"Destruction and violence are before me; strife and contention arise" (v3).** This is life inside Judah, not an outside enemy. The Tyndale notes connect it with the moral collapse of Jehoiakim's reign.
- **"So the law is paralyzed" (v4).** The verb (*pug*) means to grow numb or cold. It describes Jacob's heart when he heard Joseph was alive and could not take it in (Gen 45:26). The *torah*, God's law, has gone numb. It is still on the books, but it has lost its grip. JFB explains it simply: "Decisions are given contrary to right."
- **"For the wicked surround the righteous; so justice goes forth perverted" (v4).** "Perverted" means twisted or bent. Courts were supposed to protect the weak (Deut 16:18–20). Instead the wicked hem in honest people, and judgment comes out crooked.
- **Commentators:** Henry calls this a very melancholy complaint about the decay of religion and righteousness, and he hears Habakkuk saying, "I have cried long; but thou wilt not hear, thou wilt not save." He reads the problem as the abuse of the sword of justice among God's own people. JFB defines the "burden" as "the prophetic sentence." Guzik dates the book to about 607 BC, suggests Habakkuk had lived through Josiah's revival and then watched it collapse, and draws on Spurgeon for the earnestness that prayer like this requires.

#### The Lord's Answer (vv5–11)
- **"Look among the nations, and see; wonder and be astounded" (v5).** Habakkuk complained that God made him look at evil in Judah. God tells him to look further, at the nations. The Tyndale notes point out that the verbs pick up the words of v3, so God answers the prophet in his own terms. The "you" in this verse is plural. The message is for all Judah.
- **"For I am doing a work in your days that you would not believe if told" (v5).** God is not idle (v3). He is already at work. But the work is unbelievable. Guzik calls it a work too bad to be true. Paul quoted this verse in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch as a warning to those who might reject the gospel: "Look, you scoffers, be astounded and perish; for I am doing a work in your days, a work that you will not believe" (Acts 13:41). Paul used the Greek Septuagint, which reads "scoffers" in place of "among the nations," which comes from a slightly different reading of the Hebrew letters. JFB notes the quotation.
- **"For behold, I am raising up the Chaldeans" (v6).** The Chaldeans were the people of southern Mesopotamia whose kings now ruled Babylon. Nabopolassar declared Babylon's independence from Assyria in 626 BC, and his son Nebuchadnezzar would become the greatest king of the age. In Habakkuk's early years Babylon might still have seemed a rising local power. The answer to Judah's injustice is that God will bring an even more ruthless nation against it.
- **"That bitter and hasty nation" (v6).** "Bitter" means fierce and cruel. "Hasty" means impetuous, rushing on without pause. JFB defines the words the same way. **"To seize dwellings not their own"** describes an empire of conquest.
- **"Their justice and dignity go forth from themselves" (v7).** This is the key to the Chaldeans' character. They answer to no law but their own. They decide what is right, and they are their own source of honor. The Tyndale notes say Babylon offered no relief from injustice, only its own law. Habakkuk had complained that "justice never goes forth" (v4). Now he hears of a people whose "justice… goes forth from themselves."
- **"Their horses are swifter than leopards, more fierce than the evening wolves" (v8).** Three hunting animals picture the cavalry: the leopard for speed, the wolf for savagery, and the eagle for swooping on its prey. The "evening wolves" are wolves that have gone hungry all day, which JFB describes as "famished with fasting all day." Zephaniah uses the same picture for Jerusalem's own judges (Zeph 3:3). The eagle recalls a covenant curse in Deuteronomy: "a nation from far away… swooping down like the eagle, a nation whose language you do not understand" (Deut 28:49). What Moses warned of is arriving.
- **"They all come for violence, all their faces forward" (v9).** Habakkuk cried "Violence!" (v2). God answers that violence (*chamas*) is coming. The next words are hard to translate. The ESV's "all their faces forward" pictures an army pressing on without turning aside. The KJV reads "their faces shall sup up as the east wind," and JFB connects that with the scorching desert wind that withers everything. **"They gather captives like sand"** means countless prisoners.
- **"At kings they scoff… they laugh at every fortress, for they pile up earth and take it" (v10).** Siege ramps were the standard way to take walled cities: soldiers heaped earth and rubble against the wall until they could roll their engines up to it (2 Kgs 19:32). The Assyrian siege ramp at Lachish, built in 701 BC, can still be seen today. The Babylonians would build ramps against Jerusalem (Jer 32:24).
- **"Then they sweep by like the wind and go on" (v11).** The army passes through like a storm and moves on to the next conquest.
- **"Guilty men, whose own might is their god!" (v11).** This is the root of their guilt. They make their own power their god. Nebuchadnezzar would one day stand on his palace roof and say, "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power… for the glory of my majesty?" and lose his mind that very hour (Dan 4:30–31). JFB draws exactly that connection. The Tyndale notes say Babylon trusted its military power as another idol alongside its many gods. God uses the Chaldeans, but he does not excuse them. They are "guilty."
- **Commentators:** Henry reads vv5–11 as God's answer announcing the Chaldean army as the punishment for Judah's injustice. JFB sees in v5 a warning to the Jews and notes that Paul quotes it with the Septuagint's different wording: "The despisers are to be given up to their own stupefaction, and so perish." He also ties v11 to Nebuchadnezzar's boast in Daniel 4. Guzik stresses that God does not plant conquest in Babylon's heart. Babylon already longs to conquer, and God permits that desire and uses it. He also notes that Babylon will give its own false gods the credit for its power.

#### Habakkuk's Second Complaint (vv12–17)
- **"Are you not from everlasting, O LORD my God, my Holy One?" (v12).** Before Habakkuk raises his second, harder question, he reminds himself who God is. The Tyndale notes highlight the personal words, "my God, my Holy One." Guzik passes on Martyn Lloyd-Jones's advice for facing a problem like this: stop and think, restate the basic truths about God, apply them to the problem, and leave it with God in faith. That is exactly what Habakkuk does here.
- **"We shall not die" (v12).** God is eternal and he is in covenant with Israel, so Israel will not be wiped out. Discipline, yes; annihilation, no. JFB notes that Jewish scribal tradition lists this among a small group of places where the scribes changed the wording out of reverence. The original may have read "you shall not die," addressed to God, and scribes softened it because the idea of God dying, even denied, seemed improper. Most translations keep "we shall not die," and both readings make good sense.
- **"O LORD, you have ordained them as a judgment, and you, O Rock, have established them for reproof" (v12).** Habakkuk grasps the answer: Babylon is God's instrument of discipline, as Assyria had been (Isa 10:5). "Reproof" means correction, not destruction, and Henry stresses that God's people are corrected, not destroyed, by their enemies. "Rock" (*tsur*) is an old title for God's steadiness and faithfulness (Deut 32:4, 15; Ps 18:2). The Tyndale notes point ahead to Christ as the rock (1 Cor 10:4).
- **"You who are of purer eyes than to see evil and cannot look at wrong" (v13).** This does not mean God cannot see evil. He sees everything. It means he cannot look on it with approval or tolerate it. That is what makes the problem so sharp, as Guzik says: if God is this holy, why does he seem to put up with Babylon? There is irony in the verbs. Habakkuk began by asking why God "idly looks" at wrong in Judah (v3). Now he asks why God "idly looks" at traitors in Babylon. Henry insists that God does not approve of the wickedness he permits.
- **"Why do you… remain silent when the wicked swallows up the man more righteous than he?" (v13).** Habakkuk is not saying Judah is innocent. He has just described its corruption (vv2–4). He is saying Judah is "more righteous" than Babylon. Guzik puts the prophet's protest in plain terms: the cure seems worse than the disease. Many believers have asked the same question. Why does God allow those who are worse to triumph over those who are less bad? Asaph asked it too (Ps 73:2–14).
- **"You make mankind like the fish of the sea, like crawling things that have no ruler" (v14).** Habakkuk addresses God directly. "You make" — he still holds God responsible. Without a protector, people are like fish with no shepherd, easy prey for whoever casts a net.
- **"He brings all of them up with a hook; he drags them out with his net" (v15).** Babylon is the fisherman. The image was not new. A Sumerian victory monument from about 2450 BC, the Stele of the Vultures, shows a god holding the king's enemies trapped in a great net. Amos pictured Israel's captives being led off with fishhooks (Amos 4:2). Guzik passes on Adam Clarke's picture of Nebuchadnezzar as a fisherman who never fails to catch something. **"So he rejoices and is glad."** The conqueror enjoys it.
- **"Therefore he sacrifices to his net and makes offerings to his dragnet" (v16).** This picks up v11: their might is their god. The net stands for the army and its weapons. Whether Babylonians literally offered sacrifices to their weapons is uncertain, but the point is plain. They worship the tools of their success. Henry calls it self-idolatry. JFB says they "idolize themselves." The Tyndale notes point to John's warning: "Little children, keep yourselves from idols" (1 John 5:21).
- **"Is he then to keep on emptying his net and mercilessly killing nations forever?" (v17).** The chapter ends with a question hanging in the air. Will it ever stop? Habakkuk does not answer it himself. He takes his question to the watchtower and waits for God (2:1).
- **Commentators:** Henry draws comfort from v12: because God is from everlasting, "then we shall not die," and his people are disciplined, not destroyed. On v16 he sees the pride of those who give their own nets the credit. JFB mentions the scribal tradition behind "we shall not die" and calls God the Rock who stays unshaken. Guzik sets out the second problem sharply: God's holiness makes his use of Babylon harder, not easier, to understand. His help for readers comes through Lloyd-Jones's steps: think, recall what is known of God, apply it, and leave it with him.

#### Pointing to Christ
Habakkuk asks why a holy God, whose eyes are too pure to look on evil, seems to stay silent while the wicked swallow the righteous. The cross is where that question is answered most deeply. There the most righteous man who ever lived was swallowed up by the wicked, and God seemed silent. Jesus himself cried a lament psalm, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matt 27:46). Yet God was not idle. He was doing "a work in your days that you would not believe if told" (v5), judging sin and saving sinners in one act. Paul offered that very work to his hearers in Acts 13:38–41: forgiveness through Jesus, and a warning not to scoff at it.
