*Setting:* Around 1100–1050 BC. Chapter 3 ended with God speaking again at Shiloh through the young prophet Samuel, and with a terrible word: God was about to do something in Israel "at which the two ears of everyone who hears it will tingle" (3:11). Chapter 4 is that event. The enemy is the Philistines, a seafaring people who had settled on the southern coastal plain of Canaan around 1200 BC. The Bible says they came from Caphtor, probably Crete (Amos 9:7), and they ruled from five cities: Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath and Ekron. They had better weapons and organization than Israel's tribes, and they had already oppressed Israel in Samson's day (Judg 13:1). Samuel himself disappears from the story from 4:1b until 7:3. The ark narrative (4:1–7:2) shows what happens when God's people try to use God rather than obey him.

#### The Philistines Capture the Ark (vv1–11)
- **"And the word of Samuel came to all Israel" (v1).** This line belongs with the end of chapter 3: God's word, once rare, now reaches the whole nation through Samuel. But notice what happens next. Israel goes to war, and there is no word from Samuel and no record that anyone asked God. The Greek translation adds that the Philistines gathered against Israel first. JFB calls Israel's rush to battle "rash and inconsiderate," and says "the Philistines were the aggressors."
- **Ebenezer and Aphek (v1).** Aphek lay at the head of the Yarkon River, near the coast highway, at the northern edge of Philistine reach and about 20 miles west of Shiloh. Ebenezer, Israel's camp, was nearby, perhaps at Izbet Sartah in the hills just east. The name "Ebenezer" means "stone of help." Here it is used ahead of time: Samuel will name a different stone "Ebenezer" after a later victory (7:12). The first battle near the "stone of help" ends in disaster.
- **"Why has the LORD defeated us today?" (v3).** The elders ask exactly the right question and then do not wait for the answer. In Joshua's day, defeat sent Joshua to his face before the ark to ask God why, and the answer was hidden sin (Josh 7:6–12). Here no one prays, no one asks, and no one repents. They reach for a solution instead.
- **"Let us bring the ark… that it may come among us and save us" (v3).** The ark of the covenant was a gold-covered chest holding the stone tablets of the law. It was the place where God promised to meet with Israel. The ESV's "it may come… and save us" could also be translated "*he* may come… and save us." Either way, the elders' thinking is that if the ark is present, God must act. It had gone before Israel at the Jordan and at Jericho (Josh 3–4; 6), but on God's command. Now Israel is treating the most sacred object in its worship like a good-luck charm, a box that guarantees victory. That is close to what the surrounding nations did with their idols, carrying them into battle.
- **"The LORD of hosts, who is enthroned on the cherubim" (v4).** The two golden cherubim on the ark's lid stretched out their wings, and God was pictured as invisibly enthroned above them, with the ark as his footstool (Ps 99:1; 1 Chr 28:2). This full, glorious title makes what follows all the more shocking. It is matched by a grim note: "the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were there with the ark." The King's throne is being carried into battle by men God has already sentenced to die (2:34).
- **"All Israel gave a mighty shout, so that the earth resounded" (v5).** This was the war cry (*teru'ah*) that went up when the walls of Jericho fell (Josh 6:20). It sounds like faith. It is not. Loud enthusiasm and genuine trust are not the same thing.
- **"A god has come into the camp" (vv6–8).** The Philistines are terrified. They know the stories of the Exodus, the "mighty gods" who "struck the Egyptians with every sort of plague." Their theology is muddled: they speak of Israel's God in the plural, as pagans naturally would, and they place the plagues "in the wilderness." But they take Israel's God more seriously than Israel does. The Tyndale notes point out that God will indeed strike the Philistines with plagues in the next two chapters.
- **"Take courage, and be men, O Philistines… be men and fight" (v9).** Their fear drives them not to surrender but to desperate courage. They have oppressed the Hebrews and do not want to become their slaves.
- **"Thirty thousand foot soldiers of Israel fell" (v10).** More than seven times the losses of the first battle (v2). The Hebrew word translated "thousand" (*'eleph*) can also mean a military unit or clan group, so some scholars think the numbers were smaller. Whatever the count, it was a national catastrophe. Psalm 78:59–64 remembers it as the day God "forsook his dwelling at Shiloh" and "delivered his power to captivity, his glory to the hand of the foe."
- **"The ark of God was captured, and the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, died" (v11).** In one day, Israel loses its army, its priests and the ark itself, and the sign given to Eli comes true exactly: "both of them shall die on the same day" (2:34). Shiloh was probably destroyed soon afterward. The ark never returned there, and centuries later Jeremiah could still point to its ruins as a warning: "Go now to my place that was in Shiloh… and see what I did to it" (Jer 7:12–14).
- **Commentators:** All three see the elders' plan as superstition. Henry calls it a "foolish project," bringing the ark into the camp "upon the shoulders of Hophni and Phinehas," and says it "struck a fear into the Philistines, but such a fear as roused them." JFB: "strange that they were so blind to the real cause of the disaster," which needed national repentance, not ceremony. Guzik argues that the ark itself was good, since God commanded it, but Israel turned it into an idol, and God will not be manipulated. He also warns that a great shout in worship proves nothing on its own. He mentions an ostracon (inscribed pottery piece) found at Izbet Sartah that some have read as referring to this event and even naming Hophni. Most scholars read that inscription as an alphabet practice text, so the claim should not be leaned on.

#### The Death of Eli (vv12–22)
- **"A man of Benjamin ran from the battle line and came to Shiloh the same day" (v12).** From Aphek to Shiloh is about 20 miles, much of it climbing into the hill country. Torn clothes and dirt on the head were the signs of mourning and disaster (Josh 7:6; 2 Sam 1:2). The people would know the news before he spoke. A Jewish tradition says the runner was Saul, a Benjamite, but there is nothing to support it.
- **"Eli was sitting on his seat by the road watching, for his heart trembled for the ark of God" (v13).** The "seat" is the same word as in 1:9, often used of a throne. Eli, who could not stop his sons, now sits by the road fearing for the ark that they carried out. To his credit, it is the ark he trembles for.
- **"Eli was ninety-eight years old and his eyes were set so that he could not see" (v15).** He hears the city cry out before anyone tells him. "My son," he says to the messenger (v16), the same words he used to Samuel.
- **The news, in four blows (v17).** The messenger reports in rising order of horror: Israel fled; a great slaughter among the people; your two sons are dead; and the ark of God has been captured.
- **"As soon as he mentioned the ark of God, Eli fell over backward… and his neck was broken" (v18).** It is the ark, not his sons, that kills him. "For the man was old and heavy." The word "heavy" (*kabed*) is the same root as "honor" or "glory" (*kabod*). God had charged Eli with honoring his sons above God by "fattening" himself on the offerings (2:29), and had warned "those who honor me I will honor" (2:30). The heavy man falls from his seat. "He had judged Israel forty years." Eli was priest and also judge, the last but one of the judges. Forty years is the length of several judges' terms (Judg 3:11; 5:31; 8:28), a round figure for a generation.
- **The wife of Phinehas (vv19–20).** The news sends her into labor, and she dies giving birth. The women try to comfort her with the joy that should have been hers: "Do not be afraid, for you have borne a son." Compare Rachel, who died giving birth to Benjamin (Gen 35:16–18). "She did not answer or pay attention." Her grief was past comforting.
- **"She named the child Ichabod, saying, 'The glory has departed from Israel!'" (v21).** *I-kabod* means "Where is the glory?" or "No glory." "Glory" (*kabod*) is the weight and splendor of God's presence, which once filled the tabernacle in the wilderness (Exod 40:34–35). "Departed" (*galah*) is the ordinary Hebrew word for going into exile. God's glory has been carried off as a captive into a foreign land. There is a bitter echo of 3:21, where God "revealed himself" (another form of *galah*) at Shiloh. The God who revealed himself there has now, it seems, gone into exile from there.
- **"For the ark of God has been captured" (v22).** She says it twice, and the second time she mentions only the ark. Her husband was a wicked priest, but she understood something the elders missed: what mattered was not winning battles, but God's presence with his people. Ichabod's family line continues, briefly: his nephew Ahijah later serves as priest under Saul (14:3).
- **Commentators:** JFB explains that Eli sat on "an official chair" by the gate "as a public magistrate," and that the news of the ark "proved to him a knell of death." He calls Eli a good man "in spite of his unhappy weaknesses," and says the infant's name "perpetuated the fallen glory of the church and nation." Henry says simply that "Eli fainted away, fell, and broke his neck." Guzik notes that Eli survived the news of his sons but not the news of the ark, which shows what he truly cared about. He also argues that the glory had already departed before the ark was captured, when Israel began trusting the box instead of the God it represented. The Tyndale notes draw out the wordplay between "heavy" and "glory."

#### Pointing to Christ
Israel learned at Aphek that God's presence cannot be carried around and used like a tool. The ark was a gift, but it was never a guarantee apart from trust and obedience. The glory "departed" in exile, and would depart again from the temple in Ezekiel's day (Ezek 10). But the Bible does not end with Ichabod. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). In Jesus, God's glory came to live among his people in a way no enemy could capture. He did allow himself to be handed over to his enemies, taken outside the city and killed, the true Ark seemingly lost. Three days later he rose, and he promised, "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (Matt 28:20).
