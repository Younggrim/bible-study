*Setting:* About 853 BC, late in Jehoshaphat's reign over Judah. He has sought God, taught the law in every town, and been given peace, wealth and a great army (ch 17). In the north, Ahab has ruled Israel for some twenty years with his wife Jezebel, a Phoenician princess who made Baal worship the official religion and tried to wipe out the LORD's prophets (1 Kgs 16:29–33; 18:4). Elijah has confronted them on Mount Carmel (1 Kgs 18). Ahab has also fought the Arameans of Damascus twice, and let their king Ben-hadad go free on promises he did not keep (1 Kgs 20:34). This chapter is copied almost word for word from 1 Kings 22:1–35. The Chronicler, who normally skips northern history, keeps the whole story because it shows the danger of a godly king of David's line tying himself to a godless one. He adds only a few touches, the most important in v31.

#### Jehoshaphat Allies with Ahab (vv1–3)
- **"Now Jehoshaphat had great riches and honor, and he made a marriage alliance with Ahab" (v1).** The two halves of the verse sit uneasily together. He did not need Ahab. He was already rich and strong (17:5, 12). The alliance was sealed by marrying his son Jehoram to Ahab's daughter Athaliah (21:6; 22:2). It ended the long hostility between the kingdoms, but it brought the house of Ahab into the house of David. Within a few years Athaliah would murder nearly all of David's descendants and seize Judah's throne (22:10).
- **"After some years he went down to Ahab in Samaria" (v2).** Samaria, built by Ahab's father Omri, was Israel's capital, about 35 miles north of Jerusalem. "Went down" is the usual idiom for leaving Jerusalem, which was on high ground.
- **"Ahab killed an abundance of sheep and oxen for him… and induced him to go up against Ramoth-gilead" (v2).** The Chronicler adds the feast and the word "induced." The verb (*sut*) means to lure or incite, and it comes back in v31, where God "drew" the Aramean chariots away. A different word (*patah*) is used for the lying spirit who will "entice" Ahab in vv19–21, but the picture is the same: Jehoshaphat is being lured by Ahab while Ahab is being lured to his judgment.
- **Ramoth-gilead.** A fortified city east of the Jordan in Gilead, about 30 miles east of the river and near the Aramean border. It had been one of Solomon's district capitals (1 Kgs 4:13) and a city of refuge (Deut 4:43). The Arameans held it, and Ahab wanted it back.
- **"I am as you are, my people as your people. We will be with you in the war" (v3).** A sweeping promise of complete partnership. Ruth said nearly the same to Naomi (Ruth 1:16), but she was joining herself to the LORD's people. Jehoshaphat is joining himself to a king who has turned from the LORD.
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry calls it "the worst match that ever was made by any of the house of David," and says the feast "was designed only to wheedle him into the expedition." He warns, "Some men's kindnesses are dangerous, as well as their society infectious." Guzik stresses that Jehoshaphat's safety lay in obedience, not alliances, and quotes F. B. Meyer's description of Ahab as a weak man used by a crafty and cruel wife. JFB simply notes that the chapter is "word for word, the same as" 1 Kings 22, and saves its notes for that chapter.

#### Ahab's Prophets Promise Victory (vv4–11)
- **"Inquire first for the word of the LORD" (v4).** To his credit, Jehoshaphat will not go to war without asking God. The trouble is that he has already promised (v3).
- **"The prophets… four hundred men" (v5).** These are not Baal's prophets; Elijah had already dealt with those (1 Kgs 18:40). They speak in the LORD's name (v10–11) but they are Ahab's court prophets, paid to tell the king what he wants to hear. **"Go up, for God will give it into the hand of the king."** Note the vagueness: "the king," but which king?
- **"Is there not here another prophet of the LORD of whom we may inquire?" (v6).** Jehoshaphat senses something is wrong. Four hundred voices saying the same thing is not the same as hearing from God.
- **"Micaiah the son of Imlah; but I hate him, for he never prophesies good concerning me, but always evil" (v7).** One of the most honest and revealing lines a king ever spoke. Ahab does not claim Micaiah is a false prophet. He hates him because he tells the truth. Micaiah, "who is like the LORD?", is mentioned only in this story. **"Let not the king say so."** Jehoshaphat gently objects.
- **"Sitting on their thrones, arrayed in their robes… at the threshing floor at the entrance of the gate of Samaria" (v9).** A threshing floor was a large, flat, open space, ideal for a public assembly. City gates were where official business and judgment took place. Two kings in full royal dress, with hundreds of prophets performing before them, made a grand scene. Archaeologists have uncovered Samaria's royal quarter from Ahab's time.
- **"Zedekiah the son of Chenaanah made for himself horns of iron" (v10).** A prophetic acted sign. Horns stand for strength, especially a bull goring its enemies (Deut 33:17; Ps 92:10). Prophets often acted out their messages (Jer 27:2; Ezek 4). But a vivid, confident performance is no proof that a message comes from God.
- **Commentators:** Guzik argues the 400 were prophets of the LORD in name but unfaithful ones, and through Trapp jokes that it was a whole council agreeing together. Like Selman, he notes that a striking presentation does not make a message true. JFB calls the prophets "the creatures of Ahab" who "unanimously predicted a prosperous issue," and says Zedekiah's two horns stood for two heroes, the two kings.

#### Micaiah Prophesies Against Ahab (vv12–27)
- **"Let your word be like the word of one of them, and speak favorably" (v12).** The messenger means well, perhaps, but he asks a prophet to be a team player. Kings adds that Micaiah had to be fetched, and Ahab's order to send him "back" (v25) suggests he was already in custody.
- **"As the LORD lives, what my God says, that I will speak" (v13).** The heart of a true prophet, and of any faithful teacher of Scripture. Balaam, of all people, said much the same (Num 22:18; 24:13). The Chronicler adds "my God" to Kings' wording, making it personal.
- **"Go up and triumph; they will be given into your hand" (v14).** Micaiah echoes the 400 word for word. Ahab instantly knows it is not meant seriously (v15). Most readers hear sarcasm: Micaiah is mimicking the chorus, perhaps in their own sing-song tone. Others think he gave an answer as vague as theirs, which Ahab could read either way. Either way, Ahab knows the truth when he hears its opposite.
- **"How many times shall I make you swear that you speak to me nothing but the truth?" (v15).** The irony is thick. Ahab demands the truth and then refuses to believe it.
- **"I saw all Israel scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd" (v16).** In the Bible, kings are shepherds of their people (2 Sam 5:2; Ezek 34). Sheep without a shepherd means the king will die. Moses asked God not to let Israel be "as sheep that have no shepherd" (Num 27:17). **"These have no master; let each return to his home in peace."** The army will go home without its king.
- **"I saw the LORD sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing on his right hand and on his left" (v18).** Micaiah lets them see behind the curtain. The real throne room is not at Samaria's gate but in heaven. Isaiah (Isa 6:1–8), Job's narrator (Job 1:6; 2:1) and Daniel (Dan 7:9–10) show similar scenes of God's heavenly council. Two kings sit on their thrones below. One King sits on his throne above.
- **"Who will entice Ahab the king of Israel, that he may go up and fall at Ramoth-gilead?" (v19).** God has already decided Ahab's judgment. Elijah announced it after Ahab and Jezebel murdered Naboth for his vineyard (1 Kgs 21:19–24). The question is only how it will come about.
- **"A spirit came forward… 'I will be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets'" (vv20–21).** Hebrew has "the spirit," which some take to mean a particular known spirit, even Satan, and others take as a spirit who personifies prophecy. This is one of the hardest scenes in the Old Testament. Does God deceive?
- **How to understand the lying spirit.** Faithful readers broadly agree on several points. God does not lie (Num 23:19; Titus 1:2), and Micaiah himself exposes the lie, so Ahab is not deceived without warning. Ahab has been told the truth plainly (vv16, 22) and chooses the lie. God's judgment on those who reject the truth is often to give them over to what they prefer (Ezek 14:9; Rom 1:24–28). Paul says the same of those who "refused to love the truth": "God sends them a strong delusion, so that they may believe what is false" (2 Thess 2:10–11). Many interpreters, including JFB, take the scene as a vision in picture language and read God's command as permission. Others take it more literally, as God sovereignly using even an evil spirit. Either way, God rules over evil without being its author.
- **"The LORD has declared disaster concerning you" (v22).** Micaiah gives the plain verdict.
- **"Zedekiah… struck Micaiah on the cheek" (v23).** A slap on the cheek was an insult as much as an injury. **"Which way did the Spirit of the LORD go from me to speak to you?"** Zedekiah sincerely, or arrogantly, claims the Spirit. Jesus was struck in the face before the high priest (John 18:22–23), and Paul too (Acts 23:2).
- **"You shall see on that day when you go into an inner chamber to hide yourself" (v24).** When disaster comes, Zedekiah will be hiding in a back room, and he will know who spoke the truth.
- **"Amon the governor of the city and… Joash the king's son" (v25).** "The king's son" may mean a royal prince, or may be an official title for a member of the royal family who served as a prison officer (Jer 36:26; 38:6).
- **"Meager rations of bread and water until I return in peace" (v26).** Literally "bread of affliction and water of affliction." Ahab defies the prophecy in his very words: "until I return in peace."
- **"If you return in peace, the LORD has not spoken by me" (v27).** Micaiah stakes everything on the test Moses gave for a true prophet: does the word come true (Deut 18:21–22)?
- **"Hear, all you peoples!" (v27).** These are the opening words of the prophet Micah's book (Mic 1:2). Micah came over a century later and has a nearly identical name. Some scholars think a scribe added this line to link the two. Others think Micah consciously echoed his earlier namesake. It is in both Kings and Chronicles.
- **Commentators:** JFB, writing on 1 Kings 22, says Micaiah's first answer "must have been given in a sarcastic tone and in ironical mockery" of the court prophets, and that "the command must be viewed as only a permission to the lying spirit." Guzik notes that Ahab asked for the truth but could not bear it, and quotes Morgan that hating God's messenger is clear evidence of willful wickedness. Through Payne, Guzik raises the view that "the spirit" was Satan, and through Selman connects the scene with Ezekiel 14, where God answers idolaters through deceived prophets. He also cites Trapp that to a man who had seen God on his throne, two kings on theirs looked very small.

#### The Death of Ahab (vv28–34)
- **"So the king of Israel and Jehoshaphat the king of Judah went up to Ramoth-gilead" (v28).** The most surprising sentence in the chapter. Jehoshaphat asked for a true prophet, heard one, and went anyway. Having given his word to Ahab, he may have felt unable to back out.
- **"I will disguise myself and go into battle, but you wear your robes" (v29).** Ahab tries to cheat the prophecy. It is a cynical plan. If the Arameans look for the king, they will find Jehoshaphat. King Josiah would later also disguise himself to fight against a warning God had given, and die (35:22).
- **"Fight with neither small nor great, but only with the king of Israel" (v30).** The king of Syria, probably Ben-hadad, whom Ahab had spared after defeating him (1 Kgs 20:31–34), now targets Ahab alone. Mercy to an enemy of God had not bought gratitude.
- **"And Jehoshaphat cried out, and the LORD helped him; God drew them away from him" (v31).** Kings says only that "Jehoshaphat cried out" (1 Kgs 22:32). The Chronicler adds "the LORD helped him; God drew them away from him." "Drew them away" is the same verb translated "induced" or "lured" in v2. Ahab lured Jehoshaphat into danger. God lured his enemies away from him. Whether his cry was a prayer or a shout to identify himself, the Chronicler makes clear that his rescue came from God. It is the same pattern as Abijah (13:14–15) and Asa (14:11–12): a king of Judah cries out, and God answers.
- **"A certain man drew his bow at random" (v33).** Literally "in his innocence," meaning without aiming at anyone in particular. The archer had no idea whom he had hit. **"Between the scale armor and the breastplate."** Ahab wore a coat of overlapping metal scales, with a gap at the joints, probably where the armor met under the arm or at the waist. An unaimed arrow found the one gap in a disguised king's armor. What looks like chance is God's word coming true (Prov 16:33).
- **"Propped up in his chariot facing the Syrians until evening. Then at sunset he died" (v34).** To Ahab's credit, he stayed in the chariot so his troops would not panic. Kings adds that his blood pooled in the chariot, and that when it was washed at the pool of Samaria, dogs licked up his blood, as Elijah had said (1 Kgs 22:35–38; 21:19). The Chronicler stops at his death. Micaiah's word came true exactly: Israel's army scattered, "every man to his city" (1 Kgs 22:36).
- **Commentators:** Matthew Henry thinks Jehoshaphat may have cried "either to his friends to relieve him (but Ahab took no care of that)" or "to God for succour and deliverance (to whom else should he cry?)," and notes that "God has all men's hearts in his hand, and turns them as he pleases." On Ahab: "No art, no arms, can save those whom God has appointed to ruin," and "Jehoshaphat is safe in his robes, Ahab killed in his armour." Guzik admits Jehoshaphat's willingness to wear his robes is hard to explain, whether by fatalism, foolishness or great faith, and quotes Morgan on the arrow: the worldly man says it just happened, the man of faith says God did it. JFB describes Ahab in "the uniform of a subaltern," a junior officer, while urging Jehoshaphat to fight "in his royal attire."

#### Pointing to Christ
Micaiah stood alone against four hundred and spoke what God said, though it cost him a slap and a prison cell. The truest Prophet stood alone before rulers, was struck on the face, and did not change his word (John 18:19–23). Ahab was a shepherd who led his sheep to scatter on the mountains. Jesus saw the crowds "harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" and had compassion on them (Matt 9:36). He is the Good Shepherd who does not disguise himself and send others into danger in his place, but "lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). And when Jehoshaphat, who had no business being on that battlefield, cried out, the LORD helped him. Grace meets even a compromised believer who cries out.
