*Setting:* Chronicles was written after the exile, around 450–400 BC. The first attempt to bring the ark to Jerusalem has ended in Uzzah's death, and the ark is resting at the house of Obed-edom, where "the LORD blessed the household" (13:14). This chapter fills those three months with signs that God was blessing David too: a foreign king sends builders for his palace, his family grows, and he defeats the Philistines twice. In 2 Samuel these events come before the ark story (2 Sam 5:11–25). The Chronicler has arranged his material by theme rather than strict date. Hiram of Tyre probably sent his builders somewhat later in David's reign, and the Philistine wars came soon after David became king over all Israel, around 1003 BC. Placed here, the chapter shows that God had not abandoned David after Perez-uzza. And it gives a sharp contrast with Saul. Saul did not seek the LORD (10:14). David, in this chapter, asks God before every move.

#### David's Wives and Children (vv1–7)
- **"Hiram king of Tyre sent messengers to David, and cedar trees, also masons and carpenters" (v1).** Tyre was the great Phoenician port on the coast north of Israel (in modern Lebanon), famous for trade, seafaring and skilled craftsmen. Its hills held the cedars of Lebanon, the finest building timber in the region. Israel, a nation of farmers and shepherds, did not have builders of that skill. Hiram later supplied Solomon with timber and craftsmen for the temple (2 Chr 2:3–16). An alliance with Tyre gave David a peaceful northern border and access to Mediterranean trade. JFB calls the alliance one of the most fortunate features of David's reign. Henry notes that David had a large kingdom and Hiram a small one, and yet David still needed him: no one is so great that he does not need his neighbors.
- **"David knew that the LORD had established him as king over Israel" (v2).** David read the signs of God's favor rightly. A foreign king honoring him was proof that his throne was secure, and that it was God who had secured it.
- **"His kingdom was highly exalted for the sake of his people Israel" (v2).** This is the key phrase of the section. David's greatness was not for David. It was for Israel. A king existed to serve God's people, not the other way around. JFB calls this "an important truth." Henry: "We are blessed in order that we may be blessings." Guzik makes it one of two things every godly leader must know: God put me here, and he put me here for others.
- **"And David took more wives in Jerusalem" (v3).** The Chronicler records it without praise. 2 Samuel 5:13 adds "concubines and wives." The Law had warned that Israel's king "shall not acquire many wives for himself, lest his heart turn away" (Deut 17:17). Taking several wives was common for ancient kings, and was often a way of sealing alliances, but it was not God's design (Gen 2:24; Matt 19:4–6). David's large household later brought great grief: Amnon's rape of his half-sister Tamar, Absalom's murder of Amnon and then his revolt, and Adonijah's attempt to seize the throne (2 Sam 13–18; 1 Kings 1). Henry calls it "David's infirmity that when he settled in his kingdom he took more wives." Guzik, quoting J. Barton Payne, calls it historical fact but moral failure, and observes that David handled hardship better than success. The seeds of trouble are often sown in good times.
- **"Shammua, Shobab, Nathan, Solomon" (v4).** The four sons of Bathsheba, as 3:5 makes clear ("Bath-shua"). The Chronicler never tells the story of David's sin with her, but it is quietly here. Two of these sons matter far beyond their own lives. **Solomon** became king and built the temple. **Nathan**, named perhaps after the prophet who confronted David, is the son through whom Luke traces Jesus' ancestry (Luke 3:31), while Matthew traces it through Solomon (Matt 1:6).
- **"Ibhar, Elishua, Elpelet, Nogah, Nepheg, Japhia, Elishama, Beeliada and Eliphelet" (vv5–7).** Thirteen sons in all, matching the list in 3:5–8. Samuel's list (2 Sam 5:14–16) has eleven and lacks Elpelet and Nogah. **Beeliada** means "the Lord knows." It is called Eliada ("God knows") in 3:8 and 2 Samuel 5:16. As with Eshbaal and Merib-baal (8:33–34), *baal* here meant simply "lord," and other copies changed it.

#### Philistines Defeated (vv8–17)
- **"When the Philistines heard that David had been anointed king over all Israel, all the Philistines went up to search for David" (v8).** As long as David ruled only Judah, the Philistines may have regarded him as a useful vassal. He had once served Achish of Gath (1 Sam 27). A king over all twelve tribes was a threat. JFB suggests they wanted to crush him before his throne was secure, fearing that he knew their weak points from his years among them. The Tyndale notes make the same point.
- **"The Philistines had come and made a raid in the Valley of Rephaim" (v9).** The broad valley running southwest from Jerusalem toward Bethlehem. It was an easy route into the hill country, and it cut Jerusalem off from Judah's heartland.
- **"And David inquired of God, 'Shall I go up against the Philistines? Will you give them into my hand?'" (v10).** The Chronicler's central contrast. Saul "did not seek guidance from the LORD" and turned to a medium against the Philistines (10:13–14). David, facing the same enemy, asks God first, probably through the high priest and the Urim (1 Sam 23:9–12; 30:7–8). Notice that the question is double: should I go, and will you give me victory? Henry: when "we know not what to do," we should "ask counsel at his oracles."
- **"Go up, and I will give them into your hand" (v10).** A clear promise. David's victory would be God's gift.
- **"God has broken through my enemies by my hand, like a bursting flood" (v11).** "Broken through" is *parats*, the same verb as the "breaking out" against Uzzah at Perez-uzza (13:11). There, God broke out against his own people for treating him carelessly. Here he breaks out against his enemies for his obedient king. The picture is a flash flood bursting through a dam or a dry wadi, sweeping everything before it. Guzik, following Martin Selman, sees the judgment of the previous chapter turned into blessing.
- **"Therefore the name of that place is called Baal-perazim" (v11).** "Lord of breaking through," or "Master of breakthroughs." David uses *baal* in its plain sense, "lord," for the LORD himself. Isaiah later remembers this battle. He warns rebellious Judah that the LORD will "rise up as on Mount Perazim," but this time against his own people, "to do his deed — strange is his deed!" (Isa 28:21). JFB points to the same verse.
- **"They left their gods there, and David gave command, and they were burned" (v12).** The Philistines carried images of their gods into battle as protection, as Israel had once carried the ark (1 Sam 4:3–4). Then the Philistines had captured Israel's ark and carried it off to Dagon's temple. Now Israel captures the Philistines' gods. 2 Samuel 5:21 says David's men "carried them away." The Chronicler adds that David had them burned, as the Law commanded: "You shall burn their carved images with fire" (Deut 7:5, 25). The Tyndale notes point to the same law. David did not keep them as trophies or treat them as powerful objects. Henry: this should "teach us a holy indignation against idolatry and all the remains of it."
- **"And the Philistines yet again made a raid in the valley" (v13).** Same enemy, same valley. The obvious move would be to repeat what worked the first time.
- **"When David again inquired of God" (v14).** David does not assume. Last time's answer does not settle this time's question. Guzik makes this one of the chapter's main lessons: a familiar enemy does not always call for a familiar strategy, and past success is no substitute for fresh guidance.
- **"You shall not go up after them; go around and come against them opposite the balsam trees" (v14).** This time God gives a different plan: no head-on attack, but a flanking move from behind. JFB explains it as circling around to strike the enemy's rear. The Hebrew name of the trees, *baka*, is uncertain. Translations have balsam, mulberry, poplar or aspen. The same word may lie behind the "Valley of Baca" in Psalm 84:6. The point does not depend on the species: they were trees whose leaves rustle easily.
- **"When you hear the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees, then go out to battle, for God has gone out before you" (v15).** The signal was a sound like an army on the move, high in the treetops. JFB takes it to be a sudden wind rustling the leaves. Others, including F. B. Meyer as Guzik notes, see it as the sound of God's own heavenly army going ahead of David. Scripture elsewhere speaks of God's unseen hosts at war, as when the LORD made the Syrians hear "the sound of chariots… the sound of a great army" (2 Kings 7:6), and when Elisha's servant saw the hills full of fiery chariots (2 Kings 6:17). Either way, the battle was the LORD's before it was David's. When Deborah sent Barak into battle she said the same thing: "Does not the LORD go out before you?" (Judg 4:14). Henry: when God goes ahead, "let us gird up our loins, gird on our armour, and follow him."
- **"David did as God commanded him" (v16).** Simple, complete obedience, the very thing missing at the threshing floor of Chidon. **"From Gibeon to Gezer"** traces the rout west and downhill from the hills north of Jerusalem to Gezer on the edge of the coastal plain, about fifteen miles. 2 Samuel 5:25 reads "Geba," a similar name. JFB notes that the victory cleared the Philistines out of the hill country.
- **"And the fame of David went out into all lands, and the LORD brought the fear of him upon all nations" (v17).** A verse found only in Chronicles. It echoes the promise to Israel in the wilderness: "I will begin to put the dread and fear of you on the peoples" (Deut 2:25). David's fame, like his kingdom, came from the LORD, and it served God's purpose of making his name known among the nations.
- **Commentators:** Guzik collects several voices on the sound in the trees. Adam Clarke asks why such direct guidance seems rare today and answers that many do not ask or expect it. John Trapp says believers must notice and obey the Spirit's promptings. Spurgeon, working from the older translation "bestir thyself" (2 Sam 5:24, KJV), urges Christians to stir themselves when God is at work, not wait for others to stir them. Payne observes that David depended on God for strategy as well as strength. Henry adds that in distress we should "fly to him" first rather than last. JFB and Guzik both treat Baal-perazim and the second battle as separate events in the same valley, as the text plainly does.

#### Pointing to Christ
The chapter shows a king who asked God before every battle, waited for his signal, and won because "God has gone out before you." That is the kind of king Saul was not, and the kind David only partly was. His many wives sowed the troubles that later tore his house apart. Jesus is the King who perfectly does what David did at his best: "the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing" (John 5:19). He is the true Lord of the breakthrough, who broke through death itself, and whose victory over the powers of darkness is complete (Col 2:15). Like David burning the idols left on the field, he leaves no rival god standing. And the sons listed in vv4–7 point to him too. Both Solomon and Nathan, two sons of Bathsheba, appear in the family line of Jesus, so God brought the promised Son of David out of the very household where David had sinned so badly.
