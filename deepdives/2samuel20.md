*Setting:* Gilgal, the road to Jerusalem and the far north of Israel, around 976 BC, right after Absalom's revolt. David has crossed back over the Jordan, but his return has opened an old crack. The men of Judah escorted him home first, and the northern tribes complained that they had been shut out. The quarrel ended with Judah's words "fiercer than the words of the men of Israel" (19:43). David has also made a risky move: he promised Absalom's general Amasa the command of the army in place of Joab (19:13), hoping to win back the rebels. This chapter shows what all of that costs. A second revolt breaks out, Joab settles the command question with a sword, and a wise woman in a border town ends the war with one sentence. It closes the long story of David's troubles (chapters 9–20) with a list of his officials, as chapter 8 opened it.

#### The Rebellion of Sheba (vv1–22)
- **"There happened to be there a worthless man" (v1).** "Worthless" is *beliyyaal*, literally "without worth," the word behind "Belial" (2 Cor 6:15). The ESV keeps it as an adjective, but it is a strong word, used of the sons of Eli and of the men of Gibeah (1 Sam 2:12; Judg 19:22). Shimei threw the same insult at David (16:7). Now the narrator uses it of a rebel.
- **"Sheba, the son of Bichri, a Benjaminite" (v1).** He came from Saul's tribe. Old loyalty to the house of Saul had not died, and Sheba saw his moment in the north's resentment of Judah. Nothing else is known of him.
- **"We have no portion in David… every man to his tents, O Israel!" (v1).** A short poem, easy to shout. It calls David only "the son of Jesse," a put-down that Saul used (1 Sam 22:7–8). Almost the same cry tears the kingdom in two under Solomon's son: "What portion do we have in David?… To your tents, O Israel!" (1 Kings 12:16). Sheba's revolt fails, but it shows exactly where the kingdom will later break.
- **"All the men of Israel withdrew… But the men of Judah followed their king steadfastly" (v2).** "Steadfastly" is literally "clung to," the verb used of Ruth clinging to Naomi (Ruth 1:14). The tribes that argued over who loved David more (19:43) walk away within a day. Judah stays.
- **The ten concubines (v3).** These were the women David left to keep the palace when he fled (15:16), whom Absalom took publicly on the roof as a claim to the throne (16:21–22). David cannot take them back as wives, since that would look like sharing what his son had seized, and he cannot send them away. So he provides for them in a guarded house for the rest of their lives, **"living as if in widowhood."** They did nothing wrong. They pay for Absalom's sin and for David's flight, and Nathan's word comes true in them (12:11).
- **"Call the men of Judah together to me within three days" (v4).** Amasa's first task as general. David is keeping his promise and testing the new commander.
- **"He delayed beyond the set time" (v5).** The text gives no reason. Perhaps Judah's men were slow to follow Absalom's former general, or perhaps Amasa was simply not up to the job. Either way, the delay gives Joab his opening.
- **"Sheba… will do us more harm than Absalom" (v6).** David sees the danger clearly. A revolt in the north that reached walled cities could split the nation for good. He still will not use Joab, so he gives the command to Abishai, Joab's brother. **"Your lord's servants"** are David's own troops, the core of the standing army.
- **Joab's men, the Cherethites and Pelethites, and the mighty men (v7).** The Cherethites and Pelethites were David's foreign bodyguard, probably from Crete and from the Philistine coast (8:18). Notice who is listed first: "Joab's men." Joab has no rank in this verse, but he rides out anyway.
- **"The great stone that is in Gibeon" (v8).** Gibeon was about 6 miles north of Jerusalem. Here Joab's men and Abner's men once fought by the pool (2:12–17). It is a place with bad memories.
- **"As he went forward it fell out" (v8).** The details are hard to follow in Hebrew, but the sense is clear: Joab's sword drops from its sheath, so he looks unarmed, or he picks it up in a way that seems harmless. Josephus says he let it fall on purpose. Whether accident or trick, it ends up in his left hand while his right hand reaches for Amasa's beard.
- **"Is it well with you, my brother?" (v9).** "Is it well" is the Hebrew greeting *shalom*: peace. Taking a man by the beard to kiss him was a warm greeting between equals. Amasa was Joab's cousin (17:25; 1 Chr 2:16–17), so "my brother" was close to true. It is a kiss of betrayal, centuries before Judas (Matt 26:49; compare Prov 27:6).
- **"Struck him with it in the stomach" (v10).** The Hebrew word *chomesh* means "belly," the same spot where Abner struck Asahel and where Joab struck Abner (2:23; 3:27). The KJV's "fifth rib" came from reading it as the Hebrew word for "five," and the lexicon does not support that. Joab has now killed two men David had chosen to lead the army, and both times by a show of friendship.
- **"Whoever favors Joab, and whoever is for David, let him follow Joab" (v11).** One of Joab's men ties Joab's name to David's, as if they were the same thing. The army knows Joab. Abishai has the commission, but from here Joab gives the orders and no one argues.
- **"Amasa lay wallowing in his blood in the highway" (v12).** The soldiers stop and stare. The body is dragged into a field and covered with a cloak, not out of respect but to keep the march moving. It is a picture of how quickly the murder is put out of sight, though not out of God's sight. David remembers it on his deathbed (1 Kings 2:5).
- **"Abel of Beth-maacah" (v14).** A town in the far north, near Dan and the foot of Mount Hermon, about as far from Jerusalem as an Israelite could go. Sheba runs the length of the land, and the Bichrites (his clan) follow him in. "All the tribes" seem to have produced few recruits for him, since one walled town is all he can hold.
- **"They cast up a mound against the city" (v15).** A siege ramp, earth and rubble piled against the wall so soldiers and battering rams can reach the top. Assyrian palace carvings show the same method. The city is about to fall, and everyone in it will pay for one man.
- **"A wise woman called from the city" (v16).** Like the wise woman of Tekoa (14:2), she has no name. Unlike Tekoa's woman, who was Joab's tool, this one speaks for herself and for her town. She calls for the general by name, checks it is really him, and makes him listen: "Listen to the words of your servant." "I am listening."
- **"Let them but ask counsel at Abel" (v18).** An old proverb: Abel was known for its wise people, a place where disputes were settled by talking. She is reminding Joab that this is a town of counsel, so he should try counsel before rams. Some older translations, followed by JFB, read the line as "they will surely ask at Abel," a reminder that the law required an offer of peace before a siege (Deut 20:10–12). Either way, she is asking him to talk first.
- **"A mother in Israel" (v19).** A leading town, with smaller villages around it called its "daughters." Deborah was also called "a mother in Israel" (Judg 5:7). **"The heritage of the LORD"** is the land and people God gave Israel. Her charge is bold: Joab, an Israelite general, is about to destroy part of God's own inheritance to catch one man.
- **"Far be it from me, far be it" (v20).** Joab, who stabbed his cousin a few days before, protests that he would never destroy anything. The irony is hard to miss. But his terms are fair: **"Give up him alone, and I will withdraw."**
- **"His head shall be thrown to you over the wall" (v21).** She does not haggle. One rebel's life for the whole town.
- **"The woman went to all the people in her wisdom" (v22).** She persuades the town, and the revolt ends with a head over a wall and a trumpet. Sheba began with a trumpet blast and "every man to his tents" (v1). It ends with Joab's trumpet and "every man to his home." The war is over with only one more death.
- **Commentators:** Henry sees in Sheba how fickle crowds are ("The favour of the many is not to be depended upon") and calls Joab's murder all the worse for its deceit: he "contentedly sacrificed the interest both of the king and the kingdom to his personal revenge." JFB calls the greeting proof of Joab's "deep hypocrisy" and remarks that "such a magic spell was in the word 'Joab'" that Amasa's men simply followed him. JFB repeats the old "fifth rib" reading, which the Hebrew does not support. JFB suggests the wise woman may have been a local judge, "like Deborah." Guzik notes how a catchy slogan carried the north away and draws a lesson for keeping unity (Eph 4:3). He sees the concubines as innocent victims of other men's sins. Henry and Guzik (following the Puritan Trapp) both turn the siege into a picture of the heart. The soul is a walled city with a traitor inside, God's quarrel is with the sin, not the sinner, and in Henry's words "The single condition of peace is, the surrender of the traitor."

#### David's Officials (vv23–26)
- **Why a list here?** A nearly identical list closes David's rise in 8:15–18. The two lists frame the whole story of chapters 9–20: Bathsheba, Amnon, Absalom and Sheba. After all of it, the government still stands. The Tyndale notes make the point that David's rule did not collapse. Chapters 21–24 that follow are an appendix of other material about David, not a continuation of this story.
- **"Joab was in command of all the army of Israel" (v23).** No one appointed him. Amasa is dead, and the job is his again. Nothing in the text says David approved. David's silence here says enough. On his deathbed he will tell Solomon to deal with Joab for "the blood of war in peacetime" shed on Abner and Amasa (1 Kings 2:5–6).
- **Benaiah son of Jehoiada (v23).** Head of the royal guard and one of the mighty men (23:20–23). He will one day carry out that sentence on Joab (1 Kings 2:34) and take his place.
- **"Adoram was in charge of the forced labor" (v24).** A new office since chapter 8. Forced labor was work for the crown, building and farming, owed by subjects, exactly what Samuel warned a king would demand (1 Sam 8:11–17). Under Solomon it grows heavy, and when Rehoboam sends Adoram (called Adoniram in 1 Kings 4:6) to the northern tribes, they stone him to death (1 Kings 12:18). The man on this list becomes the first casualty of the split that Sheba's slogan foreshadowed.
- **Jehoshaphat the recorder, Sheva the secretary (vv24–25).** The recorder probably kept the royal records and announced the king's decisions. The secretary handled letters and official writing. The secretary's name is spelled differently in each list (Seraiah in 8:17, Shisha in 1 Kings 4:3), likely the same man.
- **Zadok and Abiathar (v25).** The two high priests who stayed loyal through Absalom's revolt and sent their sons as David's messengers (15:24–36). Abiathar will later back the wrong heir and lose his office (1 Kings 2:26–27).
- **"Ira the Jairite was also David's priest" (v26).** The word is *kohen*, the normal word for priest. The Tyndale notes suggest a royal chaplain or court official rather than a Levitical priest. In 8:18 the same title is given to David's sons, which the parallel in 1 Chronicles 18:17 explains as "chief officials in the service of the king." The exact role is unclear, and the text does not explain it.
- **Commentators:** Henry draws a plain lesson from the list: "It is well when able men are appointed to discharge public duties." JFB says the list shows "the government was re-established in its wonted course," and reads Joab's return to command as David "winking at the murder of Amasa," since he had no power to remove him. Guzik notes that the kingdom rested on a team and not on David alone, and that even a king needed someone like Ira to help him spiritually.

#### Pointing to Christ
Two scenes in this chapter look toward Christ by contrast and by likeness. Joab's kiss with a hidden sword is the pattern Judas follows when he greets Jesus with "Rabbi" and a kiss. And at Abel a whole city is spared when one man dies for it, but Sheba was the guilty one and his death was the price of peace. At the cross the pattern is turned around: "one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish" (John 11:50), only this time the one who dies is innocent, and he gives himself up freely so that rebels can go home in peace.
