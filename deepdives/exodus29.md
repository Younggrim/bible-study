*Setting:* Moses is still on Mount Sinai, inside the cloud, receiving God's plans for the tabernacle (24:18). Chapters 25–27 described the house and its furniture, and chapter 28 the clothes of the priests. Now God gives the ceremony that will turn Aaron and his four sons from ordinary Israelites into priests. The service is laid out here as instructions. It is carried out, almost step for step, in Leviticus 8, after the tabernacle is built. The chapter closes by stepping back to say what the whole tabernacle is for: "I will dwell among the people of Israel and will be their God" (v45).

#### Consecration of the Priests (vv1–46)
- **"This is what you shall do to them to consecrate them" (v1).** "Consecrate" means to make holy, set apart for God. The word and its relatives run all through the chapter (vv1, 21, 27, 33, 36, 37, 43, 44). The ceremony has a clear order: washing (v4), clothing (vv5–9), anointing (v7), then three sacrifices, a sin offering (vv10–14), a burnt offering (vv15–18) and an ordination offering (vv19–28), and finally a holy meal (vv31–34). The whole thing is repeated for seven days (v35).
- **"One bull of the herd and two rams without blemish" (v1).** "Without blemish" is *tamim*, "whole, complete." The same word is used when God tells Abraham to "be blameless" (Gen 17:1) and when the psalmist says God's "way is perfect" (Ps 18:30). Only a whole, healthy animal could stand in for sinners before a holy God. Peter draws the line straight to Christ, "a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Pet 1:19).
- **Unleavened bread, cakes and wafers (vv2–3).** Three kinds of bread made with fine wheat flour and oil, carried in one basket. Leaven was left out, as at Passover (12:15). Some of this bread was burned on the altar and some eaten by the priests (vv23–25, 32).
- **"Bring Aaron and his sons to the entrance of the tent of meeting and wash them with water" (v4).** This was done in public, at the doorway where the people could see, and Moses did the washing. They did not wash themselves. This full washing happened once. After this, priests washed only their hands and feet before serving (30:19–21). Jesus makes a similar distinction with Peter: "The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet" (John 13:10).
- **"Put on Aaron the coat and the robe of the ephod, and the ephod, and the breastpiece" (v5).** The garments of chapter 28 go on in order, from the inside out, ending with the turban and **"the holy crown"** (v6), the gold plate engraved "Holy to the LORD" (28:36; 39:30). The priests did not design or choose their clothes. They wore what God provided.
- **"Take the anointing oil and pour it on his head" (v7).** Only Aaron is anointed this way at this point (his sons are sprinkled with oil in v21). The recipe for this oil comes in 30:22–33. Psalm 133:2 remembers the oil "running down on the beard, on the beard of Aaron." Anointing marked a person out for God's service. Kings and some prophets were later anointed too, and "Messiah" (Hebrew) and "Christ" (Greek) both mean "anointed one."
- **"The priesthood shall be theirs by a statute forever" (v9).** The Tyndale notes point out how short of this the priests fell: Aaron himself makes the golden calf in chapter 32, Eli's sons abuse the sacrifices (1 Sam 2:12–17), and Malachi rebukes the priests of his day (Mal 2:1–9). Hebrews says the "forever" priesthood finds its fulfilment in one who holds his priesthood "permanently, because he continues forever" (Heb 7:24).
- **"Thus you shall ordain Aaron and his sons" (v9).** "Ordain" is literally "fill the hand" (*mille' yad*), an old idiom for installing someone in office. It may come from the moment in vv24–25 when the priests' hands are filled with the offering. The ram of v22 is called the "ram of ordination," literally the "ram of filling."
- **"Aaron and his sons shall lay their hands on the head of the bull" (v10).** The verb is *samak*, "lean, lay, rest upon," the word Ezekiel uses for an army leaning its weight against a city (Ezek 24:2). It was more than a touch. The worshiper identified with the animal and, in some sense, put his sin on it. The same act is repeated over each ram (vv15, 19). On the Day of Atonement the high priest lays both hands on the scapegoat and confesses the people's sins over it (Lev 16:21).
- **"Put it on the horns of the altar… pour out at the base" (v12).** The altar is the bronze altar of burnt offering in the courtyard (27:1–8). The Hebrew word for altar, *mizbeach*, comes from the verb "to slaughter": it is the place of sacrifice. The horns were the corner points, the most important part of the altar (see 1 Kgs 1:50).
- **"All the fat… the long lobe of the liver, and the two kidneys" (v13).** The fat and the inner organs were thought of as the richest, best part, and they belonged to God (Lev 3:16, "all fat is the LORD's").
- **"The flesh of the bull and its skin and its dung you shall burn with fire outside the camp; it is a sin offering" (v14).** The sin offering (*chatta't*) dealt with sin and its defilement. Here, because it was offered for the priests themselves, none of it could be eaten. It was carried away from God's dwelling and burned. Hebrews picks this up: "the bodies of those animals… are burned outside the camp. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (Heb 13:11–12).
- **"The whole ram on the altar. It is a burnt offering to the LORD" (v18).** In a burnt offering (*'olah*, "what goes up") nothing was kept back. The whole animal went up in smoke to God, a picture of complete dedication (Lev 1). **"A pleasing aroma"** is a human way of saying God delights in a sacrifice offered rightly. Noah's first sacrifice after the flood is described the same way (Gen 8:21), and Paul uses the phrase of Christ, who "gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God" (Eph 5:2).
- **Blood on the right ear, thumb and big toe (v20).** This is the most striking act in the chapter. Blood from the second ram was dabbed on the ear that hears God's word, the hand that does his work, and the foot that walks in his ways. The whole person, from head to foot, was claimed by God and covered by sacrifice. The right side was the side of strength and honor. The only other place this rite appears is the cleansing of a healed leper (Lev 14:14, 25). The priest and the outcast were brought near to God the same way.
- **Blood and oil sprinkled together (v21).** Blood from the altar and the anointing oil were sprinkled on the priests and on their clothes. Blood speaks of atonement, and oil of being set apart and empowered for service. Here they come together, and **"he and his garments shall be holy."**
- **"The fat tail" (v22).** The sheep of that region were broad-tailed sheep, whose tails store fat and can weigh many pounds. Leviticus 3:9 counts the fat tail among the parts given to God.
- **"Put all these on the palms of Aaron… and wave them for a wave offering" (v24).** Fat, thigh and bread were placed in the priests' open hands, literally "filling their hands," and lifted or moved before the LORD. The wave offering (*tenufah*) was a public act of presenting something to God. Moses then took the pieces from their hands and burned them on the altar (v25). The new priests' very first act was to hold an offering and give it up to God.
- **"It shall be your portion" (v26).** At this ordination Moses acted as the priest, so the breast of the ram went to him. From then on, the breast and the right thigh of the people's peace offerings would go to the priests as their food (vv27–28; Lev 7:31–34). The "contribution" (*terumah*, the KJV's "heave offering") was the share set aside from the people's gifts for God and his servants. God provided for those who served at his house.
- **"The holy garments of Aaron shall be for his sons after him" (v29).** The office outlasted the man. When Aaron died on Mount Hor, Moses took off his garments and put them on Eleazar his son (Num 20:25–28). **"Shall wear them seven days" (v30):** each new high priest went through the same seven days of consecration.
- **"Aaron and his sons shall eat the flesh of the ram and the bread" (v32).** The ordination ram was eaten like a peace offering, a meal shared in God's presence at the entrance of the tent. It recalls the elders who "beheld God, and ate and drank" on the mountain (24:11). To share a meal with someone in the ancient world was to share friendship and covenant.
- **"Those things with which atonement was made" (v33).** "Atonement" is *kipper*. Scholars debate its root. The traditional view is "to cover," and many now link it to "wipe away" or "cleanse." Either way, as the Tyndale notes say, the result is the same: sinners are made able to live in the presence of a holy God. **"An outsider shall not eat of them"** means anyone who was not a priest. **Leftovers burned by morning (v34)**, as with the Passover lamb (12:10), so the holy meat was never treated as ordinary food.
- **"Through seven days shall you ordain them, and every day you shall offer a bull as a sin offering" (vv35–36).** Seven is the number of completeness, the number of creation's week. During the seven days the priests stayed at the entrance of the tent day and night (Lev 8:33–35). A new bull every day showed that one animal's death did not settle the matter. Hebrews makes the point that these sacrifices had to be "continually offered every year," because "it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (Heb 10:1–4).
- **"You shall purify the altar, when you make atonement for it" (v36).** Even a brand-new altar, built at God's command, had to be cleansed. The problem was not the bronze. It was that sinful people would use it. The same cleansing of the altar happens every year on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:18–19).
- **"The altar shall be most holy. Whatever touches the altar shall become holy" (v37).** This is read two ways. Some take it to mean that holiness passes to whatever touches the altar, so it now belongs to God and can't be taken back for ordinary use. Others, following the old translations ("shall be holy," "must be holy"), take it as a warning: only what is holy may touch it. Either way, the altar is set apart in the highest degree. Haggai later notes that holiness does not spread to ordinary food through a fold of a garment (Hag 2:11–13), and Jesus speaks of "the altar that makes the gift sacred" (Matt 23:19).
- **"Two lambs a year old day by day regularly" (v38).** The word "regularly" is *tamid*, "continually." Later Judaism called this daily offering simply "the Tamid." One lamb was offered in the morning and one **"at twilight"** (v39), literally "between the two evenings," the same phrase used for the time of the Passover lamb (12:6). This was the heartbeat of Israel's worship for centuries. Elijah prayed "at the time of the offering of the oblation" (1 Kgs 18:36), David asked that his prayer rise "as the evening sacrifice" (Ps 141:2), and Peter and John went to the temple "at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1), the time of the evening offering. Daniel foresaw a time when "the regular burnt offering" would be taken away (Dan 8:11).
- **"A tenth measure of fine flour… a fourth of a hin of beaten oil… a fourth of a hin of wine" (v40).** A tenth of an ephah of flour was perhaps two quarts (about two liters), and a quarter of a hin of oil or wine about a quart (one liter), though ancient measures are only estimates. "Beaten oil" was the finest olive oil, crushed gently rather than pressed. The wine was a **drink offering**, poured out before God. Paul uses this picture of his own life: "I am to be poured out as a drink offering" (Phil 2:17; 2 Tim 4:6).
- **"Where I will meet with you, to speak to you there" (v42).** The goal of all this ritual is meeting and speaking. The tabernacle is called the "tent of meeting" because there God meets his people (25:22).
- **"It shall be sanctified by my glory" (v43).** Moses makes the things, but God's own glory makes them holy. This comes true when the cloud fills the finished tabernacle (40:34–35), and again at the first offerings, when fire comes out from the LORD and consumes the sacrifice (Lev 9:23–24). **"I will consecrate… Aaron also and his sons I will consecrate" (v44).** Moses performs the ceremony, but God is the one who consecrates.
- **"I will dwell among the people of Israel and will be their God" (v45).** "Dwell" is *shakan*, from which comes *mishkan*, "tabernacle," God's dwelling place (25:8–9). John writes of Jesus that "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), using a Greek verb that means "pitched his tent." **"Will be their God"** is the heart of God's covenant promise, repeated from Genesis to Revelation: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people" (Rev 21:3).
- **"Who brought them out of the land of Egypt that I might dwell among them" (v46).** This verse gives one of the clearest statements of *why* God brought Israel out of Egypt. Freedom from slavery was not the end goal. God rescued his people so that he could live with them. **"I am the LORD their God"** echoes the opening of the Ten Commandments (20:2) and seals the chapter.
- **Commentators:** All three see Christ in the ceremony. Henry says the washing signified "that those must be clean who bear the vessels of the Lord," and that in the sin offering the priests were "confessing that they deserved to die for their own sin." On the meal he says God "called them not only servants but friends," and on the seven days, that "they were not to look upon their consecration as completed till the seven days' end." His best line is on the daily lamb: "prayer-time must be kept up as duly as meat-time," and he reads Christ's one sacrifice as a continual offering, since "one offering thus becomes a continual offering." Guzik notes that the priests were washed by another, not by themselves, and in public, and he presses the Hebrew of laying on hands as leaning one's weight, not a light touch, which fits *samak*. He calls the altar a "killing-place," and the lexicon supports "place of slaughter." He reads blood on ear, thumb and toe as claiming hearing, work and walk, and he stresses that blood and oil belong together. JFB comments only on a few verses (1, 4, 10, 35–36 and 38), leaving most of the detail without a note. It sees the blood on the extremities as applying the atonement to the whole person. It calls the seven days a wise preparation that gave time for quiet reflection, and treats the daily lamb as the goal toward which the whole consecration pointed.

#### Pointing to Christ
Aaron was washed, clothed, anointed and covered with blood before he could serve, and the bulls had to be offered again every day. Jesus needed no washing or sin offering for himself (Heb 7:26–27). He was the spotless lamb, anointed with the Spirit (Luke 4:18), consecrated through suffering, and his one sacrifice "outside the gate" (Heb 13:12) did what a week of bulls never could. Through him believers are washed (1 Cor 6:11), clothed (Gal 3:27), anointed (1 John 2:20), and made "a royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9) to offer the continual sacrifice of praise morning and evening. And the goal is the same as it was at Sinai: God brought his people out so that he might dwell among them.
